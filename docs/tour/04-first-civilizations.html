    Chapter IV  ·  3500 – 1200 BCE

    First Civilizations
    & the Bronze Age

    c. 3500 – 1200 BCE · roughly 2,300 years

    In two dozen centuries humans invented the city, writing, law, and empire on four continents at once — then watched the first globalized world shatter in the Late Bronze Age Collapse.

    Scroll to begin, 3500 BCE

    The chapter before us

    In roughly two dozen centuries, humans invented almost everything we still mean by the word civilization — the city, the state, writing, law, bureaucracy, organized religion, long-distance trade, and the first empires.

    Chapter III ended with farming villages: domesticated wheat, rice, maize and millet, herded sheep and cattle, pottery, and the first permanent settlements of a few thousand people. What carried forward was the surplus — grain that could be stored, counted, taxed, and taken — together with the plough, the ox, irrigation, wool and the loom. None of those made a state by themselves. Put them in a floodplain that flooded on schedule, and within a few centuries you get temples, canals, scribes, tax collectors, and standing armies.

    Crucially, this happened not once but repeatedly and largely independently — along the Tigris and Euphrates, the Nile, the Indus, the Yangtze and the Yellow River, the Murghab delta and the Oxus, and the desert coast of Peru. Urban life was a recurring human answer, not a single invention that diffused outward from one lucky valley. Writing itself was born more than once: cuneiform in Mesopotamia, oracle-bone script in Shang China, and — much later and an ocean away — glyphs in Mesoamerica, each devised without knowledge of the others.

    Bronze — an alloy demanding copper and scarce tin from sources often thousands of kilometres apart — wired these worlds into the first international system, bound by trade, diplomacy, and a shared appetite for metal. When that system shattered around 1200 BCE, entire literate civilizations went dark within two generations: a sobering lesson in the fragility of an interconnected world, and the note on which this chapter ends.

    01 · Mesopotamia

    Between two rivers

    In the marshy south of Iraq, Uruk swells into the world's first great city (Tell Brak in the north grew large a little earlier, but nothing matched Uruk's scale) — temples, canals, and crowds of strangers. Sumer lights the first lamp, but it will be far from the only one.

    02 · The Nile

    One river, two powers

    Narmer unites Upper and Lower Egypt into a god-king's state; far upstream in Nubia, Kerma is founded c. 2500 BCE and grows over the following centuries into a walled city of some 25 hectares — by c. 1750 BCE a peer power guarding the gold road south. The Nile carries two civilizations, not one.

    03 · The Americas

    An ocean away, the same idea

    On the desert coast of Peru, Caral raises platform mounds and sunken plazas while Egypt is building pyramids — without pottery, metal, or writing. Urban life is a human answer, not a Eurasian export.

    04 · Indus & Aegean

    Grids and labyrinths

    Mohenjo-daro lays out ruler-straight streets and covered drains for tens of thousands; on Crete, the palace of Knossos and swift ships make the Aegean Europe's first literate world.

    05 · China & Oceania

    The world fully lit

    Along the Yellow River, Shang kings cast towering bronzes and inscribe oracle bones — writing born again from scratch. Meanwhile Austronesian voyagers are already deep in the open Pacific — the Marianas were reached from the Philippines, perhaps as early as c. 1500 BCE — and Lapita sailors now push past the Solomons into Remote Oceania.

    Climb Uruk's wall and walk its length; survey the foundations, examine the brickwork — did not the Seven Sages themselves lay out its plans?

    01 · The alloy

    A metal no one place could make

    Bronze is copper hardened with tin — but tin is rare (Central Asia, the Taurus, distant Cornwall) and copper is concentrated (Cyprus, Oman, the Alps). To make it, every palace had to reach halfway across the world.

    02 · The web

    One market, many kingdoms

    The hunger for metal wove Egypt, Hatti, Babylon, Assyria, and Mycenae into a single system of caravans, ships, and palace warehouses — the first age of globalization in miniature.

    03 · The ship

    The world in one hull

    The Uluburun wreck carried ten tons of Cypriot copper, a ton of tin, Baltic amber, African ivory, and Canaanite jars — a single cargo drawn from at least seven cultures, bound together by letters written in Akkadian.

    04 · The collapse

    When the threads snap

    Drought, earthquakes, migration, and the raiding "Sea Peoples" cascade through an over-connected world. Within about fifty years Hattusa is abandoned, Ugarit burns, and Linear B literacy vanishes from Greece for four centuries.

    01 · The cargo

    One hull, three continents

    In 1982 a sponge diver working off Uluburun, near Kaş on Turkey's south coast, reported "metal biscuits with ears" on the seabed: the ingots of a ship that went down around 1320 BCE (by radiocarbon; an older tree-ring date of 1305 is no longer thought secure). Lead isotopes put its ten tons of copper in Cyprus. A 2022 isotope study traced about a third of its ton of tin to Central Asia — the best match is the Mušiston deposit in Tajikistan — and the other two-thirds to the Taurus mountains of Anatolia around Kestel, much of it stream tin and the leftovers of Early Bronze Age mining; a 2023 critique rejects the Central Asian match and favours south-west England for part of it. The ship also carried some 200 glass ingots whose chemistry marks all of them as Egyptian glass, cast in as few as 28 batches, Baltic amber beads from more than 2,000 km north, and logs of African blackwood — the "ebony" Egypt obtained through Nubia — with elephant and hippopotamus ivory. Sailing west from the Levant or Cyprus, probably for a Mycenaean palace, it never arrived.

    02 · The Gulf road

    Ur, Dilmun, Magan, Meluhha

    A thousand years earlier the sea road already ran east. Sargon of Akkad (c. 2300 BCE) boasted that ships of Meluhha, Magan and Dilmun moored at his quay: Meluhha was the Indus world, whose cities — Mohenjo-daro, the bead-making port of Lothal — sent carnelian and ivory; Magan was the Oman peninsula, selling copper from the Hajar hills; Dilmun, on Bahrain, was the broker, using weights on the Indus standard. Lapis lazuli came overland from its main ancient source, Sar-i Sang in Badakhshan, through workshop towns such as Shahr-i Sokhta in Seistan, to the royal graves of Ur. Around 1750 BCE a customer named Nanni complained on a clay tablet to Ea-nasir, a Dilmun copper trader at Ur, about substandard ingots — often called the oldest customer complaint on record.

    03 · The caravan road

    Letters from Kanesh

    Between about 1950 and 1750 BCE, merchant houses of Assur loaded donkeys with tin brought from farther east and with woollen cloth, much of it woven by the women of Assur, and walked them some 1,000 km across the Jazira, the Euphrates and the Taurus to Kanesh (Kültepe, near Kayseri), bringing silver and gold home. We know because they wrote: about 23,000 Old Assyrian tablets from the merchants' quarter at Kültepe record prices, loans, tolls, smuggling and family quarrels — the largest private archive from the ancient Near East, including letters from wives such as Lamassi and Taram-Kubi, who ran the home end of the business.

    04 · The chariot

    A war machine crosses Asia

    Graves at Sintashta in the southern Urals hold the oldest known spoked-wheel chariots, radiocarbon-dated to c. 2000 BCE — light cars behind paired horses steered with antler cheekpieces. The package then travelled faster than any empire: the Hittites fielded chariots by about 1650 BCE, Egypt had them by the start of the New Kingdom, c. 1550, and they appear fully formed in royal burials at Anyang, the late Shang capital, around 1200 BCE, carried across the steppe by Andronovo-period herders or their neighbours. How it reached each place is argued, but a horse-training manual by Kikkuli of Mitanni, filed in the archives of Hattusa, still counts its laps in Indo-Aryan numerals.

    05 · North America

    Across the ice, down the river

    From about 2500 BCE, people of the Arctic Small Tool tradition — microblades, burins, the bow and arrow — spread east from Alaska's Bering Strait coast, where the Denbigh Flint complex was first defined at Cape Denbigh, across the Canadian Arctic and over Smith Sound into Greenland, where the Saqqaq culture begins c. 2400 BCE. At Qeqertasussuk in Disko Bay, a tuft of Saqqaq hair preserved in permafrost gave, in 2010, the first ancient human genome ever sequenced. Far to the south, the fisher-foragers of Poverty Point, Louisiana (c. 1700–1100 BCE), drew galena from Missouri and Iowa, soapstone from the southern Appalachian foothills and copper, some of it from the Great Lakes — sources hundreds of kilometres away, over 1,000 km for the farthest, in a network run by people who did not farm.

    06 · Australia

    Old channels, a new dog

    In south-west Victoria, the Gunditjmara's eel traps and channels at Budj Bim, cut into an old lava flow and one of them radiocarbon-dated to about 6,600 years ago, were already older than the city of Uruk and still in use — a managed wetland, now a World Heritage site. Around 3,500 years ago a newcomer arrived by boat from island Southeast Asia: the dingo. The oldest directly dated dingo bones, from Madura Cave on the Nullarbor Plain, are 3,350–3,080 years old (re-dated in 2018); genetic estimates run earlier, and who brought it, and when, is still contested. Dingoes spread across the mainland but never reached Tasmania, cut off by the sea thousands of years before, and by about 3,200 years ago the thylacine and Tasmanian devil had vanished from the mainland; dingoes, growing human populations and a drying climate are all suspects.

    Milestones

    Thirty-nine turning points

    c. 3500 BCE

    The wheel & wheeled vehicles

    Mesopotamia & the Eurasian steppe

    Whether it grew from the potter's wheel or from rollers under sledges, the cart and wagon appear almost at once across Eurasia — Uruk pictographs, the Bronocice pot, steppe wagon burials. The later spoked-wheel chariot remade war; yet the wheel went unused for transport in the pre-Columbian Americas, which had no draft animals.

    c. 3500–3100 BCE

    Uruk & the birth of the city

    Sumer, southern Iraq

    Uruk swelled to some 250 hectares and perhaps 40,000 people by c. 3100 BCE — an order of magnitude beyond its neighbours — with monumental temples, mass-produced bowls, canal irrigation, and full-time specialists. The template of urban life: anonymous crowds, sharp stratification, and the administrative pressure that would soon force writing into being.

    c. 3300 BCE

    Cuneiform — the first writing

    Sumer (Uruk)

    Born from accounting — clay tokens and sealed tallies of grain, beer, and sheep — pictographs pressed into wet clay hardened into abstract wedge signs recording Sumerian. Writing decoupled memory from the human lifespan, and would in time carry the Epic of Gilgamesh, humanity's oldest surviving epic — though not its oldest literature: the Sumerian Instructions of Shuruppak and the Kesh Temple Hymn survive on tablets of c. 2600–2500 BCE.

    c. 3250–3100 BCE

    Hieroglyphs & a unified Egypt

    Nile Valley, Egypt

    Hieroglyphic labels at Abydos are as old as the earliest cuneiform — most likely an independent birth of writing. The Narmer Palette (c. 3100 BCE) records the joining of Upper and Lower Egypt into the world's first large territorial state, a god-king's realm that would endure three millennia.

    c. 3300–3000 BCE

    Bronze metallurgy goes mainstream

    Near East & Caucasus

    Alloying copper with tin produced a metal harder than either, for tools, weapons, and prestige goods. Because tin was rare (Central Asia, the Taurus, later Cornwall) and copper concentrated (Cyprus, Oman, the Alps), bronze forced the first continent-spanning trade — and made metal a currency of state power.

    c. 3400–2000 BCE

    The first Caribbean islanders

    Cuba, Puerto Rico & Hispaniola

    The Antilles are the only islands in the Americas settled across water from which no land could be seen. A 2019 Bayesian model of vetted radiocarbon dates puts the first people on Cuba c. 3400–2700 BCE (older, unvetted dates run earlier) and on Puerto Rico and Hispaniola by c. 2700–2000 BCE — though a 2023 re-analysis of more than a thousand Puerto Rican dates puts people there by c. 4300–4050 BCE, before Cuba; which mainland coast they sailed from is still argued. At Canímar Abajo in Cuba, a cemetery in use from c. 2200 BCE holds the oldest human remains yet reported from the islands: starch and bone chemistry show that these supposed fisher-gatherers ate cultivated plants, and strontium in their teeth (2025) shows a community that stayed put.

    c. 3300–2300 BCE

    Liangzhu — a water state on the Yangtze

    Lower Yangtze, Zhejiang, China

    More than a thousand years before Erlitou, the Liangzhu culture walled a 290-hectare city near today's Hangzhou, raised a 30-hectare palace platform at Mojiaoshan, and dammed the valleys to the north — high dams whose radiocarbon dates cluster at c. 3250–2850 BCE, in a flood-control system among the earliest large-scale water works known anywhere. Jade cong and bi were concentrated in elite graves; the city has been a World Heritage site since 2019. Cave stalagmites record decades of torrential rain shortly before 2300 BCE, when the capital was abandoned: East Asia's first state-scale society, if that is what it was, was undone by too much water.

    c. 3300 BCE

    Cacao in the upper Amazon

    Santa Ana-La Florida, south-east Ecuador

    Mayo-Chinchipe pottery from the Amazonian foothills carries three independent traces of cacao — its starch grains, absorbed theobromine and ancient cacao DNA — about 5,300 years old: the earliest evidence of cacao use in the Americas, some 1,400 years before the Mokaya of Pacific Mexico were drinking it (Zarrillo et al. 2018). It makes the upper Amazon, where the tree's wild genetic diversity is greatest, the oldest centre of cacao domestication yet identified — crop-making in Amazonia in the same centuries the Old World was inventing the city.

    c. 3000–1800 BCE

    Caral-Supe & Norte Chico

    Supe Valley, coastal Peru

    Platform mounds and sunken circular plazas — planned cities contemporary with Egypt's pyramids — built without pottery, metal, or writing, though knotted khipu-like cords appear here. Proof that complex urban society arose independently in the Americas, fed by irrigation cotton and the teeming Pacific fishery.

    c. 3000–2500 BCE

    The steppe turnover, read from DNA

    Pontic-Caspian steppe → Central Europe

    Ancient genomes published in 2015 by Wolfgang Haak, David Reich and colleagues showed that after c. 2900 BCE roughly three-quarters of central European ancestry was replaced by people carrying Yamnaya steppe ancestry, arriving with ox-drawn wagons and probably early Indo-European speech. Whether they rode is argued: five Yamnaya skeletons show wear read as a rider's (2023), but 475 ancient horse genomes date the modern domestic horse, and horse-based mobility, to c. 2200 BCE (2024). A parallel 2015 survey of Bronze Age skeletons by Eske Willerslev's group found Yersinia pestis in seven Eurasian individuals, the oldest an Afanasievo burial of c. 2800 BCE; a Latvian hunter-fisher sequenced in 2021 pushed the pathogen back to c. 3300 BCE, and in 2024 it turned up across Neolithic Scandinavian farming communities — though these early lineages lacked the gene that lets fleas transmit it.

    c. 3000–2300 BCE

    Lothagam North — a monument without chiefs

    West of Lake Turkana, Kenya

    As the African Humid Phase ended and Lake Turkana fell, eastern Africa's first herders dug a mortuary cavity inside a boulder-ringed platform marked with stone pillars and buried an estimated minimum of 580 people in it — thought to be the region's earliest and largest monumental cemetery. People of both sexes and many ages went in with ornaments, yet the excavators found “no evidence for social stratification” (Hildebrand et al. 2018). Mobile pastoralists in an unstable world built a monument without a city, a surplus or a king.

    c. 3000–1100 BCE

    Australia remade, without cities

    Aboriginal Australia

    Small stone blades blunted (“backed”) along one edge for hafting, made in small numbers since the end of the Ice Age, proliferated in the mid-Holocene to become the commonest retouched tool in the south-east — on Peter Hiscock's reading, one way of reducing risk once an El Niño-dominated climate, setting in 4,000–5,000 years ago, made rainfall less predictable. Pama-Nyungan languages spread from the Gulf Plains in the same broad era to cover some seven-eighths of the continent, and the dingo arrived by sea: bones from Madura Cave are directly dated to 3,350–3,080 years ago. Whether this adds up to deliberate “intensification” or adaptation to a harsher climate is a live debate.

    c. 2560 BCE

    The Great Pyramid of Giza

    Giza, Old Kingdom Egypt

    Khufu's tomb — 146 m tall, some 2.3 million blocks, aligned to true north within a twentieth of a degree — stayed the tallest human structure for nearly 4,000 years. It was raised not by slaves but by rotating levies of paid, bread-and-beer-rationed workers: the logistical reach of the early bureaucratic state.

    c. 2600–1900 BCE

    The Indus Valley civilization

    Indus & Ghaggar-Hakra basins

    The largest Bronze Age civilization by area: grid-planned Mohenjo-daro and Harappa with the world's earliest urban sanitation — covered drains, private bathrooms, standardized bricks and weights — yet no clear palaces, royal tombs, or signs of war. Its short seal inscriptions remain undeciphered, leaving it voiceless.

    c. 2500–2000 BCE

    The peopling of the High Arctic

    Alaska → Arctic Canada → Greenland

    Carriers of the Arctic Small Tool tradition crossed some 5,000 km of tundra and sea ice in a few centuries, reaching Greenland as the Saqqaq and Independence I cultures — the last habitable frontier on Earth, taken with microblades, tailored skin clothing, and soapstone lamps. It is an engineering achievement on the order of any irrigation system in this chapter. In 2010 a team led by Eske Willerslev sequenced a Saqqaq man's preserved hair from Qeqertasussuk: the first ancient human genome ever recovered, and proof that these people were a distinct migration from the later Inuit.

    c. 2500–1000 BCE

    Pearl millet & the stone villages of Dhar Tichitt

    Tilemsi valley, Mali · Dhar Tichitt, Mauritania

    West Africa bred its own cereal for a Sahel where wheat and barley fail: impressions in potsherds at Karkarichinkat in Mali's Tilemsi valley show domesticated pearl millet by c. 2500 BCE. On the sandstone escarpment of Dhar Tichitt in southern Mauritania, herders were growing millet by c. 1900–1700 BCE and, from the middle of the millennium, building villages of dry-stone compounds and granaries, some of them walled — hundreds of settlements over the tradition's life, and West Africa's earliest known complex society. Archaeology and linguistics make its people probable ancestors of the Soninke, founders of the later Ghana empire.

    c. 2334 BCE

    Sargon of Akkad — first empire

    Akkad & Sumer

    Sargon fused the Sumerian city-states into one multi-ethnic empire administered in Akkadian — pioneering imperial governance, standardized administration, and royal propaganda. His daughter Enheduanna, high priestess of Ur, is the first author in history known by name — if the poems that carry her name are hers.

    c. 2300 BCE

    Enheduanna, the first named author

    Ur, Sumer

    Sargon's daughter, installed as high priestess of the moon-god Nanna, is named as the author of hymns: The Exaltation of Inanna speaks in her voice about exile, a usurper, and vindication, and is the earliest literature anywhere attributed to a named individual — though every copy is Old Babylonian, five centuries after her death, and some Sumerologists doubt she composed the poems that name her. That the first author we can name is a woman running a temple estate as an instrument of imperial policy is a useful correction to how this era is usually pictured.

    c. 2500–1500 BCE

    Kerma & the kingdoms of Nubia

    Upper Nile, Sudan

    Africa's oldest urban state beyond Egypt: walled Kerma, with its massive mud-brick deffufa, controlled the gold, ivory, and ebony flowing north. By c. 1750 BCE a walled city of some 25 hectares, it raided deep into Egypt c. 1575–1550 BCE and was courted as an ally by the Hyksos king in the north, until Thutmose I destroyed it c. 1500 BCE — a corrective to the notion that the Nile's civilization was Egyptian alone.

    c. 2480–1470 BCE

    The sea road to Punt

    Red Sea & the Horn of Africa

    The first recorded Egyptian expedition to Punt, under Sahure in the 25th century BCE, brought back cargo and Puntites; Middle Kingdom crews sailed from Mersa Gawasis on the Red Sea, where excavators have found the timbers of seagoing ships and stelae recording Punt voyages; and Hatshepsut's year-9 expedition (c. 1470 BCE) is carved at Deir el-Bahri with myrrh trees, baboons, ebony and gold. Where Punt lay was long argued; in 2020 oxygen and strontium isotopes in two mummified New Kingdom baboons traced them to a region spanning much of today's Ethiopia, Eritrea and Djibouti and parts of Somalia and Yemen, backing the majority view — the Horn of Africa.

    c. 2200–1700 BCE

    The Oxus civilization (BMAC)

    Bactria & Margiana — Turkmenistan, Afghanistan, Uzbekistan

    A fifth Bronze Age urban civilization that almost nobody can name: monumental walled compounds at Gonur Depe and Togolok, irrigated from the Murghab delta, excavated from 1972 by Viktor Sarianidi. Its stamp seals, composite chlorite-and-limestone figurines, and lapis reached both Mesopotamia and the Indus, which makes Central Asia a participant in the Bronze Age system rather than the blank space between its civilizations.

    c. 2100–1800 BCE

    Sintashta & the spoked-wheel chariot

    Southern Urals & the Kazakh steppe

    Fortified circular settlements on the Ural steppe left graves containing the oldest known chariots — light, two-wheeled, spoked, drawn by paired horses, radiocarbon-dated to c. 2000 BCE at Sintashta and Krivoye Ozero. Within four or five centuries chariots were fighting for Hatti and Egypt; by c. 1250–1200 BCE they had reached Shang China, where the earliest chariot burials lie at Anyang; the descendant Andronovo cultures carried the technology, the horses, and Indo-Iranian languages east across Asia.

    c. 2000–1450 BCE

    Minoan Crete — palaces & the sea

    Crete & the Aegean

    Europe's first literate society — the labyrinthine palace of Knossos, frescoes of bull-leapers and dolphins, the still-undeciphered Linear A, and a trade web across the eastern Mediterranean. The Thera eruption (c. 1600 BCE) shook the region before Mycenaean Greeks absorbed the island.

    c. 2000–1000 BCE

    Bronze reaches Southeast Asia

    Khorat plateau, Thailand

    Rice farmers at Ban Chiang and Ban Non Wat cast socketed axes and bangles in bivalve moulds from copper mined at Phu Lon — a metal industry run out of villages, with no palace, no king, and no script to organize it. Its date is a live fight: Joyce White reads the Ban Chiang sequence as starting near 2000 BCE, while Charles Higham's radiocarbon series from Ban Non Wat puts first bronze around 1050 BCE. Either way, Southeast Asia worked bronze on its own terms.

    c. 2000 BCE

    Leprosy at Balathal

    Rajasthan, north-west India

    A middle-aged man buried at Balathal, a farming village of the post-urban Indus age, shows lesions of the face, joints and shin characteristic of lepromatous leprosy — the oldest skeletal evidence for the disease yet documented, some 1,400 years before the first texts to describe it (Robbins et al. 2009). It places a chronic infectious disease in South Asia's villages in the centuries when the great Indus cities were emptying, and shows how much of disease history can be read only from bone.

    c. 1750–1500 BCE

    Erlitou — China's first palace-city

    Yellow River, Henan

    Just before the Shang — radiocarbon since 2007 has moved its start from c. 1900 to c. 1750 BCE — Erlitou laid rammed-earth palace platforms on a north-south axis, ran East Asia's earliest known bronze foundry, and cast the first ritual jue vessels — the template every later Chinese dynasty inherited. Whether it is the semi-legendary Xia dynasty is unresolved and politically loaded: the archaeology is solid, the identification with a name from far later texts is not.

    c. 1800 BCE

    Plimpton 322 — Babylonian mathematics

    Probably Larsa, southern Iraq

    A clay tablet of four columns and fifteen rows of base-60 numbers lists Pythagorean triples — whole-number sides of right-angled triangles, some of them very large — more than a thousand years before Pythagoras. Old Babylonian scribes calculated in a place-value sexagesimal notation, the one that still divides our hours into minutes and seconds, and set their students quadratic problems. What the tablet was for is argued: most specialists read it as a teacher's aid built from reciprocal pairs; a 2017 claim that it is a trigonometric table has won little support.

    c. 1754 BCE

    The Code of Hammurabi

    Babylon, Mesopotamia

    282 laws cut on a black stone stele — basalt by the Louvre's reckoning, diorite in older references; the question is still open — beneath the king receiving authority from the sun-god Shamash — wages, contracts, malpractice, divorce, graded "eye for an eye" penalties. Not the first code (Ur-Nammu precedes it by ~300 years) but the emblem of publicly proclaimed, state-guaranteed justice, "that the strong might not oppress the weak." Its clauses also legislate the ordinary machinery of coercion: debt bondage capped at three years, and prices set for the sale of a person.

    c. 1700–1100 BCE

    Poverty Point, Louisiana

    Lower Mississippi valley, North America

    Six concentric earthen ridges nearly 1.2 km across and a 22 m mound — parts of it, radiocarbon and soil micromorphology suggest, thrown up in a matter of months — raised by people who did not farm: fishers and foragers of the Mississippi backswamps. Their galena came down the Mississippi from Missouri and Iowa, their soapstone from the southern Appalachian foothills and some of their copper from the Great Lakes — the farthest sources more than 1,000 km away. Monumental building and long-distance exchange did not require agriculture, grain taxes, or kings.

    c. 1700–1400 BCE

    Rubber balls & the first ballcourt

    Gulf coast & Pacific Chiapas, Mexico

    Offerings sunk in El Manatí, a sacred spring-fed bog 15 km from San Lorenzo, preserved rubber balls, the oldest dated as early as c. 1700–1600 BCE — the oldest balls known. By c. 1400 BCE villagers at Paso de la Amada, on the Pacific coast of Chiapas, had built an earthen court some 80 m long, the oldest ballcourt yet found (Hill, Blake & Clark 1998). The game — sport, spectacle and ritual — would run for nearly three thousand years through Olmec, Maya and Aztec worlds.

    c. 1650–1250 BCE

    The first domestic chickens

    Ban Non Wat, Khorat plateau, Thailand

    The earliest unambiguous bones of domestic chickens yet identified come from Neolithic Ban Non Wat, the same village whose graves later filled with bronze. A 2022 reassessment of the bird's archaeology argues that grain farming made it: stored cereal drew red junglefowl down from the trees and into the human niche. Claims of far older chickens elsewhere did not survive the re-examination, and the bird reached Mediterranean Europe only c. 800 BCE.

    c. 2500 & c. 1600 BCE

    Stonehenge's sarsens & the Nebra sky disc

    Britain & central Germany

    Europe north of the Aegean had no cities and no writing, and built anyway. The sarsen circle went up around 2500 BCE; in 2020 geochemical fingerprinting by David Nash's team traced 50 of its 52 stones to West Woods, 25 km away. The Nebra disc (c. 1600 BCE) sets a gold sun, moon, and the Pleiades on bronze — Únětice metalwork whose copper came from the Alps and whose gold came from Cornwall, an astronomical instrument assembled entirely out of imports.

    c. 1600 / 1250 BCE

    Shang China & the oracle bones

    Yellow River (Anyang)

    Kings divined by cracking heated ox scapulae and turtle shells, inscribing questions and answers — harvests, war, royal births — in the earliest Chinese writing, the direct ancestor of characters used by over a billion people today. Shang piece-mold casting produced ritual bronzes of unmatched sophistication.

    c. 1400–1200 BCE

    San Lorenzo & the early Olmec

    Gulf coast, Veracruz, Mexico

    Mesoamerica's first monumental centre: an artificially raised plateau, drains cut from basalt, and — at its height, c. 1200–900 BCE, just after this chapter's close — colossal portrait heads up to 3 m tall hauled some 60 km from the Tuxtla mountains — moved without metal tools, draft animals, or wheels. Maize here was still being bred toward the productivity it would later have, so this monumentality ran ahead of full farming dependence rather than following from it.

    c. 3000–1200 BCE

    Austronesian & Lapita voyagers

    Taiwan → the western Pacific

    As states rose on the continents, Austronesian farmer-sailors began history's greatest maritime migration — outrigger canoes bearing pigs, taro, and dentate-stamped Lapita pottery past the Solomons into Remote Oceania around 1100–1000 BCE. A separate voyage had already crossed more than 2,000 km of open sea from the Philippines to the Marianas — c. 1500 BCE on the excavators' dating, though stricter re-analyses of the radiocarbon bring it close to Lapita's own landfall. Open-ocean navigation without writing, metal, or the wheel.

    c. 1350–1300 BCE

    The Bronze Age international system

    Eastern Mediterranean

    Egypt, Hatti, Babylon, Assyria, and Mycenae bound by diplomacy (the Amarna letters, written in Akkadian, the age's lingua franca), royal marriage, and trade. The Uluburun wreck (c. 1320 BCE) carried ten tons of Cypriot copper, a ton of tin, Baltic amber, African ivory, and Canaanite jars — one cargo touching at least seven cultures.

    1274 BCE

    Kadesh & the first surviving peace treaty

    Orontes valley, Syria — Egypt & Hatti

    The largest chariot battle known, perhaps 5,000 vehicles, fought to a draw and then carved as a triumph on every temple wall Ramesses II could reach. Sixteen years later Egypt and Hatti signed a parity treaty of mutual defence and extradition, preserved both in Egyptian hieroglyphs at Karnak and on an Akkadian cuneiform tablet from Hattusa — the earliest peace treaty whose text survives from both sides, and the earliest war with two rival official accounts to set against each other.

    c. 1200 BCE

    Sanxingdui — a civilization with no text

    Sichuan basin, China

    Sacrificial pits found by brick-factory workers in 1986, with six more dug in 2020–22, held bronze masks with protruding eyes, a 2.6 m standing figure, and a bronze tree nearly 4 m tall — a wealthy, technically extraordinary culture contemporary with the Shang that left not one written word and no mention in any Chinese chronicle. It is the standing measure of how much the written record leaves out.

    c. 1200–1150 BCE

    The Late Bronze Age Collapse

    Eastern Mediterranean

    In about fifty years the Hittite Empire fell, Ugarit and dozens of cities burned, Mycenaean palaces emptied, Egypt barely repelled the "Sea Peoples," and Linear B literacy vanished for four centuries. A systems failure — drought, earthquakes, migration, and severed trade — cascading through an over-connected world. From the wreckage came iron, the alphabet, and new peoples.

    Why it happened

    Floodwater, tin, and the hands that were made to carry it

    Cities did not appear because someone had the idea. Three conditions had to meet: land that produced a storable surplus, a technology whose raw materials no single region owned, and an institution willing to compel labour. Where all three lined up, the same machinery assembled itself independently on four continents.

    Start with water. Southern Iraq receives barely 100 mm of rain a year and grows nothing without irrigation; the Nile floods on a schedule you can plan a harvest around; the Indus and the Yellow River carry silt that renews their fields. Floodplain farming concentrated calories in narrow strips, which concentrated people, which made canals worth digging and worth defending. Karl Wittfogel turned that into a general law — irrigation demands central management, therefore despotism — but the excavated canals of early Mesopotamia are mostly short, local, and older than the states that supposedly required them. Coordination did not have to come first; it followed, and then it never let go. Irrigation also carried a slow poison: as Thorkild Jacobsen and Robert Adams argued from Sumerian field records, salt accumulated until salt-tolerant barley displaced wheat almost entirely in the south by 1700 BCE, one of the earliest human-made ecological reversals we can measure. Marvin Powell answered in 1985 that the texts show no such steady ruin, and the reading is still argued. Water could also be engineered on a grand scale with no Mesopotamian model at all: on the lower Yangtze, Liangzhu's builders had dammed the valleys above their capital by about 3100 BCE.

    Then the arithmetic of people. Perhaps 14 million humans were alive in 3000 BCE, around 27 million by 2000 BCE, and roughly 50 million by 1000 BCE — those are Colin McEvedy and Richard Jones's figures; the HYDE 3.3 reconstruction puts the same three dates at about 45, 73 and 110 million, so the honest error bar is a factor of two or three — a species that would fit into a handful of modern megacities, growing at a fraction of a percent per year. Against that, the cities are startling: Uruk at perhaps 40,000, Mohenjo-daro about the same, Memphis around 30,000, Anyang larger still. Yet even at the height of the Bronze Age well under one person in twenty lived in a town of any size. The era's significance is not that most people became urban — they did not, for another four thousand years — but that a small urban minority acquired the power to tax, conscript, and record everyone else. (The long arc of that curve is drawn on the population page.)

    The machinery was not the only road to a monument, though. At Lothagam North, beside a shrinking Lake Turkana, eastern Africa's first herders built a cemetery for hundreds with no trace of rank; at Poverty Point, fishers and foragers raised earthen ridges nearly 1.2 km across; Caral's platform mounds went up without pottery. These cases reverse the textbook order — surplus, then state, then monument — and suggest that gathering people to build could come first, binding scattered communities together in uncertain times, with rank and tax arriving later or not at all. The farming base itself was still widening beyond the cradles: pearl millet in the Sahel, cacao in the upper Amazon, the chicken in a rice village in Thailand, and cultivated plants in the diet of Cuba's first islanders.

    Bronze then did something no earlier technology had done: it made self-sufficiency impossible. Copper is common enough — Cyprus, Oman, the Alps, Lake Superior — but tin is geologically rare and almost never occurs beside it. To arm a Mesopotamian army you needed tin carried from Central Asia — perhaps Afghanistan — by people you did not rule, through polities you could not tax. Isotope work on the Uluburun ingots has traced a single ship's tin to sources as far apart as Central Asia and the Taurus mountains (a 2023 critique disputes the Central Asian match and favours south-west England for part of it). That is the deep reason the Late Bronze Age was both spectacular and brittle: the palace economies were welded to supply lines thousands of kilometres long, and when the lines were cut there was no local substitute. Iron, whose ore is everywhere, is what a world learns to use after that lesson.

    None of it ran on volunteers. Coerced labour is structural to every polity in this chapter, in graded forms: the rotating corvée levies that raised Giza's pyramids, fed on state bread and beer, whose logistics survive in papyri found in 2013 at Wadi al-Jarf — the logbook of an official named Merer, whose crew of about forty men shipped limestone from the Tura quarries to Giza late in Khufu's reign; the guruš work-gangs of the Ur III state, issued about 60 litres of barley a month for a man and 30 for a woman and accounted for like livestock across tens of thousands of tablets; debt bondage, which Hammurabi capped at three years because it was ordinary enough to need capping; and outright chattel slavery, with prices in shekels written into the same law code. At the far end of the scale, the royal tumuli of Classic Kerma held dozens, perhaps hundreds, of people buried with the king — though whether all of them were killed for the funeral is now argued. Mycenaean Linear B tablets from Pylos list hundreds of women and children in textile workshops, some designated by the places they came from — Miletus, Knidos, Lemnos — which is what a captive workforce looks like in an accounting document. Those workshops matter twice, because cloth was the era's great manufactured export and it was made overwhelmingly by women — at Pylos under compulsion, and in Assur by merchants' wives such as Lamassī, whose letters to Kanesh discuss the cloth they wove and what it earned. Women also appear where power was: Enheduanna named as the first author, Puabi buried at Ur with a retinue, Kubaba remembered as ruler of Kish, and Hatshepsut ruling Egypt as regent from 1479 BCE and as crowned king in her own right by c. 1472. A history of this era that mentions only kings has misplaced most of its labour force and some of its rulers.

    Crowding brought its own passenger. Dense towns beside penned animals are exactly the conditions in which livestock diseases learn to live in humans, and the genomes agree: Yersinia pestis has been sequenced from a Latvian hunter-fisher of about 3300 BCE, long before any written record of plague. The earliest epidemic orders we can read are older — c. 1775 BCE King Zimri-Lim of Mari wrote that no one should drink from the cup, sit on the chair or sleep in the bed of a sick palace woman, and his officials asked that people from towns 'where the god is striking' be kept out of healthy ones — but the first epidemic we can follow at length arrives late in this chapter, when Muršili II of Hatti prayed for release from a pestilence that had killed for twenty years and which his own texts blamed on prisoners brought back from Egypt — disease travelling the same roads as the tin. Bone keeps a longer record than text: a man buried at Balathal in Rajasthan c. 2000 BCE carries the oldest known skeletal lesions of leprosy, and in Neolithic Scandinavia at least 17 per cent of 108 people sampled carried plague, in three outbreaks within about 120 years, during the population slump of c. 3300–2900 BCE. Whether plague drove that “Neolithic decline” is one of the era's live questions.

    Climate frames both ends. Around 2200 BCE a sharp, roughly two-century arid pulse — the 4.2 ka event, now the formal boundary of the Meghalayan geological age — coincides with the fall of Akkad, the end of Egypt's Old Kingdom, and disruption in the Indus. Harvey Weiss reads the dust layers at Tell Leilan as the cause; critics answer that the political chronologies do not line up neatly, that the Indus decline was staggered over centuries, and that drought is a stress test rather than a verdict. Treat the link as contested. Not every crisis was drought: on the Yangtze, stalagmites record decades of torrential monsoon rain shortly before 2300 BCE, when Liangzhu's capital was abandoned. Around 1200 BCE a second dry phase, visible in pollen cores from Cyprus and the Levantine coast — and in juniper rings from Gordion in central Anatolia as an unusually severe run of dry years c. 1198–1196 BCE (±3), just as the Hittite state fell — meets earthquake damage, migration, and severed trade in the same fifty years; the climate spine runs under this whole tour. What crawled out of the wreckage — cheap iron, an alphabet of twenty-odd signs carried by Levantine traders, and peoples the palaces had never bothered to name — is the raw material of the next chapter.

    Competing explanations

    • Why cities at all? Surplus and coordination, ritual centres that drew crowds first, or trade nodes that grew where routes crossed. The three are not exclusive, and different cradles look like different mixes.
    • Did irrigation make despots? Wittfogel's hydraulic hypothesis is elegant and poorly supported: early canals were local, and the state arrives after them.
    • Did the 4.2 ka drought topple Akkad? Widely repeated, genuinely contested — the aridity is well attested, the causal link to specific political collapses is not.
    • Who were the Hyksos? Long told as an Asiatic invasion of Egypt; strontium-isotope work on burials at Tell el-Dab'a points instead to a long-resident immigrant community that rose from within.
    • What ended the Bronze Age? The "Sea Peoples" are as plausibly a symptom of collapse as its cause; the systems-failure reading — drought, quake, revolt, and cut trade compounding — now has the better of the argument, but no single trigger has been demonstrated.
    • Was Liangzhu a state? Colin Renfrew and Liu Bin (2018) argue that its walls, dams and graded burials make it an early state a millennium before Erlitou; others reserve the word for societies with writing and bureaucracy. The label decides whether China's first state rose on the Yangtze or the Yellow River.
    • Did plague cause the Neolithic decline? Plague DNA in at least a sixth of sampled Scandinavian farmers (2024) makes it a suspect; but those early strains could not spread by fleas, and climate, soil exhaustion and social upheaval remain rival explanations.
    • Did the Yamnaya ride? Skeletal wear in five burials of c. 3000–2500 BCE is read as riding (2023); horse genomes put the modern domestic horse and mass horse mobility after c. 2200 BCE (2024). Both can be true — a few riders on local horses long before the great herds.
    • Did salt ruin Sumer? Thorkild Jacobsen and Robert Adams (1958) read wheat's retreat and falling yields in temple records as a salinization crisis; Marvin Powell (1985) argued that the texts show no progressive decline and that barley was favoured for other reasons. Salt is a real hazard of irrigation in the region; its part in Sumer's decline is unproven.
    • Did Aboriginal Australia “intensify”? Harry Lourandos read mid-Holocene change as social intensification — more people, more ritual, more engineered landscapes; Peter Hiscock reads the backed blades as risk management in a less predictable climate. Much of the evidence can be read either way.
    • Where a claim on this page is disputed, it is flagged; the register of live debates lives at How We Know · contested.

    Meanwhile, everywhere

    Sixteen regions, twenty-three centuries

    Cities and writing arose in a handful of river valleys. Everywhere else people farmed, herded, fished, voyaged and built on their own terms, and most of what we know about them comes from excavation, radiocarbon and ancient DNA rather than from texts.

    West Asia

    c. 3500–1200 BCE

    Sumer's neighbours kept their own books. Elam, centred on Susa in south-west Iran, wrote a proto-Elamite script c. 3100–2900 BCE that is still largely unread, and in 2004 BCE its armies sacked Ur and ended the Ur III state. On the Gulf, the Umm an-Nar people of Magan (c. 2600–2000 BCE) built round stone tombs and towers beside their wells, and Dilmun, on Bahrain, raised tens of thousands of burial mounds. Hittite kings ruled from Hattusa from c. 1650 BCE, and Ugarit, on the Syrian coast, wrote in a 30-letter cuneiform alphabet until the city was destroyed c. 1185 BCE.

    North Africa & the Nile

    c. 3500–1200 BCE

    Egypt's Old, Middle and New Kingdoms ruled the valley for most of these centuries, separated by breakdowns that began c. 2180 and c. 1650 BCE. Upstream, Lower Nubia's A-Group grew rich trading with the first pharaohs, then vanishes c. 2900 BCE; a rock relief at Gebel Sheikh Suleiman seems to show an early king's victory over them. West of the Nile the Green Sahara was drying out, and the Libyan peoples of its margins pressed on the Delta: in his fifth year, c. 1208 BCE, Merneptah defeated a Libu army that had brought its women and children, and fighters of the "Sea Peoples" with it.

    West & Central Africa

    c. 3500–1200 BCE

    West Africa made its own farming. Pearl millet was domesticated in Mali's Tilemsi valley by c. 2500 BCE, and on the cliffs of Dhar Tichitt in Mauritania herders later built villages of dry-stone compounds. In Ghana, Kintampo villagers (c. 2500–1400 BCE) kept goats and cattle, grew cowpeas, ate oil-palm nuts and left puzzling ridged "cigars" of stone and clay. From the Cameroon–Nigeria grasslands, Bantu-speaking farmers began a spread that would carry their languages over much of the continent; and in central Nigeria the farming Nok culture may already have emerged by c. 1500 BCE, some six centuries before its first terracottas and longer still before its iron.

    East & Southern Africa

    c. 3500–1200 BCE

    Herders reached the Lake Turkana basin as the lake shrank, and c. 3000–2300 BCE built pillar-ringed communal cemeteries such as Lothagam North. Herding spread south slowly: at Luxmanda in northern Tanzania, the southernmost known settlement of the Savanna Pastoral Neolithic, people with cattle, sheep, goats and donkeys were living by c. 1200 BCE, and an ancient genome from the site carries ancestry traced in part to the Levant. The Horn of Africa sent incense, ebony and baboons to Egyptian ships as the land of Punt. South of the Zambezi everyone still lived by foraging, and Madagascar had no lasting settlers; cut-marked bones hint at visits c. 2000 BCE, a claim still argued.

    Europe & the Mediterranean

    c. 3500–1200 BCE

    Ötzi died on an Alpine pass c. 3300 BCE, a copper axe beside him and a flint arrowhead in his shoulder. After c. 2500 BCE the Bell Beaker way of life spread across the west, and in Britain newcomers with steppe-related ancestry replaced about 90 per cent of the local gene pool within a few centuries. El Argar in south-east Iberia (c. 2200–1550 BCE) built hilltop towns such as La Almoloya and gave up the old collective tombs for single graves; Sardinians began raising the stone towers called nuraghi, of which more than 7,000 survive; and in the 13th century BCE perhaps 4,000 warriors fought in the Tollense valley of northern Germany, while Aegean palaces kept their accounts in Linear A and then Linear B.

    Central Asia & the Steppe

    c. 3500–1200 BCE

    Yamnaya herders with ox-drawn wagons spread across the Pontic–Caspian steppe from c. 3300 BCE, and the Afanasievo culture of the Altai (c. 3300–2500 BCE), whose genomes are almost indistinguishable from theirs, shows how far east the movement reached. In the Murghab delta the Oxus civilization built walled centres such as Gonur Depe (c. 2200–1700 BCE), while Seima-Turbino casters carried socketed bronze spearheads of Altai tin across the northern forests (c. 2200–1900 BCE). The modern domestic horse was bred on the lower Volga–Don around 2200 BCE; Sintashta's chariots followed, and Andronovo herders took horses and bronze east across the steppe.

    South Asia

    c. 3500–1200 BCE

    The Indus cities peaked c. 2600–1900 BCE: five great centres, Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Ganeriwala, Dholavira and Rakhigarhi, among more than a thousand known sites. As the monsoon-fed rivers of the Ghaggar-Hakra shrank to seasonal streams around 2000 BCE, people moved east into smaller villages. On the Deccan, Southern Neolithic herders piled up cattle dung and burned it, again and again, into great ash mounds that still keep the hoofprints of their herds. In the Ganga–Yamuna doab, Copper Hoard people buried axes, harpoons and flat human-shaped copper figures in the first half of the second millennium BCE, and in the north-west, poets began composing the Rigveda's hymns in an Indo-Aryan language.

    East Asia

    c. 3500–1200 BCE

    Liangzhu's water state on the lower Yangtze (c. 3300–2300 BCE) was followed by walled towns across the north: Taosi in Shanxi, with an elite quarter walled off from commoners and what may be East Asia's oldest observatory, and Shimao on the Loess Plateau, a 400-hectare stone city of c. 2000 BCE that set jades into its walls and buried some 80 skulls, mostly of young women, beneath its gate. Erlitou and then the Shang, ruling from Zhengzhou and later Anyang, made bronze-casting an instrument of kingship. In Japan, Jōmon foragers lived at Sannai-Maruyama (c. 3900–2200 BCE), the largest Jōmon settlement known, without farming; in Korea, rice farming joins millet with the Mumun culture from c. 1500 BCE.

    Southeast Asia

    c. 3500–1200 BCE

    Rice farmers moved south from China into the mainland late in the third millennium BCE; at Man Bac in northern Vietnam (c. 1850–1650 BCE) their genomes show them mixing with local hunter-gatherers, in a blend closest to today's Austroasiatic speakers. At Khok Phanom Di on the Gulf of Thailand (c. 2000–1500 BCE), a community of fishers and potters buried its dead in family clusters for some twenty generations. Austronesian speakers crossed from Taiwan to the Batanes islands of the northern Philippines by c. 2200 BCE, and the earliest known domestic chickens turn up in the rice villages of the Khorat plateau, where bronze arrived c. 1050 BCE on the strictest dating, earlier on others.

    Australia

    c. 3500–1200 BCE

    No cities, and fast change. Small backed blades multiplied across much of the mainland, Pama-Nyungan languages spread from the Gulf Plains over some seven-eighths of the continent, and the dingo arrived by sea from island Southeast Asia: the oldest directly dated bones, from Madura Cave, are 3,350–3,080 years old. Tasmania, cut off by the sea thousands of years before, never received the dingo, and by about 3,200 years ago the thylacine and the Tasmanian devil had gone from the mainland. In Victoria the Gunditjmara kept managing eels in the Budj Bim channels, already thousands of years old.

    Oceania & the Pacific

    c. 3500–1200 BCE

    In the New Guinea highlands, gardeners at places such as Kuk Swamp carried on one of the world's oldest farming traditions, draining wetland plots and growing bananas. In the Bismarck Archipelago, dentate-stamped Lapita pottery appears by c. 1350 BCE, made by Austronesian newcomers. Far to the north, voyagers from the Philippines reached the Marianas, c. 1500 BCE on the excavators' dating, closer to Lapita's own landfall on stricter re-analyses. Everything east of the Solomons, from Vanuatu to Hawaiʻi and Aotearoa, was still unpeopled.

    The Arctic

    c. 3500–1200 BCE

    Around 2500 BCE Paleo-Inuit people of the Arctic Small Tool tradition crossed from Alaska to Greenland in a few centuries: the Saqqaq in the south and west (c. 2500–800 BCE), and Independence I (c. 2400–1900 BCE) in Peary Land, the northernmost land on Earth, where people lived mainly on musk ox. The Saqqaq man's genome ties them to north-east Siberia, where the Ymyyakhtakh culture (c. 2200–1300 BCE) spread waffle-stamped pottery and early bronze from the Lena towards Chukotka. On Norway's Arctic coast at Alta, hunters kept adding to a gallery of rock carvings, reindeer above all, made from c. 4200 BCE (a millennium earlier on a 2010 re-dating) to c. 500 BCE.

    North America

    c. 3500–1200 BCE

    Foragers at Watson Brake in Louisiana began raising an oval of eleven earthen mounds c. 3500 BCE, North America's oldest mound complex, long before Poverty Point (c. 1700–1100 BCE). In the eastern woodlands people domesticated plants of their own: squash, sunflower, marsh elder and goosefoot were all cultivated by c. 1800 BCE. Potters on the Savannah River made fibre-tempered Stallings ware, the oldest known pottery in North America, after c. 2500 BCE; maize reached the Southwest from Mexico by c. 2100 BCE, passed from group to group; and from c. 3000 BCE people on the Georgia and South Carolina coasts heaped oyster shell into rings, typically 50–65 m across, around open centres, as on Sapelo Island.

    Mesoamerica

    c. 3500–1200 BCE

    Maize became a staple slowly. In rock shelters in Belize's Maya Mountains, bone chemistry shows no clear maize before c. 2700 BCE, about 30 per cent of some people's diet over the next seven centuries, and more than 70 per cent after c. 2000 BCE (Kennett et al. 2020). On the Pacific coast of Chiapas the Mokaya began making fine pottery in the Barra phase (c. 1900–1700 BCE) and built the ballcourt at Paso de la Amada. On the Gulf coast, rubber balls, wooden busts and the bones of infants were offered in the El Manatí bog from as early as c. 1700–1600 BCE, as San Lorenzo rose to be the first Olmec centre.

    The Caribbean

    c. 3500–1200 BCE

    The Antilles were the only islands in the Americas settled across water from which no land could be seen, and when is argued: a 2019 model of vetted radiocarbon dates puts the first people on Cuba c. 3400–2700 BCE, while a 2023 re-analysis puts people on Puerto Rico by c. 4300–4050 BCE. These Archaic islanders fished, gathered shellfish, worked stone and shell and, at Canímar Abajo in Cuba, ate cultivated plants. Ancient DNA shows them as a population of their own, which barely mixed with the pottery-making farmers who arrived centuries after this era.

    South America

    c. 3500–1200 BCE

    Beyond Caral: on Ecuador's coast Valdivia villagers (c. 3500–1500 BCE) made some of the earliest pottery in the Americas and small clay figurines of women with elaborate hairstyles, while Mayo-Chinchipe people in the Amazonian foothills were already using cacao. In Peru's Casma valley, Sechín Alto grew in the second millennium BCE into the largest building in the Americas of its day. On the Atacama coast, Chinchorro fishers kept up the world's oldest known tradition of deliberate mummification, begun c. 5050 BCE, until c. 1800–1500 BCE (estimates of its end vary).

    Ordinary lives

    Four lives the king lists left out

    Kings wrote the monuments. These four people reach us by other routes: a logbook buried at a Red Sea harbour, clay accounts baked hard when a palace burned, a grave beside the Gulf of Thailand, and the DNA in an inner-ear bone.

    Tura quarries to Giza, Egypt · c. 2560 BCE

    Merer, an inspector of boatmen

    Late in Khufu's reign, in his 26th or 27th year, Merer led a crew of about forty men hauling white limestone from the Tura quarries across the Nile and along canals to Akhet-Khufu, "Horizon of Khufu", the Great Pyramid. His logbook runs day by day from July to November: hauling stone at Tura, casting off loaded, a night at She-Khufu, back upriver for more. Every ten days the crew made two or three round trips, perhaps 200 blocks of 2–3 tonnes a month, probably for the pyramid's outer casing. A team went to Heliopolis to bring back food, and the harbour at Giza was overseen by the king's half-brother Ankhhaf. These were rotating, rationed crews, not slaves; what the papyri show is a state's reach in the logistics of a single boat.

    How we know The Wadi al-Jarf papyri, found in 2013 by Pierre Tallet and Gregory Marouard's team, buried at a harbour on the Red Sea: the oldest inscribed papyri known, published by Tallet in 2017. What the blocks were for is his inference; the diary does not say.

    Pylos, south-west Greece · c. 1200 BCE

    Thirty-seven bath-attendants and their children

    When the palace of Pylos burned, the fire baked its clay accounts hard. One tablet counts, "at Pylos", thirty-seven women bath-attendants with thirteen girls and fifteen boys, and issues them wheat and figs; another lists twenty-two of their grown sons. Other groups of women are named by their work, corn-grinders, spinners, carders, flax-workers, or by where they came from: Miletus, Knidos, Lemnos, "Asia". One group is simply called "captives". Men appear mostly as the women's sons, listed under their mothers. John Chadwick first read the foreign place-names as targets of Pylian slave raids, then, since Miletus was probably Greek already, as the markets where the women had been bought.

    How we know Linear B tablets, among them PY Ab 27 and Ad 676, in Ventris & Chadwick, Documents in Mycenaean Greek (2nd ed. 1973). The words are read as Greek; that the women were unfree is inferred from their menial work, their rations and the word for "captives".

    Khok Phanom Di, Gulf of Thailand · c. 2000–1500 BCE

    A master potter

    In the fifth of the cemetery's seven burial phases, a woman was laid in a grave larger and deeper than any other, covered in red ochre and dressed in garments sewn with some 120,000 shell-disc beads, with a shell bangle, earrings of a new style and eight finely decorated pots. By her ankle, in a small shell container, lay a clay anvil and two worn burnishing stones, a potter's tools, and her strongly developed wrist bones fit a working life at the craft. Two metres away an infant of about 15 months was buried in the same way, with a tiny anvil and burnishing stone of its own. In a community of fishers whose shoreline kept moving, making pots had made some women rich.

    How we know Charles Higham's excavation of 1984–85, which found 154 burials in seven phases. That she was a potter is read from her tools and her bones; there is no text.

    Rakhigarhi, Haryana, India · c. 2800–2300 BCE

    A woman of the Indus world

    She was buried about a kilometre west of the town, laid north to south in the Harappan way with Indus-style pots beside her. Her bones held too little collagen for radiocarbon, so her date comes from the town's own. Of 61 skeletons sampled in the cemetery, hers alone yielded usable DNA, from the dense bone of the inner ear, and only after 108 separate preparations. It showed ancestry related mostly to ancient Iranians and partly to hunter-gatherers of southern Asia, with no sign of the steppe ancestry widespread in the region today. It also matched eleven people buried far away, at Gonur in Turkmenistan and Shahr-i Sokhta in Iran: probably migrants from the Indus, living in the towns of the Bronze Age trade roads.

    How we know Shinde et al., Cell (2019): a low-coverage genome, filtered for contamination. The Iranian-related part split off before farming began, so Indus farmers were not simply migrants from Iran; one person cannot stand for a whole civilization.

    Five voices, from a weaver's retort to a king's despair

    Only people who wrote, or whose words were learned by heart, can speak here: the Americas, Australia, most of Africa and Europe north of the Aegean left no words from these centuries that anyone can read.

    How we know

    • Decipherment. Cuneiform fell to a chain of scholars — Georg Grotefend, who in 1802 cracked the first Old Persian signs from Carsten Niebuhr's copies of the Persepolis inscriptions; then Edward Hincks and Henry Rawlinson, who himself copied and worked the trilingual Behistun cliff; Egyptian hieroglyphs fell to Champollion in 1822 with the Rosetta Stone; Linear B to Michael Ventris in 1952, who proved it was Greek.
    • Tree rings. The Aegean Dendrochronology Project, begun by Peter Kuniholm, built a floating Anatolian oak and juniper sequence from Gordion and Hittite sites that ties Bronze Age timbers to single years.
    • Radiocarbon against the king lists. The Thera eruption is the sharpest test we have: radiocarbon on an olive branch buried by the ash pointed to c. 1620–1600 BCE, while Egyptian-linked pottery chronologies point to c. 1530–1500. An annual tree-ring radiocarbon record (2018) shifts the calibrated range toward the 16th century BCE and narrows the gap, but decades of work have not closed it, and it is the honest example of science and text disagreeing — see dating methods.
    • Ancient DNA. Genomes from steppe and central European skeletons date the Yamnaya ancestry turnover after c. 2900 BCE; the same discipline recovered Yersinia pestis from c. 3300 BCE and the first ancient human genome from a Saqqaq man in Greenland.
    • Isotopes in metal and bone. Lead and tin isotopes fingerprint the ore behind an ingot; strontium and oxygen in tooth enamel say where a person grew up — which is how the Hyksos were re-read as long-resident immigrants rather than invaders. Oxygen and strontium in baboon mummies point to where Punt lay, and strontium in teeth shows that Cuba's first islanders at Canímar Abajo were settled; isotope maps give regions, not single places.
    • Chronometric hygiene. Before modelling, dates are vetted: the 2019 Caribbean study sorted 2,484 radiocarbon dates by reliability and built Bayesian models from the sound ones only; the same approach sets Ban Non Wat's bronze at c. 1050 BCE and drives the argument over when the Marianas were reached. A model is only as good as the dates it is allowed to use.
    • Residues in pots. Cacao at Santa Ana-La Florida rests on three independent traces — starch grains, theobromine and ancient DNA — which is why it is believed; any one alone would be weak. Residues show what touched a pot, not how much of the diet it was.
    • Bones and pathogens. Palaeopathology found leprosy at Balathal; pathogen DNA found plague in Latvia and Scandinavia. Most epidemics kill too fast to mark bone and pathogen DNA survives only in some teeth, so the disease record is a small and biased sample.
    • Climate archives. Stalagmite layers date Liangzhu's rains; Gordion's juniper rings pin a drought to 1198–1196 BCE (±3). A matching date is not a cause: the step from climate to collapse is always an argument.
    • Words and bones as evidence. Bayesian trees of vocabulary trace Pama-Nyungan to the Gulf Plains, though words can spread without their speakers moving; chicken bones are hard to tell from wild junglefowl, which is why claims of much older chickens fell on re-examination.
    • The honest limit. The Indus script and Linear A are undeciphered, and the Indus signs may not encode a language at all; Sanxingdui left no text of any kind. Whole civilizations in this chapter are mute, and the ones we quote are the ones that kept accounts. More on all of it in How We Know, including the register of live debates.

    In the hand

    Objects of the first cities

    Cuneiform tablet

    ~3300 BCE

    An accountant's tally of grain and livestock pressed into wet clay — the humble bureaucratic root from which all Mesopotamian literature grew.

    Oxhide copper ingot

    ~1320 BCE

    A four-handled slab of Cypriot copper from the Uluburun wreck, cast to be lashed to a pack animal — bulk metal as the currency of the Bronze Age world.

    Indus seal

    ~2400 BCE

    A carved steatite seal bearing a standing animal and a line of signs no one can yet read — the voice of a great civilization, still sealed.

    Shang oracle bone

    ~1250 BCE

    An ox shoulder-blade cracked by heat and inscribed with a king's question to the ancestors — the direct forebear of Chinese writing today.

    Knotted cord, Caral

    ~2600 BCE

    Knotted fiber cords from Norte Chico hint that the Americas kept records on string — complex accounting without any script at all.

    Poverty Point object

    ~1700 BCE

    Grooved balls of baked clay, heated and dropped into earth ovens in a swamp that offered almost no stone — mass-produced kitchen technology from a society with no farms and no kings.

    Bactrian composite figurine

    ~2000 BCE

    A seated woman from Gonur Depe, assembled from a carved chlorite robe and a separate limestone head — the signature object of the Oxus civilization, a Bronze Age power most timelines skip.

    Sanxingdui bronze mask

    ~1200 BCE

    A face far larger than any human head, with tubular eyes pushed out from the bronze — cast by a Sichuan culture that left no writing and is named in no Chinese chronicle.

    Cacao-residue pot, Ecuador

    ~3300 BCE

    Mayo-Chinchipe pottery from Santa Ana-La Florida, in Ecuador's Amazonian foothills, whose residues — cacao starch, theobromine and cacao DNA — are the oldest evidence of cacao use yet found.

    Backed point, Australia

    ~2000 BCE

    A thumbnail-sized stone flake blunted along one edge so it could be hafted; use-wear shows such tools worked wood, plants and bone as well as tipping spears.

    Kerma beaker

    ~1700 BCE

    Polished red pottery with a blackened top — the fine ware of Classic Kerma, whose potters carried on a black-topped Nile tradition older than the pharaohs.

    Rubber ball, El Manatí

    ~1600 BCE

    One of the balls laid in a sacred bog near San Lorenzo, made from the latex of the Castilla rubber tree — the oldest equipment of the Mesoamerican ballgame.
