    Chapter III  ·  12,000 – 3500 BCE

    The Neolithic
    Revolution

    ≈ 14,000 – 5,500 years ago  ·  about 8,500 years of change

    Farming was invented not once but at least a dozen times by peoples who never met — a bargain that bought more food and more people at the price of labour, crowd disease, and the hardening of inequality.

    Scroll to begin, as the Ice Age ends

    The chapter before us

    The previous chapter ended with a species that had already won: Homo sapiens occupied every continent except Antarctica, painted Chauvet and Sulawesi, sewed tailored clothing, buried its dead with grave goods, and had domesticated exactly one animal — the dog. What it did not have was a way to make more food appear. Population was capped by what a landscape gave freely, and for two hundred thousand years that cap held. What carries forward into this chapter is the toolkit: composite tools, boats, long-distance exchange, symbolic thought, and — decisively — the habit of altering a landscape on purpose, through fire, weirs, and replanting. The Neolithic is what happens when that habit meets a climate that finally sits still.

    Between the end of the last Ice Age and the eve of the first cities, humanity crossed arguably the most consequential threshold in its history: it began to produce food rather than only gather it. What we call the Neolithic Revolution was slow, uneven, and — most surprisingly — repeated all over the world.

    Farming and herding arose independently in at least a dozen regions across Asia, Africa, the Americas, and Oceania — peoples who never met, converging on the same idea within a few thousand years of one another. Cultivation anchored people to place and generated surplus, storable wealth, property, and crowds; with those came the first towns and monuments, durable social hierarchy, and epidemic disease jumping from newly domesticated animals into densely packed human bodies.

    The Neolithic was less a single invention than a slow, worldwide renegotiation of the human relationship with other species — one whose bargains still shape our diets, bodies, and societies. It is a global story that refuses a single hearth: the Fertile Crescent is one cradle among many, no more the source of all farming than the Yangtze, the Balsas Valley, or the highlands of New Guinea.

    01 · Southwest Asia

    One cradle among many

    The Fertile Crescent is the most famous starting point but not the only one. Here foragers domesticated emmer and einkorn wheat, barley, and pulses, then goats and sheep — a package that would later flood Eurasia. It is a beginning, not the beginning.

    02 · Asia

    Two rivers, then a third

    Rice was tamed in the wet Yangtze basin while millet was bred in the drier Yellow River loess to the north — two separate revolutions in one land. Farther west, at Mehrgarh, villagers grew wheat and barley, and by about 6000 BCE were using cotton — the earliest known anywhere.

    03 · Oceania

    An idea in the Pacific

    In the New Guinea highlands, people were working a wetland margin perhaps ten thousand years ago and by seven thousand years ago were mounding plots to grow taro, yam and banana — a wholly independent hearth that shatters any Old-World-only story of how farming began.

    04 · The Americas

    Maize, potato, and pack animals

    From the wild grass teosinte, Mexican farmers slowly bred maize; the Andes gave the potato, quinoa, and the only American herd animals — llama and alpaca. Beans and squash joined maize as the "Three Sisters" that fed later civilizations.

    05 · Africa

    Cattle on a green Sahara

    During the wet African Humid Period the Sahara was grassland, and herders drove cattle across it, raising megaliths at Nabta Playa. As the desert dried, peoples moved south and domesticated sorghum, pearl millet, African rice, and yams.

    06 · Carried outward

    Farming on the move

    These cradles did not stay put. Within a millennium or two of this chapter's close, Bantu-speaking farmers would begin spreading crops — and, later, iron — across sub-Saharan Africa; and Austronesian farmers, who had reached Taiwan from the mainland by ~3000 BCE, would set out for the Philippines around 2200 BCE — more than a millennium after this chapter closes — carrying pigs, dogs, pottery and outrigger canoes across the Pacific.

    Agriculture was invented not once but at least a dozen times — in the hills of Anatolia, the valleys of the Yangtze, the swamps of New Guinea, and the highlands of the Andes — by peoples who never met.

    01 · Surplus

    The first surplus

    Sedentism and domestication produced something foragers rarely had: a store of grain larger than the day's need. Surplus is the hinge of everything that follows — the first time human effort could be banked against the future.

    02 · Towns

    Crowds and monuments

    Surplus fed people who did not farm — builders, priests, potters. Jericho raised a stone wall and a conical tower; Çatalhöyük packed hundreds, perhaps a few thousand, into mudbrick houses entered through their roofs. The town is born.

    03 · Property

    Property and rank

    Grain in a pit can be owned, counted, hoarded, and passed on. Unequal houses, richer graves, and walls between neighbours appear in the record. With storable wealth comes durable inequality — a Neolithic invention as much as the sickle.

    04 · The bill

    The bill comes due

    Crowds living beside animals and their waste would breed the ancestors of measles, smallpox, flu and tuberculosis — measles itself split from cattle rinderpest only around the sixth century BCE, once cities were big enough to keep it alive; skeletons show shorter stature, worn joints, and rotten teeth. The bargain, laid bare: many more people, each often less healthy than the foragers before them.

    01 · Taş Tepeler

    Pillars before fields

    In the limestone hills near Şanlıurfa in southeastern Turkey, people began raising rings of carved T-shaped pillars — the tallest about 5.5 metres — at Göbekli Tepe around 9500 BCE, and at Karahan Tepe, some 37 km to the east, at about the same time. The cereals they ground were still wild in form, and their commonest prey was gazelle. Houses, rock-cut cisterns and heaps of grinding stones found in recent excavations show these were lived-in places, not only shrines — but the monuments came before domestic crops, not after them.

    02 · Jericho & Çatalhöyük

    The farming town, arriving slowly

    Jericho is where the textbook story starts: around 8300 BCE villagers in the Jordan Valley raised a solid stone tower 8.5 metres high beside a settlement wall. Yet the emmer, barley and pulses they grew were still partway through domestication. By Çatalhöyük on the Konya Plain, from about 7100 BCE — more than two thousand years after Göbekli Tepe — the full package is in place: domestic wheat, herded sheep and goats, and houses packed so tightly that the rooftops served as streets. Estimates of its population run from under a thousand to several thousand.

    03 · Green Sahara

    Cattle and standing stones

    About 100 km west of the Nile near Abu Simbel, Nabta Playa was a seasonal lake in what was then a grassy Sahara. Herders buried cattle in stone-covered chambers there around 5500 BCE, set a ring of upright slabs around 4800 BCE and raised lines of megaliths between about 4500 and 3600 BCE — yet the sorghum and millet stored in the pits of an earlier village there were still wild plants. Whether the cattle bones from the first camps, in the 9th–8th millennia BCE, came from herds or from hunted wild aurochs is still disputed, and so are the star alignments some have claimed for the stones.

    04 · Jōmon Japan

    A town without fields

    At the northern tip of Honshu, Sannai-Maruyama was lived in for some 1,700 years, c. 3900–2200 BCE, by Jōmon people who fished, hunted and gathered — rice farming would not reach Japan until more than a thousand years after the town was abandoned. They built pit houses and a great structure on six chestnut posts, each about a metre across; planted and tended chestnut groves; and traded for jade from the Itoigawa area, some 500 km to the south-west, and for obsidian and amber. Estimates of several hundred residents at its peak are debated; its permanence is not.

    05 · Sahul

    Two ways to engineer a wetland

    At Kuk Swamp, 1,550 metres up in the New Guinea highlands, people were using taro and managing plants on the swamp edge by c. 8000 BCE, heaping mounded beds of bananas by c. 5000–4500 BCE and cutting drainage ditches by c. 2400–2000 BCE — farming invented independently. Far to the south, in what is now Victoria, Gunditjmara people cut channels through the Budj Bim lava flow and built weirs and woven traps to hold and harvest short-finned eels; one trap system at Tae Rak (Lake Condah) is dated to about 6,600 years ago, c. 4600 BCE, and older phases have been argued. One wetland grew crops, the other grew fish.

    06 · Lower Mississippi

    Mounds by the Ouachita

    In the Ouachita River floodplain of northeastern Louisiana, people who fished, hunted and gathered began raising Watson Brake around 3500 BCE: eleven earthen mounds, the tallest about 7.6 metres, joined by ridges into an oval some 270 metres across and built up over roughly five centuries. No domestic crops have been found there, and it is generally accepted as the oldest mound complex in North America. Set beside Göbekli Tepe, Nabta Playa, Sannai-Maruyama and Budj Bim, the pattern is plain: farming built some towns, but monuments, permanent settlements and engineered food supplies were achieved again and again without fields of domestic grain.

    Milestones

    Forty-one turning points

    ~12,500–9700 BCE

    Natufian sedentism

    Levant (Eastern Mediterranean)

    Hunter-gatherers built stone dwellings, ground and stored wild cereals, and lived year-round in one place — proving that settling down preceded agriculture rather than resulting from it, and setting the stage for domestication.

    ~10,000 BCE

    Hilazon Tachtit — a woman buried as a shaman

    Galilee, Israel (Late Natufian)

    A small, elderly, disabled woman was laid in a specially built grave in a cave with 50 complete tortoise shells, parts of a wild boar, an eagle, a cow, a leopard and two martens, and a complete human foot — read by her excavators (Grosman, Munro & Belfer-Cohen 2008) as one of the earliest known shamans. Butchered aurochs and tortoises in the same cave point to funeral feasts. A ritual specialist, and a woman, stands at the centre of Natufian religion well before anyone in the Levant grew a domesticated crop.

    ~9500 BCE

    Göbekli Tepe

    Southeastern Anatolia, Turkey

    Massive carved T-shaped limestone pillars in stone circles, raised by pre-agricultural foragers. It upends the idea that farming surplus was needed before monuments — cooperation and ritual may instead have helped drive the shift to farming.

    ~9500–8500 BCE

    Fertile Crescent founder crops

    Southwest Asia

    The "eight founder crops" — emmer and einkorn wheat, barley, lentil, pea, chickpea, bitter vetch, and flax — were domesticated here, selected for non-shattering seed heads: one of the most influential agricultural packages on Earth.

    ~8500–8000 BCE

    Sheep, goat, pig, cattle

    Fertile Crescent & Anatolia

    Goats and sheep were tamed in the Zagros and eastern Anatolia, cattle from the wild aurochs, and pigs across the region — giving meat, milk, hides, and traction, but also the intimate contact that would breed zoonotic epidemics.

    from ~9600 BCE

    Jericho — walled town & tower

    Jordan Valley, southern Levant

    One of the world's oldest continuously occupied settlements. Its great stone wall and solid conical tower (~8300 BCE) are the earliest known monumental communal architecture in a town — evidence of organized labour and collective identity.

    ~18,000 BCE onward

    Independent origins of pottery

    East Asia, then Southwest Asia

    The earliest ceramics were made by hunter-gatherers in China (Xianrendong) and Japan (Jomon) long before farming, decoupling pottery from agriculture. In the Near East the order reversed: farming came first, with fired pottery arriving only ~7000 BCE.

    ~8000–6000 BCE

    China's two cradles — rice & millet

    East Asia (China)

    Rice was domesticated in the wet Yangtze basin while broomcorn and foxtail millet were domesticated in the drier Yellow River loess to the north — two separate agricultural revolutions underpinning East Asian civilization.

    ~7000–5700 BCE

    Jiahu — flutes, signs & early beer

    Henan, central China

    A rice-and-pig village that also produced the oldest playable instruments on Earth — seven-holed flutes cut from crane wing bones — incised tortoise-shell signs more than five millennia before the oracle-bone script, and jars whose residues Patrick McGovern identified in 2004 as fermented rice, honey and fruit — then the oldest chemically confirmed alcohol anywhere, since challenged by claims of older brews at Natufian Raqefet Cave and at Shangshan on the lower Yangtze. Music, marking and alcohol arrive with the first farming villages, not with cities.

    ~3900–2200 BCE

    Sannai-Maruyama — a town without farms

    Northern Honshu, Japan (Jomon)

    Perhaps 500 people lived here permanently in pit dwellings around six huge chestnut posts, tending a chestnut grove bred for larger nuts and trading jade and obsidian hundreds of kilometres — permanence, monument and storage achieved with almost no agriculture, the strongest single counter-example to the idea that villages require fields.

    ~10,000–2000 BCE

    Hoabinhian foragers & the rice frontier

    Mainland Southeast Asia

    Toolmakers at Spirit Cave in Thailand harvested candlenut, betel and bottle gourd from a managed forest for thousands of years without domesticating a cereal; rice arrived only after ~2500 BCE with Austroasiatic-speaking farmers moving south from the Yangtze, whose ancestry shows up directly in the burials at Man Bac in Vietnam — a case where farming demonstrably spread by people, not by ideas.

    ~12,600–5000 BCE

    Sundaland drowns

    Island Southeast Asia (the Sunda Shelf)

    At the glacial maximum Sumatra, Java and Borneo were uplands of a subcontinent joined to mainland Asia, with the sea some 120 metres lower than today. Sediments cored from the drowned shelf (Hanebuth et al. 2000) record a rise of as much as 16 metres in 300 years around 12,600 BCE, and by about 5000 BCE the islands of today had taken shape. The foragers' Ice Age coastlines now lie under the Java Sea and the Gulf of Thailand, with whatever they built on them — which is why island Southeast Asia's early Holocene record is so thin.

    ~8000 BCE (contested); mounds by ~5000 BCE

    New Guinea highlands — Kuk Swamp

    Oceania (New Guinea highlands)

    Pits and stake-holes on a wetland margin by ~8000 BCE (disputed), then mounded plots by ~5000 BCE and ditch networks by ~2400 BCE show cultivation of taro, yam and banana — an entirely independent centre of agriculture in the tropical Pacific, decisive proof that farming was invented by many peoples, not diffused from one hearth.

    ~11,100–5300 BCE

    The drowning coast, remembered

    Australia (coastal Aboriginal Countries)

    Aboriginal stories from 21 places around Australia's coast tell of dry land where there is now sea. Patrick Nunn and Nicholas Reid (2016) calculated, for each, how far the sea must have been lowered for the story to be true, and matched it against the post-glacial sea-level curve: the scenes described date to between about 11,100 and 5300 BCE. If the method holds — it assumes the stories record things people saw, not later reasoning — these are among the oldest dated memories any people has kept, carried by word of mouth for hundreds of generations.

    ~4600 BCE

    Budj Bim — engineered eel country

    Victoria, southeastern Australia

    Gunditjmara engineers cut channels through a basalt lava flow and built weirs, ponds and woven traps to steer, hold and harvest short-finned eels year-round — one of the oldest aquaculture systems on Earth, older than the pyramids, and a food-production revolution that supported stone-hut villages with no crop and no herd.

    squash ~8000 BCE; maize ~7000 BCE

    Mesoamerica — squash & maize

    Mesoamerica (Mexico)

    Squash was among the earliest domesticates in Oaxaca, and maize was bred from the wild grass teosinte in the Balsas Valley through a remarkable feat of selection. Maize — with beans and squash — became the foundation of every major Mesoamerican civilization.

    ~8000–4000 BCE

    Andes — potato, quinoa & camelids

    South America (Andes)

    High-altitude farmers domesticated the potato and quinoa and — uniquely in the Americas — herd animals, the llama and alpaca, for transport, meat, and wool. This vertically stacked mountain economy later sustained Andean states up to the Inca.

    ~8000 BCE onward

    Manioc & the Llanos de Moxos

    Southwestern Amazonia (Brazil & Bolivia)

    Manioc, peach palm, chili and a squash were domesticated in the Amazon's southwestern rim, and thousands of forest islands raised above the seasonally flooded Moxos savanna — middens deliberately built up into permanent farmland from ~8000 BCE — make the rainforest a genuine independent centre rather than the "counterfeit paradise" it was long written off as.

    ~5050 BCE onward

    Chinchorro — the first mummies

    Atacama coast, northern Chile

    By about 5050 BCE fisher-foragers of the Atacama coast had begun making mummies — taking bodies apart, rebuilding them around sticks and plant fibre, and coating them in a clay-and-ash paste painted black — the oldest known artificial mummification anywhere, beginning with a child from the Camarones valley. They had no crops, no herds and no metal; the practice lasted some 3,000 years, and their settlements and cemeteries became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2021.

    ~4000 BCE

    Huaca Prieta — the oldest known indigo

    Chicama valley, north coast of Peru

    Cotton fabrics about 6,000 years old from this coastal mound carry indigotin, the blue of indigo — the earliest known use of the dye anywhere, some 1,500 years before its first known use in the Old World (Splitstoser et al. 2016), and probably from a native Indigofera. The people who wove them were fishers who also grew cotton: a maritime road to farming, run as much from the shore as from the field.

    Trinidad ~6000 BCE; the islands ~4000 BCE

    The first Caribbean peoples

    Trinidad, then the Greater and Lesser Antilles

    Banwari Trace on Trinidad, a shell midden left by fisher-foragers around 6000 BCE when the island was still joined, or nearly joined, to South America, is the oldest known site in the Caribbean and holds its oldest skeleton. Stone-tool-using foragers first crossed open water to the islands about 6,000 years ago, c. 4000 BCE (the date Fernandes et al. 2021 give), and the oldest sites on the separate islands spread over the following millennia. Ancient genomes (Nägele et al. 2020; Fernandes et al. 2021) show these first islanders were a population distinct from the Arawak-related potters who arrived from about 500 BCE and largely replaced them.

    ~5000–1800 BCE

    The Eastern Agricultural Complex

    Midwest & mid-South, North America

    Squash was growing in the Illinois valley by ~5000 BCE, probably still wild; squash with larger, domestic-type seeds appears at Phillips Spring, Missouri, by ~3000 BCE, and sunflower, marshelder and goosefoot were all domesticated by ~1800 BCE, when they turn up together at Riverton, Illinois — a home-grown crop package identified largely through Bruce D. Smith's work on seed size and seed-coat thickness, and North America's own place on the list of regions that invented agriculture from scratch, some three thousand years before maize arrived from Mexico.

    ~6500–1600 BCE

    The Old Copper Complex

    Great Lakes, North America

    Around Lake Superior, foragers quarried native copper on Isle Royale and the Keweenaw and cold-hammered and annealed it into socketed points, awls, fish hooks and knives — one of the oldest sustained metalworking traditions anywhere, produced without farms, towns or smelting, which breaks the tidy assumption that metal has to follow agriculture.

    ~3500 BCE

    Watson Brake

    Louisiana, North America

    Eleven earthen mounds joined by a ridge, raised over some five centuries by hunter-fisher-gatherers of the lower Mississippi — the American counterpart to Göbekli Tepe's argument, older than Stonehenge or any pyramid, and further proof that monumental building can precede farming instead of depending on its surplus.

    ~7100–5700 BCE

    Çatalhöyük — a dense proto-city

    Central Anatolia, Turkey

    Hundreds, perhaps a few thousand, people living in mudbrick houses packed wall-to-wall with no streets, entered through the roofs and decorated with vivid murals and installed animal remains — early urban-scale life, household ritual, and a strikingly non-hierarchical social order. Genomes from 131 burials (Yüncü et al. 2025) add a twist: the relatives buried together in a house were linked mainly through their mothers, and girls who died young were given grave goods more often than boys.

    ~6900–6300 BCE

    Atlit-Yam — a drowned village and a disputed disease

    Carmel coast, Israel (now 8–12 m under the sea)

    A farming and fishing village with a stone-lined well and a semicircle of standing stones, drowned by the rising Mediterranean and excavated by divers. A woman and an infant buried there carry bone lesions, and traces reported as Mycobacterium tuberculosis DNA and cell-wall lipids (Hershkovitz et al. 2008) — long cited as the oldest case of TB, though lesions and lipid traces from Dja'de el-Mughara in Syria, c. 8800–8300 BCE, are an older claim still (Baker et al. 2015). Both are contested: genome-based molecular clocks put the common ancestor of the TB bacteria less than 6,000 years ago (Bos et al. 2014), and the old finds and the clock cannot both be right.

    ~6500–4000 BCE

    Halaf, Ubaid & canal irrigation

    Mesopotamia & northern Syria

    Halaf potters spread one painted style across the rain-fed north while Ubaid villages pushed south into land too dry to farm without canals — the earliest at Choga Mami around 5500 BCE — and the temples rebuilt on their own foundations and the first two-tier settlement hierarchies here are the direct bridge from Neolithic village to the city of Uruk.

    ~7000 BCE; cotton by ~6000 BCE

    Mehrgarh — farming & first cotton

    South Asia (Balochistan, Pakistan)

    An early farming village growing wheat and barley and herding cattle, goats, and sheep, ancestral to the later Indus Valley civilization. It also yields the earliest known use of cotton, launching a textile tradition central to South Asian life.

    ~7000–5500 BCE

    Mehrgarh's drilled teeth — the first dental drilling

    Balochistan, Pakistan

    Eleven molar crowns from nine adults in Mehrgarh's Neolithic graveyard carry small, neat holes drilled in living people (Coppa et al. 2006) — the earliest known drilling of living teeth anywhere, more than a millennium older than any other drilled tooth. Cruder dentistry is older: Ice Age foragers in Italy had scraped out decay with pointed flint tools some 14,000 years ago (Oxilia et al. 2015). The tool was probably the same flint-tipped drill the villagers used to bore beads; the patients had mouths fed on grain, and prone to decay.

    Jeitun ~6000 BCE; Botai ~3500 BCE

    Jeitun & Botai — the steppe joins in

    Central Asia (Turkmenistan & Kazakhstan)

    Jeitun carried wheat and goats to the edge of the Karakum, and at Botai in northern Kazakhstan bit wear on horse premolars, corral-soil chemistry and mare-milk lipids on potsherds record people managing horses — a first attempt rather than the first success, since ancient DNA shows Botai's animals were Przewalski's horses and not the ancestors of modern breeds.

    ~4900–3500 BCE

    Khvalynsk — herders of the Volga

    Middle Volga, Pontic-Caspian steppe (Russia)

    At the Khvalynsk cemetery, used c. 4500–4300 BCE, some 200 graves yielded 373 copper objects, among them rings and spirals — the largest copper assemblage of the fifth millennium BCE on the steppe (Anthony et al. 2022) — beside offerings of sheep or goats, cattle and horses: among the earliest signs of herding on the steppe, though whether its horses were domestic is unproven. Ancient DNA places Khvalynsk's men within the blend of Eastern hunter-gatherer and Caucasus ancestry that would later dominate the Yamnaya herders, which is why this cemetery sits in the middle of the argument over where the Indo-European languages began.

    ~7000 BCE

    Zhokhov Island — bred sled dogs

    Arctic Ocean, 76°N (Siberia)

    A hunting camp far above the Arctic Circle preserved sled runners and other sled parts, and dogs of two deliberately different builds — husky-sized sled dogs of 16–25 kg and larger, wolf-sized animals probably kept for hunting — the earliest known evidence anywhere of breeding an animal for a specific job, and proof that engineered transport was working the high Arctic while the Fertile Crescent's first farming villages were still young.

    ~9000–3600 BCE

    Green Sahara & Nabta Playa

    Northeast Africa (Sahara / Nubia)

    In the wet African Humid Period the Sahara was grassland, and people were camping by the seasonal lake at Nabta Playa from the ninth millennium BCE. Whether their earliest cattle bones are herds or hunted wild aurochs is contested; most specialists now think domestic cattle of Near Eastern origin arrived in the late seventh millennium BCE. What followed was a pastoral tradition distinct from crop farming — cattle buried under stone c. 5500 BCE, a ring of upright slabs c. 4800 BCE, megalith alignments c. 4500–3600 BCE. As the desert dried, peoples moved south: sorghum was being domesticated in eastern Sudan by ~3500 BCE, and pearl millet in Mali by ~2500 BCE.

    ~9400–4600 BCE

    Ounjougou, Gobero & Takarkori

    Sahara (Mali, Niger & Libya)

    Foragers at Ounjougou in Mali were firing pottery by ~9400 BCE, among the oldest anywhere and made to cook wild grains, not crops; Gobero in Niger holds the largest Green Sahara cemetery, with two populations separated by a thousand-year hiatus (Kiffian fishers until ~6200 BCE, Tenerian herders from ~5200 BCE); and lipid residues from Takarkori in Libya, analysed by Julie Dunne, put dairying in Africa by ~5200 BCE. Genomes from two women buried at Takarkori some 7,000 years ago (Salem et al. 2025) belong to a long-isolated North African lineage — so herding crossed the Sahara mainly as an idea, not with incoming herders. The Sahara's wet millennia, recorded in pots, graves, milk fat and DNA.

    ~6000 BCE

    Shum Laka — West-Central Africa's first ancient genomes

    Cameroon Grassfields, West-Central Africa

    Four children buried in a rock shelter about 8,000 and 3,000 years ago yielded the first ancient DNA from the region (Lipson et al. 2020); one boy carried A00, the deepest-rooted Y-chromosome lineage known, still found almost only here. All four resemble today's Central African hunter-gatherers, not the Bantu-speaking Cameroonians who live there now — though the Grassfields are widely thought to be the Bantu homeland. The ancestors of the Bantu expansion were a different population, one ancient DNA has not yet sampled.

    ~5500–4000 BCE

    Fayum, Merimde & Badari — Egypt's first farmers

    Lower & Middle Egypt (Nile valley and the Fayum)

    Wheat, barley, sheep and goats from Southwest Asia reached the Nile in the sixth millennium BCE; Fayum villagers stored grain in basket-lined pits but folded the new crops into lives still built on fishing and hunting, and Merimde, on the western Delta edge (c. 4800–4300 BCE), grew into a sprawling village of reed huts. By 4400–4000 BCE the Badarians of Middle Egypt were burying some of their dead apart from the rest, with ivory, malachite ground on stone palettes, Red Sea shells and turquoise — the Nile's first visibly richer graves, on the eve of Naqada and the state.

    ~5000 BCE

    The donkey — Africa's beast of burden

    Africa (the exact hearth is not yet located)

    Genomes from 207 modern and 31 ancient donkeys point to a single domestication in Africa around 5000 BCE, from the African wild ass (Todd et al. 2022). It suited a drying Sahara — an animal that carries loads over arid ground on little water — and by the end of the fourth millennium BCE it had reached Southwest Asia, where it would carry the caravans of Chapter IV: Africa's contribution to the world's pack animals.

    ~5000–4200 BCE

    Belovode, Rudna Glava & Varna

    Balkans (Serbia & Bulgaria)

    Copper was smelted from ore at Belovode by ~5000 BCE — the earliest known anywhere, two millennia before the Bronze Age — and mined at Rudna Glava; then the Varna cemetery on the Black Sea buried one man with over a kilogram of worked gold, the oldest processed gold on Earth and the first unmistakable proof in the ground that some people were now worth vastly more than others.

    ~4100–3500 BCE

    Trypillia mega-sites

    Ukraine & Moldova (Cucuteni–Trypillia)

    Taljanky and Maidanetske sprawled over 200 to 320 hectares in concentric rings of houses and may have held anything from a few thousand people, perhaps only seasonally, to well over ten thousand — larger in area than any contemporary town in Mesopotamia — yet were burned and rebuilt every few generations and show no palace and no ruler's grave — only outsized communal buildings, read as temples by their Ukrainian excavators and as assembly houses by others: urban scale reached, and apparently refused.

    ~8000 BCE onward

    The paradox of farming

    Global (farming societies)

    Dense sedentary populations living beside animals and waste bred crowd epidemics (ancestors of measles, smallpox, flu, TB) and parasites. Skeletons show shorter stature, dental decay, anemia, and hard labour — early farmers were often less healthy than foragers, even as their numbers exploded.

    ~4000–3500 BCE

    Secondary Products Revolution

    Southwest Asia & Europe

    Milk had been taken since the seventh millennium BCE, but now animals were exploited alive on a new scale: wool-bearing sheep, the ox-drawn plough, and the wheel and cart. This intensification of surplus, transport, and textiles set the immediate stage for cities, writing, and the Bronze Age.

    Why it happened

    A steady climate, a crowded landscape, and a bargain nobody chose

    Farming has no inventor and no single cause. What it has is a window. Every independent centre of domestication on Earth begins inside the same post-glacial window, and that window is the first stretch of climate since the last interglacial steady enough to make planting in spring a sane bet on autumn.

    The last glacial period was violently unstable: Greenland ice cores record Dansgaard–Oeschger swings of several degrees within a human lifetime. Then, after one final cold snap — the Younger Dryas, roughly 10,900–9700 BCE, which slammed the warming Levant back into drought and appears to have pushed Natufian villagers into managing wild stands harder than before — the Holocene arrives and the thermometer simply stops lurching. Holocene temperature variability runs at roughly a third of the Pleistocene's, and atmospheric CO₂ rises from about 190 to 265 ppm, measurably improving the yield of C3 cereals. Agriculture appears independently in Southwest Asia, China, New Guinea, Mesoamerica, the Andes, Amazonia, the Sahel, and eastern North America — all of them inside this one window, and none of them before it. That is not a coincidence; see the climate spine of the whole tour. The window was not perfectly calm: around 6200 BCE the 8.2-kiloyear event — meltwater from Lake Agassiz flushing into the North Atlantic — cooled the northern hemisphere for some 150 years, and some settlements in the Levant and central Anatolia were disrupted or abandoned in exactly the centuries when farming's spread into Europe accelerated fastest — though a systematic review of the radiocarbon record (Flohr et al. 2016) found no region-wide collapse, and Çatalhöyük answered the cold by changing how it herded and what it ate. A cold snap did not stop the Neolithic; whether it helped scatter it is still argued.

    The seas were moving too. At the glacial maximum the oceans stood about 120 metres lower than today, and the melt drowned the continental shelves in pulses: sediments cored from the Sunda Shelf record a rise of as much as 16 metres in 300 years around 12,600 BCE (Hanebuth et al. 2000). By about 5000 BCE, when the rise all but stopped, Sundaland had become Sumatra, Java and Borneo, Bass Strait had cut Tasmania off from the Australian mainland, and Doggerland lay under the North Sea. Almost every coast people had lived on through the Ice Age was now under water — one reason the early Holocene seaboard is so thin in the record, and why the Aboriginal stories that appear to remember the drowning count as evidence.

    The demographic payoff was enormous and it was paid for in bodies. Perhaps four million people were alive worldwide at 10,000 BCE — the estimates range from one to ten million and none of them is a census. On Colin McEvedy and Richard Jones's widely used figures there were roughly seven million people by 4000 BCE, fourteen million by 3000 BCE and fifty million by 1000 BCE. The HYDE 3.3 reconstruction (2023) puts 4000 BCE nearer thirty million, which shows how wide the uncertainty is. The doubling time collapses from tens of thousands of years to one or two thousand. The mechanism was not longer life but shorter birth spacing: a foraging mother carrying an infant on the move typically bore a child every three to four years, while a settled mother with stored grain to wean onto could bear one every two. Farming did not make individuals healthier; it made them more numerous, which in evolutionary terms is the only vote that counts. Cemeteries record the switch: in burial sequences from several continents, Jean-Pierre Bocquet-Appel found the share of juvenile skeletons jumping abruptly once farming arrives, which he read as roughly two more births per woman — the Neolithic Demographic Transition (2011). Nor was the growth smooth. Summed radiocarbon dates from central and northwest Europe (Shennan et al. 2013) show each region booming after farming arrived and then collapsing, with no clear link to climate; the authors suggest, cautiously, that the causes lay inside the farming societies themselves. The same curve, continued to the present, is the subject of the population series.

    Living inside a herd has a price, and it is the one this tour pays off five thousand years later. Measles shares an ancestor with cattle rinderpest; smallpox with a rodent-borne poxvirus; influenza A cycles through waterfowl and pigs; tuberculosis, brucellosis, anthrax and Q fever all move between livestock and people. Crucially, several of these could not exist at all before the Neolithic: measles burns through a population so fast that it needs a continuously connected pool of a few hundred thousand people to survive, and no forager world ever supplied one. The crowd diseases are not an unlucky side effect of farming — they are a product of it, manufactured by density, sedentism and daily contact with dung, and stockpiled in Eurasia and Africa for the catastrophic encounters of Chapters VI and VII.

    What the bones themselves show is subtler, and much of it is new. Hepatitis B was circulating among European farmers at least 7,000 years ago (Krause-Kyora et al. 2018); Salmonella genomes up to 6,500 years old trace a lineage adapting to people as herding spread (Key et al. 2020); and a 2025 screen of 1,313 ancient Eurasian genomes (Sikora et al.) detects diseases caught from animals only from about 6,500 years ago, peaking around 5,000 years ago. The famous crowd killers came later still: molecular clocks put measles' split from rinderpest around the sixth century BCE, once cities could keep it alive. The Neolithic built the conditions; the epidemics took millennia to arrive.

    The work was reorganised too, and along a fault line that is legible on bone. At Abu Hureyra in Syria, Theya Molleson found in the skeletons of young women a repeated signature: arthritic and deformed big toes, buttressed knees, collapsed last dorsal vertebrae, and grossly enlarged arm muscle attachments — the anatomy of kneeling for hours a day at a saddle quern, pushing a stone back and forth over grain. Stored cereal has to be processed before it is food, the processing is relentless, and the archaeology says who did it. Who lived with whom was not settled once for all, either, and chemistry can now see it. Strontium in the teeth of more than 300 early farmers from central Europe varies less among men than among women — daughters marrying out, sons staying on the land their fathers worked (Bentley et al. 2012). At Çatalhöyük the genomes point the other way: the dead buried together in a house were linked mainly through their mothers, and for a thousand years (Yüncü et al. 2025). There was no single Neolithic family. Meanwhile grain in a pit could be counted, owned, inherited and taken. Direct evidence of enslavement this early is thin, but the ingredients are all present by the chapter's end: storable wealth, defensible ground, and organised labour on a scale one household could not supply. At Varna the result is finally unmistakable, with a single grave — no. 43, a man in his forties — holding some 1.5 kg of gold, a quarter of everything in a cemetery of nearly three hundred graves, and more than was then known from the entire rest of the world for that age.

    Nor did farming invent war. Jebel Sahaba in Nubia (~11,500 BCE) and Nataruk in Kenya (~8000 BCE) both preserve groups of people killed with projectiles and clubs — organised, repeated intergroup violence, in both cases before anyone in Africa sowed a field. What agriculture changed was the stakes: fixed fields and full granaries cannot be walked away from. The Linearbandkeramik farmers who swept up the Danube and across central Europe between 5500 and 4900 BCE left behind the mass graves at Talheim, where thirty-four people were killed with the farmers' own stone adzes, and Asparn-Schletz, where roughly two hundred lay unburied in a ditch. Both are farming villages, and both date to the LBK's final generations around 5000 BCE — a crisis in the long-settled interior, centuries after the frontier had passed, now joined by Herxheim, Halberstadt and Schöneck-Kilianstädten, where the victims' lower legs were systematically broken. Young women are strikingly scarce among the dead at some of these sites, and excavators have suggested they were carried off alive — if so, the earliest trace in Europe of people taken as spoils.

    Farming was also an ecological project, and not only where fields were sown. In Australia, Aboriginal burning — the "fire-stick farming" the archaeologist Rhys Jones named in 1969 — kept country open, patchy and rich in game; Gunditjmara engineers turned a lava flow into eel ponds; Amazonians raised forest islands out of their own refuse; Jōmon villagers tended chestnut groves. Every one of these is a managed landscape, and none needed a domestic cereal. The costs were real too. On Cuba and Hispaniola, ground sloths had outlived their mainland relatives by some six thousand radiocarbon years; they disappear from the record at about the time people reached the islands. David Steadman and colleagues (2005) argue that across the Americas sloth extinctions track human arrival, not climate — and the islands, where people came last, are their sharpest test.

    The honest verdict is that the bargain was bad for most of the people who made it, for a very long time. In J. Lawrence Angel's widely cited eastern Mediterranean skeletal series, male stature falls from about 177 cm in the late Paleolithic to about 161 cm in the late Neolithic (5000–3000 BCE), and no later ancient period in his table fully regains the loss. Ancient DNA complicates that picture without overturning it: in Europe, a 2022 study pairing skeletons with genomes found early farmers some 4 cm shorter than their genes predicted, a gap that narrows once ancestry is modelled more finely (Marciniak et al. 2022). Dental caries jump from a few per cent of teeth among foragers to a tenth or more among cereal eaters; enamel defects, iron-deficiency anaemia and heavy parasite loads follow the plough everywhere it goes. Nobody chose this, because nobody could see it: each step — weed a stand, save the best seed, fence the herd, clear another hectare for the extra mouths that step produced — was locally rational and collectively irreversible. By 3500 BCE the farmers of the Mesopotamian plain — Ubaid, and now Uruk — have been digging canals for some two thousand years, first at Choga Mami on the plain's eastern edge and then across a south that cannot be farmed without them, and the next chapter opens on what happens when a Neolithic village refuses to stop growing: Uruk, the plough, the seal, and writing.

    Competing explanations

    • Oasis / propinquity (V. Gordon Childe, 1920s) — post-glacial drying pushed people, plants and animals together at shrinking water sources. Largely abandoned: the pollen record shows the early Holocene Near East getting wetter, not drier.
    • Hilly flanks (Robert Braidwood) — domestication happened where the wild ancestors actually grew, in the uplands, once people knew them intimately enough. Right about the geography, thin on the motive.
    • Population pressure at the margins (Lewis Binford, Kent Flannery, Mark Cohen) — packed optimal zones pushed surplus people into marginal ones, where cultivation was the only way to eat. Fits the demography; struggles to explain the richest sites starting first.
    • Competitive feasting (Brian Hayden) — surplus was first grown not for calories but for prestige, to fund feasts, brew alcohol and buy influence. Jiahu's fermented jars and Göbekli Tepe's butchered feasting debris are evidence in its favour.
    • The revolution of symbols (Jacques Cauvin) — a change in how people imagined their place among other species came first, and farming followed the idea. Göbekli Tepe is the exhibit.
    • Niche construction — no revolution at all: a continuum from firing grassland and replanting tubers to sowing fields, in which "farmer" and "forager" are labels we impose. Budj Bim, Sannai-Maruyama and the Amazonian forest islands all sit in the gap the other theories leave.

    Live debates

    • When was rice domesticated? Phytoliths from Shangshan and Hehuashan on the lower Yangtze (Zhang et al. 2024) put rice cultivation at about 13,000 years ago and domestication at about 11,000 — as early as the Fertile Crescent. Spikelet bases from Tianluoshan (Fuller et al. 2009) show non-shattering, domestic-type rice still rising from 27% to 39% of the harvest between about 4900 and 4600 BCE. The two studies measure different traits; this page's ~8000 BCE for Yangtze rice sits between them.
    • Did farmers carry their languages with them? The farming/language dispersal hypothesis (Colin Renfrew, Peter Bellwood) links great language families to spreading farmers — Austroasiatic with rice, Bantu with farming in Africa. For Indo-European the fight is open: a 2023 vocabulary study (Heggarty et al.) dates the family's root to about 8,100 years ago and allows a homeland south of the Caucasus, while a 2025 ancient-DNA study (Lazaridis et al.) traces the ancestor of both the Anatolian and the other Indo-European languages to Caucasus–Lower Volga people of about 4400–4000 BCE.
    • Did Africans domesticate their own cattle? Fred Wendorf and Achilles Gautier argued that early cattle bones at Nabta Playa and Bir Kiseiba show local domestication; most zooarchaeologists now read them as hunted aurochs, and genetics derives Africa's taurine cattle mainly from Near Eastern stock. Some local wild input has not been ruled out.
    • How were the biggest settlements run? Çatalhöyük and the Trypillia mega-sites reached town scale with no palace and no ruler's grave. Whether Trypillia's giant buildings were temples or assembly halls, and whether the mega-sites held more than ten thousand people or a few thousand gathering seasonally, decides whether "towns without kings" is a finding or a hope.
    • Did the 8.2-kiloyear cold snap push farmers into Europe? Bernhard Weninger and colleagues (2006) argued that it did; the radiocarbon review by Flohr et al. (2016) found resilience rather than exodus. The timing is close; whether close means cause is the argument.

    Meanwhile, everywhere

    Farmers, fishers, drowned coasts and one empty ocean

    While wheat was being tamed in the Fertile Crescent, other people were fishing lakes in a green Sahara, watching Sundaland drown, running dog sleds at 76°N and raising mounds in Louisiana. Most of the Pacific had never seen a human being. This is what the eight and a half thousand years looked like region by region.

    West Asia

    c. 12,400–3500 BCE

    Natufian foragers at Shubayqa 1 in Jordan baked flatbread from wild einkorn and club-rush tubers c. 12,400 BCE, four thousand years before farming. By about 9000 BCE their successors were cultivating cereals and had shipped them, with cats and dogs, across open sea to Klimonas on Cyprus; by c. 8200 BCE goats were being herded at Ganj Dareh in Iran's Zagros. In a greener Arabia, herders built more than a thousand stone enclosures called mustatils (one, of c. 5300–5000 BCE, held cattle skulls), and by 3500 BCE the canal villages of southern Mesopotamia were growing into Uruk.

    North Africa & the Nile

    c. 9000–3500 BCE

    In the African Humid Period the Sahara held lakes and grassland: fishers and then herders buried their dead at Gobero in Niger, herders at Takarkori in Libya were processing milk by c. 5200 BCE, and cattle people raised the stones of Nabta Playa. Southwest Asian wheat, barley, sheep and goats reached the Fayum in the sixth millennium BCE, and the Badarians (c. 4400–4000 BCE) and Naqada I (from c. 3900 BCE) made the Nile valley's first visibly ranked graves. In Morocco, genomes show farming arriving c. 5400 BCE with migrants from Iberia and being taken up quickly by local foragers of long-isolated ancestry (Simões et al. 2023).

    West & Central Africa

    c. 9500–3500 BCE

    Foragers at Ounjougou in Mali fired pottery to cook wild grasses by c. 9400 BCE, and at Iho Eleru in southwest Nigeria people ate canarium nuts by c. 9300 BCE and probably oil-palm fruit before 8000 BCE: forest tree foods, harvested long before any grain was grown here. An 8.4-metre dugout canoe found in 1987 at Dufuna in northeast Nigeria, dated c. 6500–6000 BCE, is Africa's oldest known boat, probably used for fishing in the Lake Chad basin. At Shum Laka in Cameroon, children buried c. 6000 BCE were Central African foragers, not ancestors of the Bantu speakers thought to have spread from this region later.

    East & Southern Africa

    c. 10,000–3500 BCE

    Lake Turkana was far larger in these wet millennia: fishers camped at Lothagam from about 9000 BCE and speared the lake's fish with barbed bone harpoons, and at Nataruk, c. 8000 BCE, ten of the twelve people found where they fell had died violently. Genomes from Kenya to Malawi show forager groups growing more local as the Ice Age ended (Lipson et al. 2022), yet ostrich-eggshell beads still travelled from the dry interior into the Lesotho highlands, in a gift exchange like the Ju/'hoãn hxaro that was already tens of thousands of years old (Stewart et al. 2020). On Madagascar, cut elephant-bird bones of c. 8500 BCE may mark visiting hunters, but the claim is disputed and no settlement is known for millennia.

    Europe & the Mediterranean

    c. 9500–3500 BCE

    Farmers from Anatolia island-hopped into Greece between 7000 and 6500 BCE, then spread up the Danube and by boat along the Mediterranean with shell-stamped Cardial pottery, from Epirus c. 6400 BCE to Iberia by c. 5500 BCE. Foragers held on beside them: at Lepenski Vir in the Danube's Iron Gates, fishers built houses with trapezoid floors and carved fish-faced boulders, and by c. 6000–5700 BCE incoming farmers, raised elsewhere to judge by their isotopes, were buried among them. The North Sea swallowed Doggerland and cut Britain off by about 6500 BCE, and from c. 4800 BCE people in Brittany raised the cairn of Barnenez, one of Europe's first megaliths.

    Central Asia & the Steppe

    c. 6000–3500 BCE

    Farmers at Jeitun brought wheat and goats to the edge of the Karakum c. 6000 BCE, while Kelteminar fishers and hunters (c. 5500–3500 BCE) worked the Amu Darya and Zeravshan deltas and later added livestock. On the Volga, the people of Khvalynsk (cemetery c. 4500–4300 BCE) buried their dead with copper ornaments and offerings of sheep, cattle and horse; at Botai in northern Kazakhstan, c. 3500 BCE, people were managing horses. Around 3900–3700 BCE farmers and herders founded Sarazm on the Zeravshan, later a centre of metalworking.

    South Asia

    c. 7000–3500 BCE

    At the foot of the Bolan Pass, Mehrgarh's villagers grew wheat and barley from c. 7000 BCE, herded goats, sheep and humped zebu cattle domesticated in the region from the Indian aurochs, had cotton by c. 6000 BCE, and drilled small holes in one another's molars. On the Ganges plain, rice from Lahuradewa dated to the seventh millennium BCE is claimed as India's oldest cultivated rice; the archaeobotanist Dorian Fuller reads it as wild rice gathered by foragers. In central India's Bhimbetka rock shelters, hunters painted animals, bow hunts and dancers in styles attributed to the Mesolithic.

    East Asia

    c. 6000–3500 BCE

    At Kuahuqiao on the lower Yangtze, villagers kept pigs and dogs, grew rice and left an 8,000-year-old dugout canoe before the rising sea seems to have drowned the site c. 5600 BCE; nearby, the Hemudu culture (from c. 5500 BCE) made one of the oldest lacquered bowls known. On the Yellow River loess, millet farmers at Banpo (c. 4700–3600 BCE) ringed 5–6 hectares of round houses with a ditch, and in the northeast Hongshan people (from c. 4700 BCE) carved jade "pig-dragons". Korea's Early Jeulmun people (c. 6000–3500 BCE) fished the open sea from pit-house hamlets, and Japan's Jōmon stayed foragers.

    Southeast Asia

    c. 12,600–3500 BCE

    The melting ice drowned Sundaland in pulses (as much as 16 metres of sea-level rise in 300 years around 12,600 BCE) until, by about 5000 BCE, Sumatra, Java and Borneo were islands. On the mainland, Hoabinhian foragers lived off the forests from Thailand to Vietnam for millennia without a cereal; rice farmers from the north arrived only after c. 2500 BCE. In South Sulawesi, Toalean foragers made small stone points called Maros points and, c. 5250 BCE, buried a young woman at Leang Panninge whose genome revealed a previously unknown human lineage.

    Australia

    c. 10,000–3500 BCE

    The rising sea flooded the Bassian plain between about 10,000 and 6000 BCE and made Tasmania an island, and Aboriginal stories from 21 places around the coast appear to remember land now under water (Nunn & Reid 2016). In Gunditjmara Country in south-western Victoria, people cut channels through the Budj Bim lava flow to trap and hold eels; one system at Tae Rak (Lake Condah) is dated to about 6,600 years ago. Across the continent, deliberate burning (the "fire-stick farming" Rhys Jones named in 1969) kept Country open and rich in game, and the dingo, whose oldest directly dated remains are about 3,300 years old, had not yet arrived.

    Oceania & the Pacific

    c. 8000–3500 BCE

    New Guinea's highlanders were among the world's first farmers, heaping mounded beds for bananas at Kuk Swamp by c. 5000–4500 BCE, and the flooding of the Torres Strait, roughly 6500–4500 BCE, cut their island off from Australia. People had lived in the Bismarck Archipelago and the Solomons since the Ice Age; Kilu Cave on Buka was used again from c. 7000 BCE, and New Britain obsidian was carried between islands. Beyond the Solomons the ocean was empty: no human had reached Vanuatu, Fiji, Micronesia or Polynesia, and the first voyage into Remote Oceania, to the Marianas, came about 1500 BCE on the excavators' dating.

    The Arctic

    c. 10,000–3500 BCE

    As the ice retreated, seal-hunting foragers of the Komsa culture followed the Norwegian coast north into Finnmark, and about 9000 BCE the sea reopened the Bering Strait between Asia and America (Jakobsson et al. 2017). On Zhokhov Island at 76°N, c. 7000 BCE, hunters of polar bear and reindeer drove sleds behind dogs bred for hauling, and at Alta, in what is now Sápmi, people began pecking reindeer, bears and boats into the shore rocks around 4200 BCE. Greenland stayed empty: its first people, the Saqqaq and Independence I, arrived only around 2500 BCE.

    North America

    c. 6500–3500 BCE

    At L'Anse Amour on the Labrador coast, Maritime Archaic people buried a boy face down beneath a mound some 7,500 years ago, among the oldest known burial mounds in the Americas; in Florida, foragers laid their dead in the peat of Windover pond (c. 6000–5000 BCE), wrapped in cloth twined from palm leaves. Around Lake Superior, Old Copper people hammered native copper into points and hooks, and in the Midwest wild squash was growing by c. 5000 BCE, the first step towards North America's own crops. By the chapter's close, Watson Brake's eleven mounds were rising in Louisiana.

    Mesoamerica

    c. 8000–3500 BCE

    Foragers moving between seasonal camps turned wild plants into crops over thousands of years: squash from Guilá Naquitz cave in Oaxaca is domestic by about 8000 BCE, and starch grains and phytoliths from the Xihuatoxtla rock shelter in the Balsas valley show maize by c. 6700 BCE. By c. 5100 BCE people on the beach ridges of the Grijalva delta in Tabasco were clearing forest to grow maize. Yet the oldest cobs yet found, from Guilá Naquitz c. 4300 BCE, are tiny, with two to four rows of grain.

    The Caribbean

    c. 6000–3500 BCE

    For most of this chapter the islands had no people. The exception was Trinidad, still joined or nearly joined to South America, where from about 6000 BCE fisher-foragers at Banwari Trace left shell middens, bone points, fish hooks cut from peccary teeth and grinding stones. On Cuba and Hispaniola, ground sloths outlived their mainland relatives by thousands of years; people first crossed open water to the islands about 6,000 years ago (Fernandes et al. 2021), and the last sloth bones date to roughly 3000–2700 BCE.

    South America

    c. 8000–3500 BCE

    Fishers at Taperinha, a shell mound near Santarém on the lower Amazon, were firing pottery 8,000–7,000 years ago, among the oldest in the Americas. On Ecuador's Santa Elena peninsula the Las Vegas people (c. 8000–4600 BCE) grew early squash, the Chinchorro of the Atacama began mummifying their dead c. 5050 BCE, and in the Andes potato, quinoa, llama and alpaca came under human care. Around 3500 BCE builders in Peru's Casma valley laid out a round plaza of stone and adobe at Sechín Bajo, among the oldest monumental buildings in the Americas and as old as Watson Brake.

    Ordinary lives

    Four lives the ground kept

    Nataruk, west of Lake Turkana, Kenya · c. 8500–7500 BCE

    A young woman, heavily pregnant, her hands perhaps bound

    Nataruk, now dry scrubland some 30 km west of Lake Turkana, then lay at the edge of a lagoon near the shore of a much larger lake, where foragers fished and hunted. The remains of at least 27 people were found there. Twelve still lay as they had died, never buried until shallow water covered them, and ten of those twelve carry wounds from arrows, clubs or blades. Four lay with their hands in positions that suggest they had been tied, among them a young woman in the last stages of pregnancy, found sitting, with the bones of a fetus of six to nine months inside her. If her hands were bound, she died a captive, and she is among the oldest hints anywhere that people were seized, not only killed.

    How we know From trauma and body position in the skeletons, dated by radiocarbon, luminescence and uranium-series methods (Mirazón Lahr et al. 2016, Nature). Christopher Stojanowski and colleagues dispute the reading. They argue that many of the injuries could be damage after death and that the site need not record a single attack.

    Çatalhöyük East Mound, central Anatolia · c. 7100–5950 BCE

    A household that kept its dead beneath the floor

    Home was a mudbrick room entered by ladder through the roof. The oven and hearth usually stood on the south wall, and the plastered walls and raised platforms were kept scrupulously clean, with the rubbish dumped in middens outside. The household's dead went under the platforms, the hearth and the beds, tightly flexed and wrapped in reed mats or baskets. Men and women ate much the same food. Genomes from 131 burials show that the relatives buried in one house were linked mainly through their mothers, and that later the people buried together were often not kin at all. Girls who died young were given grave goods more often than boys. Recent estimates put only 600–800 people on the mound in an average year of its middle phase.

    How we know From excavation of houses and burials under the floors by Ian Hodder's team, carbon and nitrogen isotopes in bone for diet, and 131 ancient genomes for kinship (Yüncü et al. 2025, Science).

    Leang Panninge, South Sulawesi, Indonesia · c. 5300–5200 BCE

    A young woman of the Toalean

    She was 17 or 18 when she died. Her people buried her in the floor of a limestone cave with her knees drawn up and partly covered with large cobbles. Stone tools, among them Maros points, the Toalean's small stone projectile tips, lay in the grave with her, with red ochre. The Toalean foragers are known only from a patch of South Sulawesi about 10,000 km² in size. Her genome is the first ancient one from the islands between Asia and Australia. It belongs to a human lineage that branched off around the time Papuans and Aboriginal Australians split from each other, about 37,000 years ago, and it carries Denisovan ancestry. No one knew that lineage existed until her.

    How we know From excavation of the grave in 2015, a radiocarbon date on a Canarium seed buried with her, age and sex from the skeleton, and DNA from the petrous bone of her inner ear (Carlhoff et al. 2021, Nature).

    Windover pond, near Cape Canaveral, Florida · c. 6000–5000 BCE

    A boy who could not walk, and the people who kept him alive

    Foragers of Florida's Atlantic coast buried their dead in the peat at the bottom of a small pond, always between July and October. The bodies were wrapped in cloth twined from palm leaves and pinned down with stakes, and the remains of at least 168 people were found there. One was a boy of 14–16 born with an open spine (spina bifida aperta), which almost certainly left him paralysed below the waist. An infection in his shin bone had partly healed, a process that may have taken up to two years. His community kept him alive for some fifteen years. Bone chemistry shows that the men and women around him ate much the same food. Arthritis in the women's shoulders and elbows fits long hours spent preparing food and palm fibre.

    How we know From excavation of the drained pond in 1984–86, skeletal pathology (Dickel & Doran 1989) and isotopes in bone. The waterlogged peat also preserved textiles and even brain tissue.

    Seen whole

    Seven ways to see the Neolithic whole

    How we know

    • Radiocarbon, calibrated. Willard Libby's method dates the charcoal, seed and bone of this entire chapter; raw ¹⁴C ages are then converted to calendar years against the IntCal20 curve, built from tree rings and corals. Every date on this page is calibrated — the uncalibrated numbers can be off by some 1,700 years at 10,000 BCE.
    • Archaeobotany. Charred seed assemblages from flotation, and above all the scar left where a seed detached: a shattering wild rachis breaks smoothly, a domesticated one tears. That single fracture surface is how the ~2,000-year slowness of domestication was discovered.
    • Phytoliths and starch grains. Microscopic silica bodies and starch granules survive where seeds do not — they are how banana and taro cultivation at Kuk Swamp and maize at the Balsas were established in wet tropics that destroy ordinary plant remains. They are also at the heart of the rice argument: phytoliths from Shangshan suggest domestic rice by about 11,000 years ago, a reading that specialists who count spikelet bases dispute.
    • Lipid residues on potsherds. Fatty acids absorbed into unglazed pottery, separated by chromatography and read by their carbon isotope ratios, distinguish dairy fat from carcass fat. This is the only reason we can say milk was being processed at Takarkori in Libya by ~5200 BCE and in Anatolia earlier still.
    • Ancient DNA — of people and of crops. Genomes from bone and tooth show that farming entered Europe with migrating Anatolian farmers rather than with borrowed ideas, and crop genomes trace maize back to Balsas teosinte — and, since 2023, to a second wild parent, highland Zea mays mexicana, whose mixing into early maize in the Mexican highlands, some four thousand years after domestication began, is written into every modern variety (Yang et al. 2023).
    • Stable isotopes. Carbon and nitrogen in bone collagen reconstruct diet — including the arrival of maize, a C4 plant, in an otherwise C3 world — while strontium and oxygen in tooth enamel reveal who grew up somewhere else — which is how the daughters-marry-out pattern among central Europe's first farmers was detected, in the teeth of more than 300 of them.
    • Use-wear and skeletal pathology. Sickle gloss on flint, and the deformed toes, knees and vertebrae of the women of Abu Hureyra, are direct physical records of what work was done and by whom; under the microscope, the walls of the holes in Mehrgarh's molars show they were drilled.
    • Radiocarbon in bulk. Bayesian models combine dates with the order of layers to narrow each range, and hundreds of dates summed together serve as a rough population curve — the source of Europe's Neolithic boom-and-bust. The honest limit: a sum also records what archaeologists chose to date and the wiggles of the calibration curve, which is why that study tested its curves against simulations.
    • Pathogen DNA. Screening ancient genomes for microbes now finds hepatitis B, Salmonella and animal-borne diseases directly in farmers' bones. Only infections that reached the blood or bone at death can show up, so a negative is not an absence — and a single old find, like Atlit-Yam's tuberculosis, can clash with molecular clocks built from many genomes.
    • Ancient DNA for kinship. Relatedness among the dead reveals household and marriage rules — maternal lines at Çatalhöyük. DNA survives badly in heat, though: Shum Laka, Takarkori and the Caribbean are hard-won exceptions, and much of Africa's and the tropics' genetic history still rests on a handful of people.
    • Cemetery demography. The share of children and adolescents among the buried is read as a birth-rate signal. Ageing a skeleton is imprecise, and infants are missing from most cemeteries, so the method sees a jump, not a number.
    • Cores and stories. Dated sediments cored from drowned shelves give the pace of sea-level rise, and that curve can date Aboriginal stories of drowned land. The stories yield only a maximum age, and only if they record something seen — see evidence that is not dug up.
    • The standing caution: pots are not people. A pottery style spreading across a map does not prove that a population moved, and for a century archaeology assumed it did. Ancient DNA now settles some of these cases and overturns others — the mechanism of nearly every spread on this page is still actively argued. For the full method, see How We Know.

    In the hand

    Objects from the first fields

    Natufian sickle

    ~11,000 BCE

    Flint blades set in a bone haft, worn glossy by cutting stems — the tool of people reaping wild grain before they ever sowed it.

    Göbekli T-pillar

    ~9500 BCE

    A carved limestone pillar taller than a person, cut with foxes, boars, and cranes — a monument raised by people who reaped and ground wild cereals in bulk but had domesticated none — domestic grain reached this region about a thousand years later.

    Incipient Jomon pot

    ~14,500 BCE

    Among the world's oldest ceramics — plain at first, the cord-marking that names the period came later — made by hunter-gatherers in Japan: proof that pottery was invented for foraging life, long before anyone farmed.

    Early maize cob

    ~4300 BCE · Guilá Naquitz

    A tiny ear, a fraction of a modern cob, coaxed out of the grass teosinte — one of humanity's most extreme feats of selective breeding.

    Polished stone axe

    ~6000 BCE

    Ground and polished rather than merely flaked, hafted for felling trees — the "neo-lithic" (new stone) tool that cleared forests to make room for fields.

    Gunditjmara eel trap

    ~4600 BCE

    A woven funnel set in a stone-walled channel cut through lava rock at Budj Bim, sized to take migrating eels and let the small ones pass — food production without a single seed sown.

    Drilled molar, Mehrgarh

    ~7000–5500 BCE

    A farmer's molar with a small, neat hole bored into the crown while its owner was alive — one of eleven from Mehrgarh's graveyard, and the oldest known dental drilling on Earth.

    Chinchorro black mummy

    ~5000 BCE

    A body taken apart, rebuilt around sticks and plant fibre and sealed in black-painted clay-and-ash paste by fisher-foragers of the Atacama coast — the oldest artificial mummies known, made by people who fished rather than farmed.

    Badarian cosmetic palette

    ~4400–4000 BCE

    A flat stone for grinding green malachite, laid in a Middle Egyptian grave with ivory and Red Sea shell — from cemeteries that set some of the dead apart, and the ancestor of the carved palettes of the first pharaohs.

    Indigo-dyed cotton, Huaca Prieta

    ~4000 BCE

    Cotton fabric from a mound on Peru's north coast, carrying indigotin — the oldest known indigo anywhere, used some 1,500 years before its first known use in the Old World.

    Old Copper socketed point

    ~4000 BCE

    Native copper from Lake Superior, cold-hammered, annealed over a fire and folded into a socket — metal made by hunter-gatherers who never smelted an ore or planted a field.

    Varna gold pendant

    ~4500 BCE

    Hammered sheet gold from a Black Sea cemetery where one grave held a quarter of all the gold found in nearly three hundred — the oldest worked gold on Earth, and the oldest hard proof of an unequal death.
