    Chapter I  ·  ~7 Mya – 300 kya

    Deep Prehistory
    & Human Origins

    7,000,000 – 300,000 years ago

    We stood up long before we grew clever. Bipedalism, fire, and stone tools accrued over millions of years — and for this entire span the story is written in Africa, with the Americas and Australia still empty of any human foot.

    Scroll to begin, 7 million years ago

    The chapter before us

    Nothing came before this chapter. There is no previous era to inherit from — only an African forest, a lineage of apes, and a climate that was beginning to dry. What this chapter hands forward is everything the next eight take for granted: a body that walks, hands that make, a gut small enough and a brain large enough to run on cooked food, and a species — ours — that arrives in the last four percent of the span.

    This is the deepest root of our story: from the first apes that stood upright on the African savanna-woodland to the emergence of Homo sapiens roughly 300,000 years ago. Four through-lines run beneath everything that follows — bipedalism, stone technology, the taming of fire, and the dramatic expansion of the brain — each unfolding over spans of time almost impossible to intuit.

    It is also the story of a once-crowded family bush. Neanderthals, Denisovans, Homo erectus and more shared the planet with our ancestors; we are the sole survivors of a genus that was, for most of its existence, plural. The whole of this chapter is set in the Old World — overwhelmingly in Africa, later reaching Asia and Europe and, at its far eastern frontier, the islands of Southeast Asia.

    Years in this chapter

    6.7M

    From Sahelanthropus (7.2–6.8 Mya by cosmogenic beryllium) to Jebel Irhoud (315,000 ± 34,000 years by thermoluminescence). Our own species has existed for less than 5% as long. Lebatard et al. 2008 · Richter et al. 2017

    Brain at the end

    ~1,400 cc

    The oldest Homo sapiens, Jebel Irhoud 1 and 2, measure about 1,375 and 1,467 ml, inside today's range (mean ~1,330). Sahelanthropus, at the chapter's start, had a chimpanzee-sized braincase of roughly 360–380 cc. The rounded shape of a modern brain came later still. Neubauer, Hublin & Gunz 2018 (CT endocasts)

    Ancestral breeding pool

    ~18,500

    The effective (genetic) size of the population ancestral to us before ~1.2 Mya, read from two whole genomes; the same study rules out anything above 26,000. It is not a head count, which would be larger, but it is fewer than chimpanzees have today (~21,000), for ancestors spread across the Old World. Huff et al. 2010 (PNAS)

    Lineages at the end

    4+

    Around 300,000 years ago: early Homo sapiens in Morocco, Homo naledi in South Africa, the Kabwe people in Zambia and the Neanderthal line in Europe, with erectus in Java and the ancestors of Denisovans in Asia less closely dated. How many "species" that makes depends on where you draw the lines. Richter et al. 2017 · Dirks et al. 2017 · Grün et al. 2020 · Meyer et al. 2016

    01 · Origin

    One birthplace

    Every hominin in this chapter traces back to Africa. Homo erectus — tall, long-legged, built for endurance walking — is the first of our line to carry the story beyond the continent.

    02 · Dmanisi

    The first exit

    At Dmanisi in the Caucasus lie the earliest well-dated hominins outside Africa — small-bodied early Homo, carrying simple Oldowan tools, already in Eurasia by ~1.8 million years ago.

    03 · Asia

    Across a continent

    From there the range sweeps east. "Java Man" at Trinil and Sangiran and "Peking Man" at Zhoukoudian record a vast expansion into East and Southeast Asia — and some of the earliest contested hints of fire.

    04 · Europe

    To the Atlantic edge

    The Sima del Elefante hominins at Atapuerca in Spain — a 2008 jaw (Homo sp.) and the 2025 'Pink' face, ~1.4–1.1 million years old and assigned to Homo aff. erectus — are, with a ~1.4 Mya tooth from Orce, the oldest firmly-dated humans in western Europe. Homo antecessor itself is later, ~850–780 kya at Gran Dolina.

    05 · Still empty

    Two continents wait

    For all seven million years of this chapter, no hominin ever reaches the Americas or Australia. More than a third of the habitable world stays entirely beyond human feet — a blank the next chapters will fill.

    Before we made history, we made footprints — three and a half million years ago, in cooling volcanic ash at Laetoli, our ancestors walked side by side and upright.

    01 · The bush

    Not a ladder

    The famous march-of-progress image — stooping ape straightening into a man — is wrong in every particular. Hominin evolution is a thicket of overlapping, coexisting lineages, most of which simply end. At almost no point in the last four million years was there only one kind of hominin on Earth. The shape of the family bush is the single hardest thing to unlearn.

    02 · Paranthropus

    The other way to be a hominin

    For more than a million years a second kind of upright African ape walked the same landscapes as early Homo. Paranthropus — aethiopicus, then boisei in the east and robustus in the south — answered the drying grasslands with chewing rather than cleverness: molars up to four times the area of ours, flaring cheekbones, and a sagittal crest anchoring jaw muscles across the top of the skull. It was not a failed draft of us. It was a working solution that ran for roughly 1.9 million years and then stopped.

    03 · Taung, 1924

    The cradle nobody wanted

    When Raymond Dart described a small fossil face from a Taung lime quarry in 1925 and called it Australopithecus africanus, British anatomists dismissed it — Piltdown had primed Europe to expect a big-brained European ancestor, and Taung was a small-brained African child. Piltdown was exposed as a forgery in 1953. The dolomite caves of Gauteng have since yielded Sterkfontein's near-complete "Little Foot" skeleton, the Paranthropus robustus of Swartkrans, and Malapa's A. sediba — a density of hominin fossils found almost nowhere else on Earth.

    04 · Homo naledi

    Alive at the same moment as us

    Deep inside the Rising Star cave system, more than 1,500 fossil elements belong to a hominin with curved fingers, modern-looking feet, and a brain of roughly 500cc — a third the size of ours. The shock was the date: Homo naledi lived around 335,000 to 236,000 years ago, overlapping the oldest known Homo sapiens. A small brain was not an early stage to be grown out of. It was a contemporary.

    05 · The plural planet

    We are the anomaly

    Around 1.8 million years ago the Turkana basin alone held Paranthropus boisei, Homo habilis and Homo erectus — at least three species from two genera of upright apes, some of them certainly toolmakers, sharing one lake shore. At 300,000 years ago the planet still carried erectus in Java, naledi in South Africa, the Sima lineage in Spain, the ancestors of Denisovans in Asia, and the first Homo sapiens in Africa. A world with exactly one human species is roughly 40,000 years old. It is the strangest thing about the present.

    01 · Bipedalism

    Standing up

    The Laetoli footprints (~3.66 Mya) and Lucy (~3.2 Mya) prove upright walking preceded the big brain by millions of years. Freed hands could carry, throw, and — eventually — make.

    02 · Stone technology

    The first tools

    By ~2.6 Mya — and possibly ~2.9 Mya at Nyayanga, Kenya — hominins were striking sharp flakes from cores to butcher meat. Who did it is open: early Homo was around, but the Nyayanga tools lie beside Paranthropus teeth, and Homo habilis itself is not known before ~2.3 Mya. Lomekwi pushes toolmaking back to ~3.3 Mya — before our own genus even existed.

    03 · Fire

    Taming fire

    Fire was in hominin hands by ~1 Mya (Wonderwerk Cave), kept in hearths by ~790 kya (Gesher Benot Ya'aqov), and routine across sites only after ~400 kya — but wherever it began, cooking, warmth and safety after dark followed. Cooked food is easier to digest — a smaller gut, and energy to spare for a hungrier brain.

    04 · Brain expansion

    Our own arrival

    Endocranial volume roughly quadrupled. By ~300,000 years ago the oldest Homo sapiens — at Jebel Irhoud in Morocco — close this chapter at the threshold of our story.

    What that curve leaves out

    A single rising line is a convenience, and a misleading one. Cranial capacity is an average across a bush of lineages, several of which flatly contradict the trend:

    • Paranthropus boisei — ~500–545cc at ~1.8 Mya, essentially australopith-sized, and it kept that brain for over a million years while Homo erectus beside it grew a larger one.
    • Homo naledi — ~465–610cc at ~335–236 kya. On the curve it belongs two million years earlier than it actually lived.
    • Homo floresiensis and the Mata Menge hominins — insular dwarfing shrank both body and brain (~426cc) on Flores. Brains can get smaller.
    • Neanderthals — ~1,410–1,500cc, on average larger than the modern human mean of ~1,350cc. Size is not a scoreboard, and the last 30,000 years have seen the sapiens average drop slightly.

    Read the line as the trend of one surviving branch, not the destiny of a genus. See the family bush.

    01 · Preservation

    A map of where bone survives

    Almost every hominin older than two million years comes from one of two kinds of place. Along the East African Rift the crust is pulling apart, so sinking basins fill with lake sediment, volcanoes seal it under ash that argon-argon dating can read, and faulting later cuts the layers open again; in the dolomite caves of Gauteng, the "Cradle of Humankind", bones fell or were dragged into sinkholes and set hard in breccia. The acidic soils of the West and Central African forest belt, by contrast, dissolve bone within centuries, which is why Toros-Menalla in Chad's Djurab Desert matters so much: Sahelanthropus turned up there in 2001, some 2,500 km west of the Rift and dated to c. 7 Mya by cosmogenic beryllium, and it shows that this is a map of preservation, not of where hominins lived.

    02 · The early Rift

    Five sites, three million years

    Between about 6 and 3 million years ago the record sits in a handful of Rift basins. In Ethiopia's Afar, the Middle Awash has yielded Ardipithecus kadabba (c. 5.8–5.2 Mya) and "Ardi" (c. 4.4 Mya), and Hadar, to the north, gave up Lucy (c. 3.2 Mya) in 1974. Beside Lake Turkana, Lomekwi on the west side has Kenyanthropus (c. 3.5 Mya) and the oldest known stone tools (c. 3.3 Mya, contested), while on the east shore the KBS Tuff at Koobi Fora was dated to 2.6 Mya in 1970 and settled at c. 1.9 Mya only after fifteen years of argument, closed by independent potassium-argon and fission-track dates from several labs — an episode that taught the field to demand agreement between independent methods. Far to the south, the Laetoli footprints were pressed into damp volcanic ash c. 3.66 Mya.

    03 · Homo appears

    Our genus, from the Afar to Gauteng

    The oldest fossil assigned to Homo, the LD 350-1 jaw from Ledi-Geraru in the Afar, lies between volcanic tuffs dated 2.84 and 2.67 Mya, which pins it to c. 2.8–2.75 Mya. The Turkana basin has Homo erectus by c. 1.9 Mya, and Olduvai Gorge, where the Leakeys' team found "Nutcracker Man" in 1959 and the type fossil of Homo habilis in 1960, has early Homo at c. 1.8 Mya. The Gauteng caves have no ash to date, so Sterkfontein, Swartkrans (where Paranthropus robustus and early Homo lie together, c. 2–1 Mya), Drimolen (the oldest known Homo erectus skull, c. 2.04–1.95 Mya) and Rising Star (Homo naledi, c. 335–236 kya) are dated by cosmogenic burial, uranium-lead and uranium-series, electron spin resonance and palaeomagnetism, and their ages are far more contested than the Rift's.

    04 · First exits

    Out of Africa, one lucky site at a time

    Beyond Africa the record thins to a scatter of places where something happened to survive: Dmanisi in Georgia (skulls and Oldowan tools, c. 1.85–1.77 Mya, argon-dated on the basalt beneath them); Ubeidiya in the Jordan Valley (early Acheulean tools usually put at c. 1.5 Mya, though a recent cosmogenic burial study puts the sediments at c. 2.1–1.9 Mya); Shangchen on China's Loess Plateau (stone tools but no bones, c. 2.1 Mya, dated only by palaeomagnetism); and Attirampakkam in Tamil Nadu (handaxes, c. 1.5 Mya, by cosmogenic burial dating). On Java, Homo erectus appears at Sangiran by c. 1.5–1.3 Mya (the exact date is debated), and at nearby Trinil Eugène Dubois found "Java Man" in 1891. The map shows today's coast, but whenever glacial cycles lowered the sea, the shallow Sunda Shelf ran dry and joined Java to mainland Asia, which is how erectus could walk there.

    05 · Across the water

    Islands nobody could walk to

    Flores and Luzon stayed islands even at the lowest sea levels, cut off by deep water, yet hominins reached both. On Flores, stone tools at Wolo Sege date to c. 1 Mya, and Mata Menge has small jaws and teeth of c. 700 kya that most researchers read as ancestral to the tiny Homo floresiensis of Liang Bua cave, whose bones date to c. 100–60 kya. On Luzon, a rhinoceros butchered with stone tools at Kalinga, in the Cagayan Valley, dates to c. 709 kya; the Homo luzonensis fossils from nearby Callao Cave are far later, at least 50,000 years old.

    06 · The far edges

    A Spanish hillside, and two skulls to end on

    In northern Spain, the Atapuerca caves hold much of the early European record: a partial face from Sima del Elefante dated c. 1.4–1.1 Mya and published in 2025 as western Europe's oldest, Homo antecessor at Gran Dolina (c. 850 kya), and the bone pit of Sima de los Huesos (c. 430 kya). The chapter closes with two skulls of almost the same age from opposite ends of Africa: Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, the oldest known Homo sapiens at c. 315 kya (thermoluminescence on burnt flint), and Kabwe in Zambia, found by a miner in a lead and zinc mine in 1921 and dated in 2020 to c. 299 kya by electron spin resonance and uranium-series. It is a heavy-browed human of quite another kind, alive at the same moment as ours.

    Milestones

    Forty-six turning points

    ~7 Mya

    Sahelanthropus tchadensis

    Djurab Desert, Chad

    The oldest fossil generally placed on the human side of the split from chimpanzees; a forward-set foramen magnum hints at upright posture near the divergence itself.

    ~6 Mya

    Orrorin tugenensis

    Tugen Hills, Kenya

    Femur necks thickened exactly where habitual upright walking loads them — independent evidence that bipedalism is as old as the human–chimp split itself, not a later invention.

    ~5.8–5.2 Mya

    Ardipithecus kadabba

    Middle Awash, Ethiopia

    Yohannes Haile-Selassie's teeth and toe bone fill the emptiest window in the record, the six-to-five-million-year gap where the lineage should be and almost nothing survives.

    ~4.4 Mya

    Ardipithecus "Ardi"

    Afar, Ethiopia

    A near-complete skeleton mixing a grasping toe with an upright-capable pelvis — showing bipedalism arose in woodland, not open savanna.

    ~4.2–3.8 Mya

    A. anamensis & the MRD cranium

    Woranso-Mille & Turkana

    The 2019 MRD skull gave anamensis a face at 3.8 Mya — and overlapped it with afarensis, breaking the neat ancestor-becomes-descendant chain into branching.

    ~3.67 Mya

    "Little Foot" StW 573

    Sterkfontein, South Africa

    Ronald Clarke spent two decades chiselling a near-complete australopith from concrete-hard breccia; cosmogenic burial dating made it a southern African contemporary of Lucy's kin.

    ~3.66 Mya

    Laetoli footprints

    Laetoli, Tanzania

    Footprints in volcanic ash record individuals walking fully upright — direct behavioural proof of habitual bipedalism.

    ~3.5 Mya

    Kenyanthropus platyops

    Lomekwi, West Turkana, Kenya

    Meave Leakey's flat-faced cranium sits beside afarensis in time and place — the first hard sign that mid-Pliocene East Africa held more than one hominin at a time.

    ~3.5 Mya

    A. bahrelghazali — "Abel"

    Koro Toro, Chad

    A second hominin 2,500 km west of the Rift, in the same basin as Sahelanthropus: the eastern concentration of fossils is a map of where bone survives, not of where hominins lived.

    ~3.3 Mya

    Lomekwi stone tools

    West Turkana, Kenya

    Deliberately flaked stones half a million years before the genus Homo — technology began with australopith-grade makers.

    ~3.3 Mya

    The Dikika child, "Selam"

    Dikika, Afar, Ethiopia

    An A. afarensis girl of about two years and four months, found by Zeresenay Alemseged's team in 2000 and published in 2006 — skull, torso, hyoid and foot, sexed from the small canines still forming in her jaw. Her legs were built for walking upright; her shoulder blades and arms were still built for climbing. She is most of what we know about growing up 3.3 million years ago.

    ~3.2 Mya

    Lucy (A. afarensis)

    Hadar, Ethiopia

    A famously complete skeleton confirming upright walking long preceded brain expansion; the public face of human evolution.

    ~2.9–1.0 Mya

    The Paranthropus radiation

    East & southern Africa

    The Black Skull, Mary Leakey's 1959 OH 5 "Nutcracker Man" at Olduvai, and robustus at Swartkrans: a second genus of upright hominins that answered aridity with enormous molars and outlasted H. habilis — now reaching back to Nyayanga's giant molars (~2.9 Mya) and north to a ~2.6 Mya jaw in the Afar (2026).

    ~2.9–2.6 Mya

    Oldowan industry

    Nyayanga, Kenya; Gona & Ledi-Geraru, Ethiopia

    The first systematic tool tradition — cores, sharp flakes and hippo butchery — appears before any Homo habilis fossil, beside the teeth of Paranthropus. Whose hands made it is genuinely unknown.

    ~2.8 Mya

    LD 350-1, Ledi-Geraru

    Afar, Ethiopia

    A single lower jaw, currently the oldest fossil assigned to Homo — and it appears at the same moment the East African record shifts toward drier, grassier faunas.

    ~2.8 Mya · found 1924

    The Taung Child

    Taung, North West, South Africa

    Raymond Dart read an ape-sized brain on a hominin body and named Australopithecus africanus; Europe, primed by the Piltdown forgery to expect a big-brained ancestor of its own, ignored him for a generation.

    ~2.4 & ~1.9 Mya

    Ain Boucherit

    Northeastern Algeria

    Oldowan tools and animal bones cut by stone edges in two layers, ~2.4 and ~1.9 Mya (Sahnouni et al., 2018): hominins on Africa's Mediterranean rim within a few hundred thousand years of the oldest Rift toolmakers. Either the technology spread across the continent fast, or it was invented more than once.

    ~2.12 Mya

    Shangchen stone tools

    Loess Plateau, Shaanxi, China

    Palaeomagnetically dated artefacts in loess, older than any hominin fossil outside Africa — if they hold, the "first exit" happened at least 300,000 years before Dmanisi and we have not found the people who made it.

    ~2.0 Mya

    Homo erectus

    Drimolen, South Africa & Melka Kunture, Ethiopia

    A toddler's skullcap at Drimolen (DNH 134, ~2.04 Mya) and an infant's jaw at Melka Kunture (~2.0 Mya) are the oldest fossils assigned to erectus: tall, long-legged, built for endurance travel, and the first hominin to spread far beyond Africa.

    ~1.98 Mya

    Australopithecus sediba

    Malapa, South Africa

    Found in 2008 by Lee Berger's nine-year-old son Matthew: a mosaic of australopith arms and Homo-like pelvis and hand that refuses to sit cleanly on either side of the genus boundary.

    ~1.8 Mya

    Dmanisi — first exit

    Dmanisi, Georgia

    The earliest well-dated hominins outside Africa, dispersing into Eurasia with simple Oldowan tools.

    ~1.8–1.6 Mya

    The oldest known cancer

    Swartkrans, Gauteng, South Africa

    Micro-CT scans of SK 7923, a hominin foot bone of unknown species, show an osteosarcoma — a malignant bone tumour (Odes et al., 2016). It is the earliest identified cancer in the human lineage, and a caution against treating cancer as purely a disease of modern life.

    ~1.77 Mya

    Dmanisi Skull 5

    Kvemo Kartli, Georgia

    Five skulls from one place and moment vary as much as habilis, rudolfensis and erectus do as named species — the sharpest argument that palaeoanthropology has been splitting single variable populations into separate species.

    ~1.76 Mya

    Acheulean handaxe

    Kokiselei, Kenya

    Symmetrical bifaces reflect a shared mental template and planning — the longest-lived tool tradition in human history.

    ~1.66–1.3 Mya

    Reaching East Asia

    Nihewan & Lantian, China; Sangiran, Java

    Tools at Majuangou (1.66 Mya), the Gongwangling cranium (~1.63 Mya) and Javanese erectus (~1.5–1.3 Mya) document a vast eastward expansion — and if a new 2026 burial date holds, the Yunxian skulls of Hubei (~1.77 Mya) are older still. 'Peking Man' at Zhoukoudian (~780 kya) is a late chapter of it, with contested early evidence of fire.

    ~1.6 Mya

    Turkana Boy, KNM-WT 15000

    Nariokotome, Kenya

    The most complete early Homo skeleton known: long-legged, heat-shedding — a body built for walking all day. The 'narrow hips' once read from him are disputed: the best female pelvis attributed to erectus, from Gona (~1.2 Mya), has a far roomier birth canal than his skeleton predicted.

    ~1.5 Mya

    Two species, one shoreline

    Koobi Fora, East Turkana, Kenya

    A single lake-margin surface preserves footprints made with two different ways of walking, attributed to Homo erectus and Paranthropus boisei (Hatala et al., 2024). Fossils had long put the two in the same basin; the prints put them on the same stretch of mud — lake margins mattered to both.

    ~1.5 Mya

    Attirampakkam

    Tamil Nadu, India

    Acheulean bifaces on the Kortallayar floodplain, palaeomagnetically dated — South Asia was not a corridor hominins passed through but a place they occupied for well over a million years.

    ~1.5 Mya & ~790 kya

    Ubeidiya & Gesher Benot Ya'aqov

    Jordan Valley, Levant

    The Levantine corridor recorded twice: an early Acheulean camp beside a vanished lake, then repeated hearths, burnt flint and charred olive and barley — the strongest early evidence of fire kept deliberately.

    ~1.4–1.1 Mya

    Sima del Elefante — 'Pink'

    Atapuerca, Spain

    A jaw (2008, Homo sp.) and a face (2025, Homo aff. erectus): the oldest firmly-dated hominins in western Europe. Homo antecessor, Gran Dolina's modern-faced species, follows at ~850–780 kya. Stone tools at Korolevo, Ukraine, cosmogenically dated to ~1.42 Mya, mark the door Europe's first people came in by — from the east.

    ~1.2 Mya

    The Gona female pelvis

    Gona, Afar, Ethiopia

    The best adult female pelvis attributed to Homo erectus (Simpson et al., 2008) is short, broad and "obstetrically capacious": wide enough, its describers estimate, to deliver a newborn with a brain of up to ~315 cc, where readings of the Turkana Boy had allowed only a small-brained, helpless one. It overturned a textbook story built on one juvenile's hips — though some researchers dispute that it is erectus at all.

    ≥1.04 Mya

    Calio: Sulawesi reached by sea

    South Sulawesi, Indonesia

    Stone artefacts in fossil-bearing layers dated by palaeomagnetism and uranium-series/ESR to at least 1.04 and possibly 1.48 million years ago (Hakim et al., 2025). Sulawesi was never joined to a mainland, so its first hominins crossed open water — about when Flores was reached, if not earlier. Nobody knows who they were.

    ~1 Mya

    Control of fire

    Wonderwerk Cave, South Africa

    Ash and burnt bone deep in a cave point to fire in hominin hands — still contested as the first controlled fire, but the start, if it holds, of cooking, warmth, protection and further brain growth.

    ~800 kya

    The Bose Basin handaxes

    Guangxi, southern China

    Handaxes lying with tektites — glass flung out by a meteorite impact — argon-dated to ~800 kya (Hou et al., 2000). They challenged the "Movius Line", the 1940s claim that East Asia's early toolmakers never made bifaces and belonged to a Palaeolithic backwater.

    ~700 kya

    Mata Menge

    So'a Basin, Flores, Indonesia

    Tiny jaw and limb fragments show erectus-grade hominins had already crossed the deep-water Wallace Line — and had begun shrinking toward Homo floresiensis on an island they could not leave.

    c. 620–370 kya

    The Karatau Culture

    Khovaling Loess Plateau, Tajikistan

    Stone tools buried in the fossil soils of a thick loess plateau, given Central Asia's first reliable absolute ages in 2026 by luminescence, cosmogenic nuclides and palaeomagnetism (Sørensen et al.). The toolmakers appear as the warm stage MIS 15 began, thrive through stages 13 and 11, and are gone as the cold stage 10 sets in — people who came north when the climate opened the door.

    ~600 kya

    The Bodo cranium

    Middle Awash, Ethiopia

    A large-faced skull found in 1976, whose facial bones carry stone-tool cut marks that Tim White identified in 1986 as deliberate defleshing after death. Ritual, cannibalism or something we have no word for — the bone cannot say. It is one of the oldest signs of hominins doing something to their own dead.

    ~540–430 kya

    The Trinil engraved shell

    Trinil, Java, Indonesia

    A freshwater mussel shell from the collection Eugène Dubois dug at Trinil in the 1890s carries a deliberate zigzag, dated to 540–430 kya (Joordens et al., 2015). Made by Homo erectus, it is the oldest geometric engraving currently known — what it meant, if anything, no one can say.

    ≥476 kya

    Kalambo Falls: the oldest wooden structure

    Kalambo River, Zambia

    Two logs, one laid across the other through a notch cut deliberately into it, preserved in waterlogged sediment and luminescence-dated to at least 476,000 years ago (Barham et al., 2023) — perhaps a platform by the water. Built long before Homo sapiens, it is the earliest known evidence of hominins shaping wood into a structure.

    ~430 kya

    Sima de los Huesos

    Atapuerca, Spain

    A pit of dozens of individuals with incipient Neanderthal features — some of the oldest hominin nuclear DNA ever recovered, and in Cranium 17 two blows to the head from the same object, one of the oldest known cases of lethal violence.

    ~340–300 kya

    An Nasim: a green Arabia

    Nefud Desert, Saudi Arabia

    354 artefacts, mostly finely made handaxes, beside the deposits of a deep, probably freshwater lake in what is now sand sea, luminescence-dated to the wet interglacial MIS 9 (Scerri et al., 2021) — the oldest dated Acheulean in Arabia. The peninsula was a door that opened with the rains, and the tools point to people re-entering it each time it did.

    ~335–236 kya

    Homo naledi

    Rising Star, South Africa

    Over 1,500 fossil elements of a ~500cc hominin that was alive at the same time as the first Homo sapiens — proof that small brains were a living contemporary, not a discarded early stage.

    ~320 kya

    Olorgesailie transition

    Rift Valley, Kenya

    Handaxes give way to small blades, worked pigment, and obsidian carried 25–95 km from its source — exchange and colour, within dating error of the earliest fossils of our species.

    ~300 kya

    Homo sapiens — Jebel Irhoud

    Jebel Irhoud, Morocco

    The oldest fossils of our own species, supporting a pan-African origin and closing this chapter at the threshold of ours.

    ~300 kya (or ~200 kya)

    The Schöningen spears

    Lower Saxony, Germany

    Ten balanced throwing spears of spruce and pine, with throwing sticks, preserved in waterlogged lakeshore muds at a lignite mine. Thermoluminescence puts the horizon at ~337–300 kya; a 2025 amino-acid redating argues ~200 kya and Neanderthal makers. Either way, the one moment wood survives — and the measure of how much of the "Stone Age" was never stone at all.

    ~299–259 kya

    Kabwe & Florisbad

    Zambia & Free State, South Africa

    A heavy-browed H. heidelbergensis-grade skull in Zambia and a lightly built partial cranium in South Africa, both contemporaries of Jebel Irhoud — Africa held several human forms at once, not a single ancestral population.

    Why it happened

    A tilting planet, a drying continent, and a cheaper gut

    Nothing in this chapter was aiming at us. Upright walking, stone, fire and a large brain are four separate answers to four separate pressures, spread across five million years — and the pressures were mostly geological and climatic, arriving from outside the story entirely.

    Tectonics made the stage. The East African Rift has been pulling the continent apart for roughly 25 million years, lifting the Ethiopian and Kenyan domes more than a kilometre and throwing a rain shadow across the land behind them. Continuous forest broke into a mosaic of woodland, gallery forest, lake margin and open grass. The Rift also does something no other landscape does: it drops fresh volcanic ash over occupied ground and then buries it, which is why East African hominins are both abundant and precisely dated while the forest belt of West and Central Africa is a blank. Carbon-isotope ratios locked into soil carbonates and tooth enamel track the rest — C4 grasses expanding across East Africa from about 8 million years ago, dominant after 4 million, and from ~3.5–3.3 Mya showing up directly in hominin teeth — individuals of Kenyanthropus and Lucy's kind were already eating C4 foods, while A. anamensis at ~4.1 Mya ate almost none. Paranthropus boisei enamel is roughly three-quarters C4: it genuinely ate the grassland, sedge by sedge, while early Homo went after the animals that did.

    Then the planet's thermostat changed gear. Through most of the Pleistocene, glacial cycles ran on the ~41,000-year tilt of Earth's axis. Between about 1.25 and 0.7 million years ago — the Mid-Pleistocene Transition — they lengthened to ~100,000 years and grew far more violent, and African rainfall swung with them. A landscape that changes faster than a lineage can specialise selects for the opposite of specialisation: general-purpose bodies, portable technology, and the ability to learn a new habitat rather than be adapted to an old one. That is the argument for variability selection, and the strongest evidence for it is that the winners of this era were the generalists and the losers were the megadont specialists. The two strategies even shared ground: around 1.5 Mya at Koobi Fora, Homo erectus and Paranthropus boisei left their footprints on the same lakeshore, and it was the chewing specialist that ended. See the climate spine for the full record.

    Deserts and cold opened and closed like doors. The same swings decided where hominins could live at all. Outside the tropics the record comes in pulses that track the warm, wet phases. The team that dated Korolevo in Ukraine to ~1.42 Mya argues that the first Europeans used warm interglacials to push north. The Karatau toolmakers of Tajikistan appear as the interglacial MIS 15 begins and are gone as the glacial MIS 10 sets in. The An Nasim handaxes sit beside a lake that existed only because MIS 9 turned the Nefud green, and their excavators read Arabia's Acheulean as people re-entering the peninsula each time the rains returned. The Sahara and Arabia were barriers in dry phases and corridors in wet ones, so "out of Africa" in this chapter was not one exit but many — and many of the early leavers, Dmanisi's people and Java's erectus among them, belong to branches that lead to no one alive today.

    The brain had to be paid for. Brain tissue is metabolically ruinous: about 2% of body mass and 20–25% of resting energy use in a modern adult. You cannot simply grow one. Something has to shrink, and in Homo erectus the gut probably does — molars and jaw muscles reduce and the body grows tall — though the classic 'narrow ribcage' reading of the Turkana Boy was overturned by a 2020 reconstruction that found a short, wide, deep chest quite unlike ours; the case rests on comparative anatomy, not a fossil torso. The bill for a bigger brain is paid by a smaller digestive tract, and that only works if food arrives pre-digested, which is what cooking is: heat gelatinises starch and denatures protein, raising the calories actually extracted from the same meal and cutting the hours spent chewing from a chimpanzee's six down to under one. Fire in this chapter is not warmth and safety. It is an external stomach, and the brain is what it bought.

    There were almost none of us. This is the demographic fact that makes deep prehistory strange. Foragers at this technological level sustain something like 0.01–0.1 people per square kilometre; across seven million years the total hominin population is best counted in the tens of thousands, occasionally the low hundreds of thousands. Genetic reconstruction gives the lineage leading to us an effective population size of roughly 10,000–30,000 for most of the Pleistocene — a species that would today be classed as endangered. One 2023 analysis of modern genomes argued for a crash to around 1,280 breeding individuals between ~930 and ~813 kya; the claim is contested and the method disputed, but even the conservative numbers describe a rare animal. Compare the trajectory in the population series: it takes until roughly 1804 CE for our species to reach one billion.

    Disease, at these densities, works differently. The epidemic crowd infections that drive later chapters — measles, smallpox, plague — need host pools in the hundreds of thousands and dense settlement to persist, and neither existed. What this era has instead is chronic parasitism and zoonosis, and one specific consequence of eating meat: the Taenia tapeworms that infect humans descend from African carnivore–antelope cycles and crossed into hominins somewhere between about 2.5 and 1.7 million years ago, long before any animal was domesticated. Scavenging carcasses from lions and hyenas bought calories and cost intestines. Bone lesions at Sima de los Huesos and elsewhere record healed fractures and, in some individuals, care during long recovery — the earliest hint that survival was becoming a social product. Two older cases sharpen the point. A hominin foot bone from Swartkrans, 1.8–1.6 million years old, carries an osteosarcoma: cancer is as old as the lineage, not an invention of modern life. And at Dmanisi, ~1.77 Mya, an elderly individual lost all but one tooth and lived long enough afterwards for the empty sockets to fill with bone. Whether that means others fed it, or only that soft foods and marrow were enough, is still argued.

    Who did what, and who got to say so. Body-size dimorphism in A. afarensis was pronounced — the "Lucy" individual stood about 1.05 m beside conspecifics half again her size — and it narrows sharply in Homo erectus, a change usually read as a shift away from male–male competition toward more stable provisioning. Meanwhile the obstetric bind is only beginning — the Gona female erectus pelvis at ~1.2 Mya is still roomy enough for a ~315 cc newborn brain — and its logic is that bigger-brained infants must be born earlier and more helpless, which makes a mother alone insufficient and alloparenting structural. Kristen Hawkes's work with Hadza foragers gave that a name — the grandmother hypothesis, in which a long post-reproductive female lifespan is itself selected for, because grandmothers feed weaned grandchildren. Even the bind is disputed: Holly Dunsworth and colleagues argued in 2012 that what sets the length of human pregnancy is the limit of the mother's metabolism, not the width of her pelvis.

    Note also who was doing the inferring: Raymond Dart's "killer ape", and the 1966 Man the Hunter symposium that followed, built an origin story out of male violence, until C. K. Brain's 1981 taphonomy at Swartkrans showed that the "weapons" and broken skulls were leopard and hyena kill debris and that our ancestors were more often prey than predator. The Taung Child itself appears to have been killed by a large raptor. The counter-story came from women anthropologists: Nancy Tanner and Adrienne Zihlman's "Women in Evolution" (1976) put gathering and mother–infant food sharing, not the hunt, at the centre of human origins. The bones can now be asked directly — in 2025 enamel proteins identified the sex of four ~2-million-year-old Paranthropus teeth from Swartkrans — and the chapter's best-known child, Selam of Dikika, is a girl. Coerced labour in the institutional sense has no purchase this early — there are no institutions to coerce with — but the division of effort, and the modern assumptions read into it, are already the contested ground.

    Work, before anyone worked for anyone else. What this era does have is work, and some of it can be seen. Bone fragments from Swartkrans carry the wear of digging into termite mounds, an insect harvest often credited to Paranthropus robustus, the commonest hominin in that cave; at Isampur in Karnataka a limestone outcrop was quarried for handaxe blanks; at Olorgesailie obsidian was carried 25–95 km. None of it implies one person labouring for another. There is no stored surplus to take and no institution to take it — which is why the coerced labour of later chapters has to be explained, not assumed to be human nature.

    And two continents stayed empty, for reasons of physics. The Americas were not walled off by water: whenever sea level fell more than about 50 m, Beringia was dry land wider than Alaska. The barrier was cold. Crossing it demands tailored, sewn clothing, reliable fire in the open, and stored fat through an Arctic winter — a technological package with no trace anywhere in this chapter. Australia's barrier was the opposite: the straits east of Bali stay 250 m deep and tens of kilometres wide even at a glacial lowstand of −120 m, so the Wallace Line never closes. Hominins did cross it — Mata Menge on Flores and Calio on Sulawesi prove that, probably by accidental rafting on tsunami debris — but reaching Sahul required deliberate open-water voyaging of 70–90 km. Neither continent was unreachable in principle. Both were unreachable without equipment that did not yet exist.

    Competing explanations

    • The savanna hypothesis. Forest gave way to grass; standing up let hominins see over it, walk between food patches, and shed heat. Elegant, and largely abandoned — Ardi was bipedal in woodland, half a million years before the grasslands took over.
    • Turnover-pulse (Elisabeth Vrba). Speciation and extinction across African mammals cluster in pulses at aridity thresholds, hominins included. Contested: the pulses partly track how hard palaeontologists have looked in each time slice.
    • Variability selection (Rick Potts). The selective agent is instability itself, not any one climate. Predicts generalists, portable toolkits and long-range transport — which is what the Olorgesailie record shows.
    • Expensive tissue and cooking (Aiello and Wheeler; Wrangham). Gut shrinks so brain can grow, financed by fire. The dispute is the date: anatomy suggests cooking by ~1.9 Mya, but the oldest hearths anyone agrees on are ~790 kya at Gesher Benot Ya'aqov.
    • The social brain (Robin Dunbar). Group size, not diet, drove encephalisation — brains grew to track relationships. Hard to test on fossils, and the group-size correlation is disputed.
    • When the first exit happened. Dmanisi at ~1.8 Mya is a well-dated fossil; Shangchen at ~2.12 Mya is a well-dated artefact with no maker attached. Which you believe determines whether the exit preceded or followed Homo erectus.
    • Who made the first tools. The textbook answer was early Homo. But the Nyayanga Oldowan, ~2.9 Mya, lies beside Paranthropus teeth, and Lomekwi is older than any fossil assigned to Homo. Either several kinds of hominin knapped stone, or the makers have not been found.
    • Why human babies are born helpless. The obstetric dilemma: upright hips cap the size of a newborn's head. The metabolic hypothesis (Dunsworth and colleagues, 2012): birth comes when the mother can no longer fuel the fetus. The roomy Gona pelvis complicates the first; neither is settled.
    • How old the Schöningen spears are. Thermoluminescence says ~337–300 kya and H. heidelbergensis; a 2025 amino-acid study says ~200 kya and Neanderthals. If the younger date holds, the oldest complete spears in the world belong to the next chapter.
    • Whether Homo naledi buried its dead. Lee Berger's team claimed deliberate burials and engravings in 2023, first as unreviewed preprints released a month before a Netflix documentary about the work. Critics — including a 2025 reanalysis that could not replicate the sediment evidence — are not persuaded.

    By 300,000 years ago the whole apparatus is assembled and still almost entirely unused: a body that walks, hands that knap, fire that cooks, a brain at modern volume, and pigment ground at Olorgesailie for reasons nobody has explained. What the next chapter adds is not a new organ but a new use for the old one — symbol, ornament, story and the first deliberate sea crossings — and with it, at last, the two empty continents.

    Meanwhile, everywhere

    Nine regions reached, seven still waiting

    The same seven million years, region by region. Where hominins lived, this is what the ground has given up, and it is a map of where bone and stone survive as much as of where anyone went. Where nobody lived yet, it is the land and the animals the first people would find, much later.

    West Asia

    c. 1.8 Mya – 300 kya

    The first ground beyond Africa, and the corridor back. At Dmanisi in the southern Caucasus, ~1.8 Mya, people 1.45–1.66 m tall shared their valley with two kinds of sabre-toothed cat and a giant ostrich. At Gesher Benot Ya'aqov in the Jordan Valley, ~780 kya, hominins kept hearths and cooked fish; at Qesem Cave, near Tel Aviv, fire was in regular use between ~400 and ~200 kya. Arabia opened with each wet phase: the An Nasim handaxes lie beside a vanished lake in the Nefud.

    North Africa & the Nile

    c. 2.4 Mya – 300 kya

    Hominins reached Africa's Mediterranean rim almost as soon as there were stone tools: at Ain Boucherit in Algeria, ~2.4 and ~1.9 Mya, they skinned and defleshed small bovids and equids. Fossils from a cave at Thomas Quarry I, Casablanca, dated ~773 kya, were argued in 2026 to sit close to the African root of our own lineage, and Jebel Irhoud (~315 kya) holds the oldest known Homo sapiens. Handaxes lie across today's Sahara, left when it was green, but few are dated.

    West & Central Africa

    c. 7 Mya & 3.5 Mya · then near-silence

    The oldest hominin candidate of all comes from here. Sahelanthropus lived ~7 Mya beside a lake in what is now Chad's Djurab Desert, among fish, crocodiles and hippo-like anthracotheres, with sand desert not far off; "Abel" (A. bahrelghazali, ~3.5 Mya) comes from the same basin. Then the record almost stops. Under the forests of the Congo basin and the Guinea coast, acidic soils dissolve bone, and even the stone tools of Senga 5A in eastern DR Congo, published in 1987 as over two million years old, are of disputed age.

    East & Southern Africa

    c. 6 Mya – 300 kya

    Almost everything else in this chapter. The Rift's lake basins and ash falls hold Orrorin, Ardi, Lucy and Selam, the Laetoli and Koobi Fora footprints, the oldest known stone tools and the Turkana Boy; the dolomite caves of Gauteng hold Little Foot, Paranthropus robustus, A. sediba and Homo naledi. By the end the region shows what comes next: obsidian carried up to ~95 km and red pigment at Olorgesailie (~320 kya), a notched-log structure at Kalambo Falls (≥476 kya), and at Kabwe (~299 kya) a heavy-browed human alive beside the first sapiens.

    Europe & the Mediterranean

    c. 1.4 Mya – 300 kya

    A late, northern, on-and-off frontier, entered from the east: tools at Korolevo in Ukraine (~1.42 Mya), then a tooth at Orce and a face at Sima del Elefante in Spain (~1.4–1.1 Mya). At Happisburgh in Norfolk a group of adults and children crossed an estuary mudflat sometime between ~1 Mya and 780 kya; at Gran Dolina (~850 kya) Homo antecessor bones carry the cut marks of cannibalism; at Boxgrove (~500 kya) people butchered horses on a beach below chalk cliffs. The Sima de los Huesos pit (~430 kya) holds early Neanderthals. In the cold stages the north seems to have emptied, again and again.

    Central Asia & the Steppe

    In warm pulses · c. 620–370 kya; first traces ~970 kya

    A frontier opened only by warm spells. On Tajikistan's Khovaling Loess Plateau the Karatau toolmakers arrive with the interglacial of ~620 kya and vanish as the cold returns ~370 kya, their tools sealed in fossil soils between thick layers of wind-blown dust. Deeper soils at Kuldara hold a few simple flakes dated by the same 2026 study to ~970–915 kya — the earliest dated trace of hominins in Central Asia, though too plain to say who made them. On the Taman Peninsula, between the Black and Azov seas, Early Pleistocene stone tools lie among the bones of an extinct southern mammoth. Farther north, across the open steppe, the record for this span is thin, undated or disputed.

    South Asia

    c. 1.5 Mya – 300 kya

    Occupied early and for a very long time. At Attirampakkam, near Chennai, handaxes date to ~1.5 Mya, and the same site shows handaxes giving way to smaller prepared-core tools by ~385 kya, about when the same shift happens in Africa and Europe. In Karnataka's Hunsgi–Baichbal valley people quarried a limestone outcrop at Isampur for handaxe blanks (~1.2 Mya). Bones are almost absent: one poorly dated skullcap from Hathnora on the Narmada stands for the whole subcontinent, and claims of ~2 Mya tools at Riwat in Pakistan are disputed.

    East Asia

    c. 1.66 Mya – 300 kya (1.77 Mya new, 2.1 Mya contested)

    Stone tools at Shangchen may be ~2.1 million years old; the secure record starts with Majuangou in the Nihewan basin (~1.66 Mya) and the Gongwangling skull at Lantian (~1.63 Mya). Handaxes in the Bose Basin (~800 kya) challenged the old claim that East Asia never made them. The crushed Yunxian skulls of Hubei, long put at ~1 Mya, were pushed back to ~1.77 Mya by cosmogenic burial dating in 2026 (a new date, from one method); one of them, rebuilt in 2025, has been placed near the root of the Denisovan lineage, a contested reading, since genetic estimates put that split from our own line at only ~765–550 kya and the older date widens the gap. Japan has no accepted trace of anyone before about 40,000 years ago: its claimed older sites collapsed in 2000, when a newspaper filmed the amateur archaeologist Shinichi Fujimura planting artefacts.

    Southeast Asia

    c. 1.5 Mya – 300 kya

    The far edge of the Old World, and the first sea crossings. When glacial seas fell, Java joined the mainland and Homo erectus walked in (Sangiran, by ~1.5–1.3 Mya); at Trinil one of them cut a zigzag into a mussel shell ~500 kya. Beyond the Wallace Line nobody could walk, yet stone tools on Sulawesi (≥1.04 Mya) and Flores (~1 Mya) and a butchered rhinoceros lying with 57 stone tools on Luzon (~709 kya) show hominins crossed open water to three islands. On Flores, an adult of ~700 kya was already smaller than the later "hobbit".

    Australia

    No people yet · first arrivals c. 65–50 kya

    Sahul (Australia, Tasmania and New Guinea, joined whenever the sea fell) was a marsupial world with no primate of any kind. Diprotodon, a wombat relative weighing a tonne or more, browsed its plains; the marsupial lion Thylacoleo and a giant monitor lizard, Megalania, hunted. The nearest hominins, on Flores and Sulawesi, never crossed the deep straits between. People arrive in the next chapter, perhaps ~65 kya and by ~50 kya at the latest.

    Oceania & the Pacific

    No people yet

    New Guinea was Sahul's mountainous northern end; beyond it, the islands of Melanesia and the open Pacific were reached by no hominin in this span, and the lineages of Flores, Sulawesi and Luzon never got past Wallacea. The remote islands were worlds of birds: in New Zealand, where the only land mammals were bats, flightless moa browsed forests with no mammal to fear.

    The Arctic

    No people yet

    No hominin came near it: the northernmost accepted traces of this span are at about 53°N, at Happisburgh in England, and a claim of tools older than ~260 kya at Diring Yuriakh in Yakutia is disputed. The Arctic was not always ice. DNA from the Kap København sediments shows that ~2 Mya the northern tip of Greenland carried open forest of poplar, birch and thuja, with mastodons, reindeer and hares; mammoth teeth from north-east Siberia have given up some of the oldest DNA ever read from bone, more than a million years old.

    North America

    No people yet

    A continent of big animals and no people. Steppe mammoths crossed from Siberia ~1.5–1.3 Mya and became the Columbian mammoth; more ground sloths and glyptodonts walked up from South America once Panama rose, and sabre-toothed Smilodon hunted. Ice sheets repeatedly buried Canada, while Beringia, dry land whenever the sea fell, lay open to anyone equipped for its winters. In this span, nobody was.

    Mesoamerica

    No people yet

    The land bridge itself. As the Isthmus of Panama closed, around 2.7 Mya, animals crossed both ways and Mesoamerica was the road: the gomphothere Cuvieronius ranged from Mexico's grasslands to Guatemala's gallery forests, and Mixotoxodon, the only South American hoofed mammal known to have pushed north into Central America, reached Veracruz, Michoacán and even Texas.

    The Caribbean

    No people yet

    Islands that had evolved without people for tens of millions of years. Ground sloths had lived on the Greater Antilles since ~32 Mya, and Cuba's Megalocnus grew to the size of a black bear. In the northern Lesser Antilles a giant hutia, Amblyrhiza, weighed perhaps 50–200 kg; bones from Saint-Barthélemy are dated 500,000–400,000 years ago. People reach Trinidad by ~6000 BCE and the islands only later, in Chapter III.

    South America

    No people yet

    A continent that had evolved alone for tens of millions of years, now being invaded. Ground sloths of the genus Megatherium grew as large as elephants, glyptodonts carried armoured shells, and the camel-like Macrauchenia belonged to a hoofed order found nowhere else on Earth. After Panama closed, newcomers came south: sabre-toothed cats, bears, horses, camelids and gomphotheres. People arrive only near the end of the last ice age, in Chapter II.

    Ordinary lives

    Four lives, read from bone and mud

    There are no houses, graves or documents in this span, and no one yet belonged to anyone else. What survives is single bodies and one afternoon's footprints. Each of these is a real individual or group, not a composite.

    Dikika, Afar, Ethiopia · c. 3.3 Mya

    A girl who died at two years and four months

    Selam was an Australopithecus afarensis child, sexed from the small permanent canines still forming in her jaw. Her legs and feet were built to walk upright, but her shoulder blades were those of a climbing ape, and her big toe was more mobile than an adult's: a child who still climbed and clung. Her brain, about 275 ml, was on a longer growth schedule than a chimpanzee's, which points to a long childhood spent depending on the adults around her.

    How we know Skeleton found in 2000 and published in 2006 (Alemseged et al.). Her age at death, 861 days, was counted from daily growth lines in her teeth on a synchrotron scan, which revised an earlier estimate of three years (Gunz et al. 2020). Shoulder: Green & Alemseged 2012. Foot: DeSilva et al. 2018.

    Koobi Fora, East Turkana, Kenya · c. 1.7 Mya

    A woman whose bones bled

    KNM-ER 1808 was an adult female Homo erectus, found in 1974 by Kamoya Kimeu, the Kenyan fossil-finder who would later find the Turkana Boy. An abnormal outer layer of bone on her thigh records bleeding before she died. The team that described her proposed an overdose of vitamin A, perhaps from eating a carnivore's liver, where it concentrates; a diet heavy in bee larvae and the infection yaws have been proposed instead. The new bone had time to form, so she did not die at once. Whether anyone looked after her, the skeleton cannot say.

    How we know Palaeopathology of the skeleton (Walker, Zimmerman & Leakey 1982); the bee-brood alternative (Skinner 1991). The diagnosis is plausible, not proven.

    Dmanisi, southern Caucasus, Georgia · c. 1.77 Mya

    An elder with one tooth left

    Skull 4 and its jaw belonged to an old individual who had lost every tooth but one. The empty sockets had filled in with bone, which takes years, so this person lived a long time unable to chew anything tough, in an upland valley with cold winters, sabre-toothed cats and no evidence of fire. Soft food such as marrow and brains may have been enough, or others may have shared and softened food. It is the oldest toothless hominin known, and the oldest good case for asking what a group owed its old.

    How we know Cranium D3444 (found 2002) and jaw D3900 (2003); healed, resorbed tooth sockets (Lordkipanidze et al. 2005). The layers are dated by argon-argon and palaeomagnetism.

    Happisburgh, Norfolk, England · c. 1 Mya – 780 kya

    A group walking south along a river

    In May 2013, winter storms having stripped the beach, a layer of old estuary mud surfaced on the foreshore with 152 hollows in it. Some still showed an arch, a heel, once even toes. From the twelve clearest, the walkers stood between about 0.93 and 1.73 m tall, perhaps five people of mixed ages, adults and children together. They were heading south over the mudflats of a large river, through open pine and spruce forest in a cool phase of an interglacial, possibly Homo antecessor, the only species known in western Europe at the time.

    How we know Photogrammetry and laser scans made over two weeks, between tides and rain, before the surface eroded away (Ashton et al. 2014). Stature from foot length; age from pollen, fauna and palaeomagnetism.

    Seen whole

    Seven ways to hold seven million years

    How we know

    • Argon-argon on volcanic tuffs. Ash falls, seals a land surface, and starts a potassium clock. This is why East African dates carry error bars of a few tens of thousands of years on a three-million-year age — and why cave sites, which have no tuffs, are the hardest to pin down. The same clock run on tektites, glass thrown out by a meteorite impact, dates the Bose Basin handaxes in China to ~800 kya; that date is only as good as the proof that glass and tools were buried together.
    • Palaeomagnetism. Earth's field has reversed repeatedly; the Brunhes–Matuyama boundary at ~773 kya is a global marker line in the sediment. It is what dates Shangchen's tools in wind-blown loess, Attirampakkam's handaxes in a floodplain and, with uranium-series and ESR, the stone artefacts at Calio on Sulawesi. Its limit: a layer is only "before" or "after" a reversal, so it gives a window, not a point, unless another method narrows it.
    • Cosmogenic burial dating. Aluminium-26 and beryllium-10 accumulate in quartz at the surface and decay once buried. This is the method behind Little Foot at ~3.67 Mya, behind the ~780 kya age for Zhoukoudian, and behind the ~1.42 Mya Korolevo tools that now open Europe's record. It dates when the sediment was buried, not when a tool was made, and needs grains that went down fast and stayed put.
    • Electron spin resonance and uranium series on tooth enamel and flowstone, used where no ash exists — Swartkrans, Isampur, and most South African cave fills. Uranium-lead dating of the flowstone sheets that seal cave deposits, with palaeomagnetism, is what brackets Drimolen's erectus skull to ~2.04–1.95 Mya.
    • Luminescence dating (TL and OSL). Heated flint records when it was last fired; buried quartz records when it last saw light. Thermoluminescence on burnt flint is what moved Jebel Irhoud from ~160 kya to ~315 kya in 2017, adding more than 100,000 years to our species in a single paper. Luminescence also dates the Kalambo Falls logs (at least 476 kya) and the An Nasim handaxes; its limits are signals that saturate beyond a few hundred thousand years, and the assumption that every grain was fully reset.
    • Amino-acid geochronology. Proteins locked in snail opercula break down at a temperature-dependent rate. Measured on shells from the Schöningen spear horizon and compared with regional sequences, they gave the 2025 age of ~200 kya. It is a relative method that leans on calibration elsewhere — one reason the older ~300 kya luminescence age still has defenders.
    • Carbon-isotope ratios in tooth enamel, which distinguish C3 (tree, shrub) from C4 (grass, sedge) foods — the direct measurement behind the claim that Paranthropus boisei ate grassland. The honest limit: "C4" can mean grass, sedge, termites or the meat of grazers, so the number says where the carbon came from, not which food it was.
    • Footprints in three dimensions. Scans of prints are compared with the feet of known species; at Koobi Fora two distinct walking patterns sit on one surface. Assigning a print to a species is an inference from its shape — no bone lies in it.
    • CT and micro-CT. X-ray imaging looks inside fossil bone without cutting it; that is how the osteosarcoma in Swartkrans foot bone SK 7923 was diagnosed. A diagnosis from bone alone can never be checked against the soft tissue it came from.
    • Taphonomy — reading what destroyed the evidence. Acidic forest soils dissolve bone within centuries, so West and Central Africa look empty for reasons of chemistry, not absence. That is precisely why Sahelanthropus and "Abel", both from Chad, matter out of all proportion to their number.
    • Ancient biomolecules. Nuclear and mitochondrial DNA from the Sima de los Huesos femurs at ~430 kya remain among the oldest hominin genetic sequences recovered anywhere. Proteins last far longer than DNA: tooth-enamel proteins have been read from Homo antecessor (~800 kya, published 2020), and in 2025 they identified the sex of four ~2-million-year-old Paranthropus teeth from Swartkrans. The limit is resolution — a few short peptides can place a lineage or a sex, not tell a life.

    The methods themselves are set out in How We Know. Several claims on this page — the bipedality of Sahelanthropus, the 2.12 Mya date for Shangchen, the age of Little Foot, fire at Wonderwerk and the date of the Schöningen spears — are contested and marked so in the tables above; the site-wide register of live debates holds the arguments that reach across chapters.

    In the hand

    Objects from deep time

    Oldowan flake

    ~2.9–2.6 Mya

    A sharp edge struck from a cobble — the first systematic technology, for cutting flesh from bone.

    Acheulean handaxe

    ~1.76 Mya

    A teardrop biface worked on both faces — a shared design held in the mind for a million years.

    Laetoli trackway

    ~3.66 Mya

    Footprints in volcanic ash — the earliest direct record of ancestors walking upright, together.

    Hearth & fire

    ~1 Mya

    Controlled fire for cooking and warmth — a technology that reshaped the body from the gut to the brain.

    Lucy's skeleton

    ~3.2 Mya

    Forty percent of one A. afarensis individual — proof that we walked upright long before we thought.

    Attirampakkam biface

    ~1.5 Mya · Tamil Nadu

    An Acheulean handaxe from a South Indian floodplain — the same design as Kokiselei, 5,000 km away, held steady for a million years.

    Mata Menge molar

    ~700 kya · Flores

    A tooth smaller than a Javan erectus's — an island shrinking its people, on the far side of a sea nobody meant to cross.

    Dmanisi Skull 5

    ~1.77 Mya · Georgia

    A tiny braincase with a massive face, from one site and one moment — the fossil that asks how many species we have been inventing.

    Cut-marked bone

    ~2.4 Mya · Algeria

    An animal bone scored by a stone edge at Ain Boucherit — toolmakers butchering on Africa's Mediterranean rim within a few hundred thousand years of the Rift's first.

    Trinil engraved shell

    ~540–430 kya · Java

    A freshwater mussel shell with a deliberate zigzag cut into it by Homo erectus — the oldest geometric engraving currently known, meaning unknown.

    Kalambo Falls logs

    ≥476 kya · Zambia

    Two logs joined through a notch cut into one of them — the oldest known wooden structure, surviving only because the river kept it wet.

    An Nasim handaxe

    ~340–300 kya · Arabia

    A finely worked biface from the shore of a lake that is now the Nefud sand sea — made when a wet phase opened Arabia to people.
