    Chapter IX  ·  c. 1945 CE – present

    The Contemporary
    & Information Age

    1945 CE – present · a single human lifetime

    One human lifetime from the first nuclear flash to machines that hold conversations — the age of acceleration, written as much in Delhi, Accra, and Shenzhen as in Washington and Moscow.

    Scroll to begin, in the summer of 1945

    The chapter before us

    The previous chapter ended in ruins. Two centuries of coal, steel, and rifled empire had produced a world where a third of the land surface answered to European capitals, and then broke it in two wars that killed close to a hundred million people and ended over Hiroshima. What carried forward into 1945 was not the empires but their machinery: fossil energy at industrial scale, mass literacy, the factory, the census, the telegraph cable — and, newly, the idea of national self-determination, which the colonised had heard promised and now intended to collect on.

    In barely eight decades — a single human lifetime — our species went from the first nuclear flash over a New Mexico desert to machines that hold conversations, from empires ruling a third of humanity to nearly two hundred sovereign nations, and from room-sized calculators to a networked supercomputer in most human pockets.

    It is the first age in which humanity could destroy itself, see itself whole from space, read its own genetic code, and knowingly alter its planet's climate. The through-line is acceleration: each revolution — atomic, postcolonial, digital, genomic, ecological — arrived faster than the one before it and reached more of the world than the last.

    And it is the first chapter of this whole tour centred on no single place. Its decisive moments were written as much in Delhi, Accra, Shenzhen, Nairobi, and Seoul as in Washington and Moscow — and its deepest change was the quietest. Behind the famous population hockey stick, from about 2.5 billion people in 1950 to more than eight billion today, stand two engines rarely put on magazine covers: the Green Revolution in the world's fields, and a global health revolution of antibiotics, mass vaccination, and the 1980 eradication of smallpox that sent child mortality into free-fall.

    01 · Midnight in Delhi

    A tryst with destiny

    At the stroke of midnight on 15 August 1947 the British Raj ended, and nearly 400 million people became citizens rather than subjects — the largest single act of decolonization in history. Its joy was shadowed by Partition, which uprooted some 14 million and killed hundreds of thousands.

    02 · Ghana's black star

    The total liberation of Africa

    In 1957 Kwame Nkrumah's Ghana became the first sub-Saharan colony to go free, a black star for African liberation on its flag. Ghana's independence, he insisted, was meaningless unless it was linked to the liberation of the whole continent.

    03 · The Year of Africa

    Seventeen flags in a year

    In 1960 alone, seventeen African nations raised their own flags. Between 1945 and 1965 the membership of the United Nations more than doubled, from 51 states to 117, and — with Bandung and the Non-Aligned Movement — the map of the world was redrawn by the people who lived on it.

    04 · The long tail of empire

    The last colonies fall

    Algeria won freedom in 1962 after a brutal war, Kenya in 1963, and the Portuguese empire collapsed in 1975 as Angola and Mozambique broke away. Fiji, Papua New Guinea, and a string of Caribbean and Pacific states followed, down to Zimbabwe in 1980 and Namibia in 1990.

    05 · The map completes

    Queues to the horizon

    In 1994 kilometre-long queues waited to vote in South Africa's first universal election, ending apartheid without civil war. With Mandela's inauguration, the great unwinding that began in Delhi reached its close — and its Truth and Reconciliation Commission became a global template.

    At the stroke of the midnight hour, when the world sleeps, India will awake to life and freedom.

    — Jawaharlal Nehru · Delhi · 14 August 1947

    01 · One switch

    The founding artifact

    In December 1947 three physicists at Bell Labs made a sliver of germanium amplify a current — the transistor, a solid-state switch with no fragile glowing tube. It is arguably the founding artifact of this whole age; everything digital descends from it.

    02 · Moore's law

    A metronome for fifty years

    In 1965 Gordon Moore noticed the number of transistors on a chip was doubling every year or two. The 1971 Intel 4004 — built for a Japanese calculator firm — put a whole processor on one chip: 2,300 transistors then, tens of billions now, an improvement of tens of millions of times over.

    03 · The mesh

    A network no one owns

    In 1969 the first ARPANET message crashed after two letters, "LO"; the 1983 switch to TCP/IP and the 1991 Web grew it into a network-of-networks that belongs to no one. It now reaches some six billion people — three-quarters of humanity (ITU, 2025) — thickening fastest in the Global South, where Kenya's M-Pesa let phones leapfrog banks entirely.

    04 · From sand to thought

    The era's open question

    From 2012, chips built for video games trained neural networks that could see, translate, and fold proteins. In late 2022 a chatbot reached some 100 million users in two months — the fastest adoption of any consumer application to that date (by 2026 it was approaching a billion weekly users), and the unfinished question this chapter ends on.

    01 · Two giants

    Two cities past ten million

    In 1950 only two urban areas on Earth held more than ten million people: New York–Newark, with about 12.3 million, and Tokyo, with 11.3 million. The rest of the top ten — London, Osaka, Paris, Moscow, Buenos Aires, Chicago, Kolkata, and Shanghai — lay mostly in the industrial North, and only about 30% of humanity lived in towns or cities at all. The circles are sized by population, from the UN's World Urbanization Prospects (2018 revision).

    02 · The urban explosion

    The giants move south and east

    Seventy years on, the nine largest were Tokyo (37.4 million), Delhi (30.3), Shanghai (27.1), and São Paulo, Mexico City, Dhaka, Cairo, Beijing, and Mumbai at 20–22 million each; Osaka and New York had slipped to tenth and eleventh, while Kinshasa and Lagos, past 14 million, were growing by about 4% and 3% a year. Those are the 2018 revision's projections for 2020, built on each country's own definition of a city. The UN's 2025 revision measures every city the same way, from 1-km population grids built from censuses and satellite imagery (the "degree of urbanisation"), and ranks Jakarta first at about 42 million, ahead of Dhaka and Tokyo — which city is "largest" depends partly on where a city is said to end.

    03 · The Green Revolution

    The fields that fed the cities

    From 1945 Norman Borlaug bred wheat at the Yaqui Valley station in Sonora, shuttling seed some 1,000 km to the highlands near Mexico City to grow two generations a year, and crossing in the dwarfing genes of Norin 10, a wheat bred in Iwate, Japan, in 1935; his programme became CIMMYT. Mexican seed reached Punjab in 1965–66 and helped lift India's wheat harvest from 12.3 to 20.1 million tonnes by 1970, while at Los Baños on Luzon the International Rice Research Institute (founded 1960) released IR8 in 1966 — a cross of Indonesia's tall Peta with Dee-geo-woo-gen, a dwarf rice from Taiwan. The bill is still coming due underground: NASA's GRACE satellites measured groundwater beneath Punjab, Haryana, and Rajasthan falling about 4 cm a year in 2002–08.

    04 · Frontiers of the Anthropocene

    Change you can see from orbit

    The same decades opened frontiers of loss, each now watched from space. The Aral Sea, the world's fourth-largest lake in 1960 at about 68,000 km², was drained for Soviet cotton until its eastern basin lay dry in August 2014 for the first time in modern history, and Brazil's INPE has tracked clearing by satellite since 1988 along the Amazon's "arc of deforestation", from Acre and Rondônia through Mato Grosso to Pará. Arctic sea ice in September, the month of its yearly low, shrank from 7.1 million km² in 1979 to about 4.8 million in 2025 (NSIDC Sea Ice Index monthly means; the record low, 3.6 million, came in 2012), and the Great Barrier Reef suffered mass bleaching in 1998 and 2002, then in 2016, 2017, 2020, 2022, and 2024, with widespread bleaching again in 2025.

    05 · The wired world

    Innovation has many capitals

    The machines of the age were made in many places at once: Silicon Valley grew around Fairchild Semiconductor (1957) and Intel (1968); Shenzhen, one of China's first special economic zones in 1980, became the world's electronics workshop and home to Huawei and Tencent; Hsinchu's TSMC, founded in 1987, now makes most of the world's most advanced chips; and Bengaluru drew Texas Instruments' first Indian design centre in 1985 on its way to becoming the country's software capital. In Nairobi, Safaricom's M-Pesa (March 2007) turned basic phones into bank accounts, and a 2016 study in Science credited it with lifting about 2% of Kenyan households out of poverty, a figure others have since questioned. In Brasília the central bank launched Pix, an instant-payment system, in November 2020; by 2025 some 160 million people used it.

    Milestones

    Forty-five turning points

    1945

    Trinity and the nuclear age

    United States / Japan

    The Trinity test and the bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki (some 150,000–250,000 dead) ended the Second World War and opened the first age in which humanity could annihilate itself — setting the terms of a Cold War arms race that peaked at about 70,000 warheads in 1986.

    1947

    The transistor

    Bell Labs, USA

    Bardeen, Brattain, and Shockley built the solid-state switch that replaced the hot, fragile vacuum tube. Every computer, phone, satellite, and AI model since descends from it — now made by the tens of trillions each second worldwide.

    Aug 1947

    Indian independence & Partition

    South Asia

    The end of the British Raj freed nearly 400 million people — the largest single act of decolonization in history — and signalled that European empire was over. Partition displaced some 14 million and killed hundreds of thousands, a wound that still shapes the subcontinent.

    1948

    Israel, and the Nakba

    Levant · Southwest Asia

    The founding of a Jewish state three years after the Holocaust, and the war that followed, made refugees of roughly 700,000 Palestinians — the Nakba, the catastrophe. It set the terms of the region's next eight decades: four interstate wars, the occupation from 1967, and the conflict that has outlasted the Cold War that once contained it.

    1949–76

    The Chinese Revolution

    East Asia

    Mao's victory ended a century of foreign concessions and civil war and put a fifth of humanity under one state. The Great Leap Forward's famine of 1959–61 then killed an estimated 15–45 million — the deadliest famine ever recorded — and the Cultural Revolution closed the universities for four years and suspended their entrance exam for eleven. Deng's 1978 reforms were the answer to this, not to the West.

    1946–96

    The Pacific proving grounds

    Oceania · Australia

    The United States, Britain, and France exploded close to 300 nuclear devices at Bikini and Enewetak, Maralinga and Monte Bello, Mururoa and Fangataufa. The people of Bikini and Rongelap were resettled, evacuated again, and remain in exile from their home islands, and Anangu country was left contaminated. The same island states are now the most exposed on Earth to sea-level rise they did nothing to cause.

    1949–91

    Semipalatinsk: the steppe as a test range

    Kazakhstan · Central Asia

    The Soviet Union set off 456 nuclear explosions at Semipalatinsk between 1949 and 1989 — 116 of them in the open air — on a steppe Beria had declared uninhabited; Kazakh experts estimate that some 1.5 million people were exposed to fallout. In 1989 the poet Olzhas Suleimenov founded the Nevada–Semipalatinsk movement, one of the first mass anti-nuclear campaigns in the Soviet Union, and on 29 August 1991 Kazakhstan's government closed the site without Moscow's consent. Most of what is known comes from records and field studies opened after 1991; it is now the most closely studied test site on Earth.

    1954–68

    The American civil rights movement

    North America · United States

    The Supreme Court's unanimous Brown v. Board of Education (1954) outlawed segregated public schools; the 381-day Montgomery bus boycott, the sit-ins, and the campaigns from Birmingham to Selma forced the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965, and nearly 250,000 Black Southerners registered to vote in 1965 alone. Martin Luther King Jr. had watched Ghana's flag go up in Accra in 1957, and many in the movement saw their struggle as part of the same unwinding of empire; he was assassinated in 1968. The record: court rulings, movement archives, and FBI surveillance files made public since the 1970s.

    1954–96

    Guatemala: a coup, a long war, and genocide

    Mesoamerica · Guatemala

    In June 1954 a CIA-organised operation (PBSuccess) forced out the elected president Jacobo Árbenz, whose land reform had taken idle United Fruit Company land; a civil war followed from 1960 until the peace accords of 1996. The UN-backed truth commission (1999) put the dead and disappeared at some 200,000, found 83% of identified victims were Maya and 93% of violations the work of state forces, and concluded that the army committed acts of genocide against Maya communities in 1981–83. Efraín Ríos Montt's 2013 genocide conviction was overturned ten days later; the evidence — forensic exhumations, and a police archive found by chance in 2005 — stands.

    1955–75

    Indochina, and the founding of ASEAN

    Southeast Asia

    The Vietnam War killed some 2–3 million Vietnamese and drew roughly three times the bomb tonnage the United States dropped in the whole of the Second World War; its spillover into Cambodia preceded the Khmer Rouge's killing of roughly 1.7 million. ASEAN, founded in Bangkok in 1967, turned the same neighbourhood into the most durable grouping of small states in the post-war world.

    1956–70

    Suez, and the Aswan High Dam

    North Africa & the Nile · Egypt · Sudan

    When the United States and Britain withdrew their offer to fund a high dam at Aswan, Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal on 26 July 1956; the Israeli, British, and French invasion that followed ended in withdrawal under American and Soviet pressure — widely read as the end of Britain and France as great powers able to act alone. The Soviet-financed High Dam (1960–70) ended the Nile's annual flood and displaced more than 100,000 people, most of them Nubians of Egypt and Sudan, as Lake Nasser drowned Lower Nubia; UNESCO's rescue of its temples, Abu Simbel among them, led to the 1972 World Heritage Convention.

    1957–60

    Ghana, Bandung & the Year of Africa

    Ghana · Sub-Saharan Africa

    Nkrumah's Ghana became the first sub-Saharan colony to go free; in 1960 alone seventeen African nations followed. Bandung (1955) and the Non-Aligned Movement gave the newly free a collective voice outside both Cold War blocs.

    1957–69

    Sputnik to Apollo 11

    USSR / United States

    Sputnik's beep, Gagarin's orbit, and Apollo 11 — watched by some 600 million — turned prestige into the satellites that now underpin communication, weather, and navigation, and the "Earthrise" image that helped ignite planetary environmentalism.

    1959–91

    The Cuban Revolution, and Cuba in Africa

    The Caribbean · Cuba → Southern Africa

    Fidel Castro's guerrillas took power on 1 January 1959; within four years Cuba had survived the Bay of Pigs invasion and become the flashpoint of the 1962 missile crisis. In 1961 some 268,000 volunteers, about 100,000 of them students aged ten to nineteen, taught 707,000 adults to read, taking literacy to 96% by the government's count. From 1975 Cuban troops fought in Angola — some 55,000 by 1988 — and the battle of Cuito Cuanavale helped bring South Africa to the 1988 accords that led to Namibia's independence in 1990. How to weigh the revolution's schools and clinics against its prisons and one-party rule is still fiercely argued.

    1960

    The contraceptive pill

    United States · Puerto Rico → global

    Funded by Katharine McCormick and championed by Margaret Sanger, Gregory Pincus and John Rock's hormone pill was first trialled as a contraceptive from April 1956 among low-income women in Río Piedras, Puerto Rico, whose reports of side effects the researchers dismissed; in 1960 the US Food and Drug Administration approved it for contraception. It gave women reversible, private control of their own fertility, and more than 100 million use it today — though Japan licensed it only in 1999, after approving Viagra in six months. With the IUD, sterilisation, and girls' schooling, it is one of the engines of the fertility fall charted below.

    1965–66

    Indonesia's mass killings, and the New Order

    Southeast Asia · Indonesia

    After the '30 September Movement' killed six generals, the army under Suharto turned on the Indonesian Communist Party — then the world's third-largest — and anyone labelled a sympathiser: at least 500,000 to a million people were killed by March 1966 (some estimates reach two or three million), and about a million were detained. Suharto's New Order then ruled for more than three decades. A US diplomat later admitted passing the army lists of thousands of alleged communists, and embassy files declassified in 2017 show Washington followed the killings as they happened; in January 2023 President Joko Widodo acknowledged them as a gross violation of human rights and expressed regret.

    1966

    The Green Revolution

    Mexico · South & SE Asia

    Borlaug's dwarf wheat and IRRI's IR8 rice, paired with irrigation and fertilizer, doubled and tripled yields and helped South Asia escape predicted mass famine — a first engine behind a population rising from 2.5 to 8 billion, at real cost in water and chemicals.

    1966–2023

    Australia counts its first peoples

    Australia

    In August 1966 some 200 Gurindji stockmen, domestic workers, and their families walked off Wave Hill station under Vincent Lingiari — a strike over pay that became a demand for their land, part of which was handed back in 1975. In 1967 Australians voted 90.8% — the largest majority in the country's referendum history — to count Aboriginal people in the census. The High Court's 1992 Mabo judgment struck down terra nullius and recognised native title, now determined over more than half the continent; parliament apologised to the Stolen Generations in 2008. The 2017 Uluru Statement from the Heart asked for a constitutionally protected Indigenous Voice to Parliament; in 2023 voters rejected it, nationally and in every state. At least 50,000 years of occupation — perhaps 65,000 — were admitted in law only this recently.

    1967–70

    Biafra, and the first televised famine

    West Africa · Nigeria

    Nigeria's Eastern Region seceded as the Republic of Biafra on 30 May 1967, after pogroms in the North had killed thousands of Igbo. The federal blockade that followed starved the enclave: between half a million and two million Biafran civilians died, most of hunger and many of them children. Images of children with kwashiorkor made it one of the first wars televised to the world and fed a night-time church airlift; French doctors who had served there and refused to keep silent about what they saw co-founded Médecins Sans Frontières in 1971.

    1945–80

    The global health revolution

    Global · WHO-led

    Antibiotics at scale, mass childhood vaccination, oral rehydration, and the 1980 eradication of smallpox — the only human disease ever wiped out — collapsed child mortality and raised global life expectancy from about 46 years in 1950 to 73 today. With the Green Revolution, the quiet engine of the population surge.

    1971

    Moore's law & the microprocessor

    United States / Japan

    Gordon Moore saw that transistors per chip doubled every year or two — the industry's metronome for half a century. The first microprocessor, the Intel 4004, was built for a Japanese calculator firm: 2,300 transistors then, tens of billions now.

    1969

    ARPANET & the Internet

    United States → global

    The first ARPANET message crashed after two letters — "LO". Packet switching and the 1983 adoption of TCP/IP grew a four-node experiment into an open network-of-networks that no one owns, now carrying some six billion people.

    1971–89

    The Arctic governs itself

    The Arctic · Alaska · Greenland · Sápmi

    The Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of December 1971, still the largest land-claims settlement in US history, gave Alaska Natives title to 44 million acres and about $963 million through twelve regional corporations. In June 1977 Inuit from Alaska, Canada, and Greenland met at Barrow (Utqiaġvik) and founded the Inuit Circumpolar Conference, later joined by Chukotka. In Norway, Sámi hunger strikes outside parliament and blockades against the Alta dam (1979–81) failed to stop it but led to the Sámi Act and a Sámi Parliament, opened in 1989 — and in 2021 Norway's Supreme Court ruled that wind farms at Fosen violated Sámi reindeer herders' rights.

    1973

    The oil embargo

    Southwest Asia → global

    After the October War, OPEC's embargo took crude from about $3 to $12 a barrel in months and ended the post-war era of cheap energy. It is the hinge of the modern economy: it broke the long boom, made energy openly geopolitical, moved the century's largest windfall to the Gulf, and started the efficiency and non-fossil build-out that decarbonisation still runs on.

    1973–90

    Operation Condor

    Southern cone · South America

    The coup against Allende on 11 September 1973 was followed, by 1976, by a pact among the dictatorships of Chile, Argentina, Brazil, Uruguay, Paraguay, and Bolivia that coordinated cross-border disappearances — some 9,000 documented and up to 30,000 claimed in Argentina alone. Their unwinding (Argentina 1983, Brazil 1985, Chile's 1988 plebiscite) produced the truth-commission model South Africa later borrowed, and the elected left of the 2000s "Pink Tide".

    1975–2017

    The Māori renaissance

    Oceania & the Pacific · Aotearoa New Zealand

    In 1975 Whina Cooper, aged 79, led a land march 1,100 km from Te Hāpua to Parliament in Wellington, growing from 50 walkers to some 5,000; the same year the Treaty of Waitangi Act created a tribunal to hear breaches of the 1840 treaty, and in 1985 its reach was extended back to 1840. Kōhanga reo language nests (from 1982) and the Māori Language Act of 1987, which made te reo an official language, turned decline into revival; settlements followed, and Te Urewera (2014) and the Whanganui River (2017) became legal persons. When a bill to redefine the treaty's principles brought tens of thousands to Parliament in November 2024, it was voted down 112 to 11 the following April.

    1978

    China's Reform and Opening

    Shenzhen · East Asia

    Deng Xiaoping's market reforms and special economic zones — Shenzhen, a fishing town become a megacity of 17 million — lifted roughly 800 million people from extreme poverty, the fastest such escape in history, and made China the workshop of the world.

    1979–2003

    Revolution in Iran, wars in Iraq

    Southwest Asia

    Iran's revolution replaced a monarchy with a clerical republic and turned political Islam from an opposition into a governing project. The Iran–Iraq war that followed killed perhaps a million people in eight years; Iraq's invasion of Kuwait and the American invasions of 1991 and 2003 kept the world's most strategic commodity also its most militarised one.

    1979–2019

    The Arctic opens

    Arctic · Canada · Greenland

    Unbroken satellite passive-microwave records since 1979 show September sea ice down by roughly a third to two-fifths in area, depending on the years compared, and far more in volume — the clearest visible signal of warming on Earth. The same decades brought Greenland home rule (1979, self-government 2009), the creation of Nunavut in 1999 as an Inuit-majority territory a fifth the size of Canada, and the first commercial cargo transits of a Northern Sea Route that cuts Rotterdam–Yokohama by roughly a third.

    1981–2020

    HIV/AIDS, and the fight over patents

    Global · Southern Africa

    A virus that crossed from chimpanzees in the Congo basin decades earlier became a pandemic that has killed some 40 million people and, at its 2004 peak, cut more than a decade off life expectancy in Botswana, Zimbabwe, and Lesotho. Antiretrovirals — and the battle over their patents, won by South Africa, Brazil, and Indian generic manufacturers — set the template for global drug access.

    1987

    The Montreal Protocol

    Global

    Mario Molina and Sherwood Rowland predicted in 1974 that CFCs would strip stratospheric ozone; Joe Farman, Brian Gardiner, and Jonathan Shanklin measured the Antarctic hole in 1985. Within two years the Montreal Protocol was signed, and by 2009 every nation on Earth had ratified it — with its parent Vienna Convention, the first treaties in UN history to reach universal membership — the ozone layer on course to recover by mid-century, and the proof that the thing can actually be done.

    1988–2020

    The Amazon frontier

    Brazil · Amazonia

    About a fifth of the Brazilian Amazon has been cleared since 1970, mostly for cattle and soy, and tracked satellite pass by satellite pass by Brazil's INPE since 1988 — the first time a nation instrumented its own land-clearing in public. Parts of the south-eastern basin have flipped from absorbing carbon to emitting it — yet clearing fell some 80% between 2004 and 2012, which proves the rate is a policy variable and not a law of nature.

    1989–91

    The Wall falls; the USSR ends

    Europe / Eurasia

    Peaceful revolutions swept Eastern Europe, the Berlin Wall fell to crowds with hammers, and the USSR dissolved into fifteen states — ending the four-decade bipolar standoff without the nuclear war so long feared.

    1989–93

    The World Wide Web

    CERN · Switzerland

    Tim Berners-Lee's URL, HTTP, and HTML gave the Internet a human face; CERN's royalty-free release may be history's most consequential act of intellectual-property generosity. From ~130 sites in 1993 to over a billion today.

    1990–94

    The end of apartheid

    South Africa

    Mandela's release, four years of talks, and kilometre-long voting queues ended institutionalized racial rule without civil war — the capstone of African decolonization. Its Truth and Reconciliation Commission became a global template.

    1994

    The Rwandan genocide

    East Africa

    In a hundred days roughly 800,000 Tutsi and moderate Hutu were killed, mostly by neighbours and mostly with hand tools, while a UN force already on the ground was ordered not to intervene. It ended the brief post-Cold-War confidence in international protection, produced the Responsibility to Protect doctrine, and stands as the exact counterweight to the same year's queues in South Africa.

    1996–2003

    The Congo wars

    West & Central Africa · DR Congo

    The Rwandan genocide spilled across the border: a Rwandan- and Ugandan-backed rebellion under Laurent-Désiré Kabila toppled Mobutu in 1997, and from 1998 nine African states and some 25 armed groups fought across Congo in what was called Africa's World War. The International Rescue Committee's surveys estimated 5.4 million excess deaths from 1998 to 2007, mostly from disease and hunger — a figure other researchers argue is far too high. Militias paid their way in coltan, tin, and gold, metals that end up on every phone's circuit board; the cobalt of the phone diagram below comes from Congo's southern Copperbelt. The International Criminal Court's first conviction (Thomas Lubanga, 2012) came out of the Ituri fighting, and M23's capture of Goma in January 2025 showed the war in the east never fully ended.

    1990–2003

    The Human Genome Project

    Six-nation international

    A ~$3 billion, six-nation effort read about 92% of the ~3 billion letters of human DNA, fifty years after the double helix — the last 8% (centromeres, ribosomal-DNA arrays, segmental duplications) was closed only in 2022 by the Telomere-to-Telomere consortium. Sequencing costs then fell faster than Moore's law, to a few hundred dollars a genome, enabling CRISPR and the rapid vaccine science of the 2020s.

    2007

    The mobile & social revolution

    USA · Kenya · East Asia

    The smartphone put the Internet in most hands, and Kenya's M-Pesa let the Global South leapfrog banks — M-PESA now handles transactions worth well over twice Kenya's annual GDP (KSh 41.7 trillion in the year to March 2026, against a GDP of about KSh 17.6 trillion). Social platforms rewired public life, for mobilization and for misinformation alike.

    2010

    The Neanderthal genome, and a girl from Denisova

    Leipzig · Altai, Siberia

    In one year Svante Pääbo's laboratory published a draft Neanderthal genome and, from a single finger bone, identified an entirely new human population — the Denisovans — with no skull, no skeleton, and no stone tools to go on. The sequencing this chapter invented reached back and rewrote Chapter I: everyone outside Africa carries 1–2% Neanderthal DNA, and some Papuans up to 5% Denisovan. Pääbo took the Nobel Prize in 2022.

    2010–

    The Arab Spring, and Syria

    North Africa · Southwest Asia

    A Tunisian fruit-seller's self-immolation in December 2010 brought down four governments within a year, organised partly on the platforms this chapter built. Tunisia held a democracy for a decade; Syria's rising became a war that killed over 500,000 and displaced some 13 million, driving what was, until Ukraine in 2022, Europe's largest refugee movement since 1945 and testing the whole post-war asylum settlement. Assad's regime fell to a rebel offensive on 8 December 2024, after more than half a century of family rule.

    2012–23

    Editing and instructing the genome

    Global

    Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier turned a bacterial defence system into CRISPR-Cas9, cheap programmable DNA surgery (Nobel, 2020); Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman's modified mRNA let a COVID-19 vaccine be designed in two days from a posted sequence and reach billions within a year (Nobel, 2023). The pandemic itself killed an estimated 18–27 million and showed both halves of this age at once.

    2013

    Rana Plaza

    South Asia · Bangladesh

    On 24 April 2013 the Rana Plaza building at Savar, outside Dhaka, collapsed after managers ordered workers back inside a day after its cracks had been shown on television, killing 1,134 people — the deadliest garment-factory disaster in history. Its five factories sewed for 29 identified international brands; Benetton at first denied any link, until its clothes and order documents were found at the site. Within a month nearly forty brands and retailers had signed a legally binding Accord on Fire and Building Safety with global unions; more than 200 eventually did. Bangladesh's four million garment workers, most of them women, make the clothes that earn over 80% of its exports.

    1988–2015

    The climate reckoning

    Global · Pacific & China

    Keeling's Mauna Loa CO₂ curve made warming visible; the IPCC, Kyoto, and the 196-party Paris Agreement made it geopolitics, with low-lying Pacific states driving the 1.5°C goal. Solar costs fell ~90% in the 2010s — largely on Chinese scale — making renewables the cheapest new power.

    2012–22

    Deep learning to generative AI

    US · UK · China · Korea

    GPU-trained neural nets cracked image recognition in 2012; AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol in Seoul in 2016; the 2017 transformer and AlphaFold (2020) followed. ChatGPT reached ~100 million users in two months — the fastest consumer adoption to that date, and the era's open question.

    Why it happened

    The engines under the hockey stick

    Acceleration is a description, not an explanation. Underneath it run four causes that had almost nothing to do with each other in 1945 and became inseparable by 2000: cheap energy, falling death rates, the collapse of empire, and the education of girls.

    Start with the bodies. World population was about 2.5 billion in 1950, 4 billion by 1974, 6 billion by 1999, and passed 8 billion in November 2022 — more than a tripling in seventy years, the fastest growth the species has ever undergone. But the curve is already bending: the growth rate peaked at about 2.3% a year in 1963, stayed above 2% through the rest of the 1960s, and is now near 0.9%, and the UN's median projection has humanity peaking around 10.3 billion in the 2080s and then declining. This chapter should not leave a runaway impression, because the runaway already stopped. What drove the surge was not a birth boom but a death collapse: under-five mortality fell from roughly one child in four to one in twenty-seven, and life expectancy went from about 46 years to 73 — antibiotics, oral rehydration salts, chlorinated water, and vaccination, with the 1980 eradication of smallpox (a disease that killed some 300 million people in the twentieth century alone) as its emblem. Disease also pushed the other way: HIV/AIDS has killed around 40 million and reversed decades of gains in southern Africa within a single decade, and COVID-19's excess deaths are estimated at 18–27 million. The population series is here.

    Then the schoolrooms. Global fertility fell from about 4.9 children per woman in 1950 (5.3 at its 1963 peak) to 2.25 in 2023 — barely above replacement — and the single best-attested driver is female education. Bangladesh went from 6.8 children per woman in 1971 to 2.2 by 2020 while its girls' school stipends made secondary enrolment majority-female; Iran's fertility fell from 6.5 to below replacement in twenty years, faster than China's under a one-child policy and without coercion. Adult female literacy rose from around 59% in the mid-1970s to over 83% today. This is one of the largest social changes in the whole tour, and it is almost never drawn as a curve (it is charted below). Coercion did not disappear alongside it: Mauritania was the last state on Earth to abolish slavery, in 1981, and did not criminalise it until 2007; the ILO estimates about 50 million people in forced labour or forced marriage today — more in absolute numbers than at the height of the Atlantic trade, though a far smaller share of a far larger species.

    Then the joules, and the politics of who owes what. Global primary energy use grew roughly sixfold after 1950, and the 1973 embargo is the hinge: it proved energy was a political instrument, ended the long boom, and started the efficiency programmes and non-fossil build-out still running. The atmosphere kept the receipts. Annual emissions and cumulative emissions now tell opposite stories — China emits about a third of the world's CO₂ each year but roughly a seventh of all CO₂ ever emitted, while the United States and Europe together account for more than two-fifths of the cumulative total on a fraction of the population, and an Indian emits about a seventh of what an American does. Every argument about climate obligation is really an argument about which of those numbers counts (the regional split is broken out here; the long climate spine is on the master timeline). Meanwhile the political map dissolved: UN membership went from 51 states in 1945 to 193, and the roughly 750 million people — a third of humanity — living under colonial rule in 1945 fell to fewer than two million in seventeen remaining territories. It is the largest transfer of formal sovereignty in recorded history, and it was won mostly by the people it concerned.

    Choice had a technology as well as a classroom. The contraceptive pill, approved in the United States in 1960 after trials among low-income women in Puerto Rico, together with the IUD and cheap sterilisation, put fertility under women's own control on a mass scale for the first time — but states reached for the same tools by force. India's Emergency carried out 8.3 million sterilisations in 1976–77 alone, mostly vasectomies and many of them coerced; China's one-child policy ran from 1980 to 2015; Peru's government sterilised roughly 300,000 people between 1996 and 2000, most of them poor Indigenous Andean women, often without informed consent. The fertility fall is at once the era's largest emancipation and one of its most contested exercises of state power.

    Work moved, and unfreedom moved with it. In the early 1950s some 2.5 million Soviet prisoners laboured in the Gulag's camps and colonies. As empires ended, people travelled the imperial routes in reverse — the Empire Windrush docked at Tilbury in June 1948 with more than a thousand passengers, most of them from the West Indies — and then the factories travelled to the workers: to Shenzhen's special economic zone after 1980, and to Dhaka, where some four million garment workers, most of them women, sew the clothes that earn over four-fifths of Bangladesh's exports, and where the Rana Plaza collapse killed 1,134 of them in 2013. Of the 27.6 million people the ILO counted in forced labour in 2021, 86% were held by private employers and 14% by states, and adult migrant workers were more than three times as likely as others to be trapped.

    Ecology kept its own accounts. Several of the era's new diseases crossed from animals at the edges people were cutting into: HIV-1 from chimpanzees in the Congo basin, most likely through the bushmeat trade, and Ebola from a wildlife host, probably bats — the 2014–16 West African epidemic infected some 28,600 people and killed about 11,300 in Guinea, Liberia, and Sierra Leone. Climate shocks still killed, but through politics: the Sahel droughts of 1968–85 and Ethiopia's famine of 1983–85 struck hardest where war and state policy had already stripped away people's reserves, and Biafra's famine was a blockade, not a drought. The great engineering answers carried costs of their own: the Aswan High Dam ended the Nile flood that had fertilised Egypt's fields since before the pharaohs, and the Aral Sea was drained for cotton.

    Institutions mattered as much as molecules. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights passed the UN General Assembly on 10 December 1948 with 48 votes for, none against, and eight abstentions; India's Hansa Mehta had pressed for its first article to speak of "all human beings" rather than "all men". It bound no one, yet at least twenty African states that won independence in the following decades cited it in their constitutions, and it became the yardstick for the truth commissions of Argentina, Guatemala, and South Africa. The demographic engine, meanwhile, has begun to run in reverse across much of the world: India passed China as the most populous country in 2023, China's population has been shrinking since 2022, and South Korea's fertility of 0.72 children per woman in 2023 was the lowest of any country.

    What none of this settles is where it ends. The tour stops here, at a species with the tools to read its own genome and cool its own planet, no shared institution capable of deciding to, and a set of curves that have never once been extrapolated correctly. Those curves are what comes next: the coda leaves the narrative behind and puts population, energy, health, carbon, and cities on the same axis, so that the last seventy years can be measured against the seven million that preceded them — The Great Acceleration.

    Competing explanations

    • Institutions or joules? One account credits open science, property rights, and state capacity; another says the post-war boom was mostly the discovery that a barrel of oil does the work of a person for a decade. 1973 is the natural test, and both sides claim it.
    • Did empires fall or were they pushed? Metropolitan exhaustion after 1945 and American and Soviet hostility to European empire are real — but so are Việt Minh, the Algerian FLN, the Quit India movement, and the Mau Mau. The weighting is genuinely disputed.
    • China's escape from poverty. Usually credited to markets after 1978; others point out that the reforms began from a Mao-era base of near-universal literacy and a life expectancy already near 65, which no other poor country had.
    • Why fertility fell. Girls' schooling, child survival, contraceptive access, and the rising cost of urban children all correlate. Their sequence is unresolved, and it matters enormously for Africa's coming century.
    • Drought or decisions? The Sahel and Ethiopian famines, Biafra, and the Great Leap all coincided with bad harvests or blockades. Amartya Sen's Poverty and Famines (1981) argued that famines are failures of people's entitlement to food more than of the food supply, and he later observed that no substantial famine has struck a functioning democracy with a free press. The thesis is widely accepted in outline and still argued case by case.
    • How many died? Estimates for Indonesia in 1965–66 run from half a million to three million, and for Biafra from half a million to two million. For the Congo wars, the International Rescue Committee's 5.4 million excess deaths were challenged by the Human Security Report Project, which argued the surveys assumed too low a "normal" death rate. The totals depend on the counterfactual (how many would have died anyway) more than on counting bodies.
    • Cold War puppets, or local wars? Documents show Washington organised the 1954 coup in Guatemala and aided Indonesia's army in 1965 and Chile's opposition before 1973, and Moscow and Havana armed Angola's government. Historians still argue over how far local armies, landowners, and parties would have acted anyway.
    • Where SARS-CoV-2 came from. Most published genomic and market evidence points scientists to an animal spillover at Wuhan's Huanan market; the US Department of Energy and the CIA (both with low confidence) and the FBI (with moderate confidence) judged a laboratory accident more likely. The earliest samples and records that could settle it have not been released.
    • When the Anthropocene starts. Candidates run from 1610 to 1750 to the 1950s "Great Acceleration" to the 1964 bomb-test radionuclide spike; in 2024 the geological establishment declined to formalise any of them (live debates).

    Meanwhile, everywhere

    One lifetime, sixteen worlds

    Decolonisation, the Cold War, and the great acceleration reached every region, but in a different order and at a different price. These are the eight decades since 1945 as they looked from each of the sixteen regions this tour follows.

    West Asia

    1948–2025

    Iran nationalised its oil in 1951 and lost its elected prime minister, Mohammad Mosaddegh, to a CIA- and MI6-backed coup in August 1953; in 1960 Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela founded OPEC at Baghdad. Oil turned the Gulf's pearling and trading towns — Kuwait, Doha, Dubai — into rich cities where most residents are now foreign workers, many bound to a single employer under the kafala system. Israel's founding and the Nakba (1948), the 1967 war and occupation, Iran's revolution (1979), and the wars in Iraq and Syria set the region's course; the Gaza war that followed Hamas's attack of 7 October 2023 became the deadliest in the conflict's history, and in June 2025 Israel and Iran fought a twelve-day war.

    North Africa & the Nile

    1951–2025

    Libya (1951) and Sudan, Morocco, and Tunisia (1956) became independent; Algeria's freedom took a war of 1954–62 that killed hundreds of thousands and brought down France's Fourth Republic. Egypt's Free Officers deposed King Farouk in 1952, Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal in 1956, and the Soviet-built High Dam at Aswan ended the Nile's annual flood. In Sudan a revolution led by professionals' unions and neighbourhood resistance committees ousted Omar al-Bashir in April 2019; the war between the army and the Rapid Support Forces since April 2023 has become the largest displacement crisis in the world, and upstream Ethiopia inaugurated its Grand Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile in September 2025.

    West & Central Africa

    1957–2023

    Ghana went first in 1957, and in 1958 Guinea became the only French territory to vote "no" to de Gaulle's new constitution and take immediate independence; in 1960 Nigeria, Senegal, Mali, Côte d'Ivoire, and Congo were among seventeen African states that followed. Congo's elected prime minister, Patrice Lumumba, was murdered in Katanga in January 1961 (Belgium admitted "moral responsibility" in 2002), and Mobutu ruled from 1965 to 1997. Nigeria grew from about 37 million people in 1950 to some 228 million in 2023; between 2020 and 2023 military coups in Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger turned the Sahel away from France.

    East & Southern Africa

    1952–2013

    Britain fought the Kenya Land and Freedom Army, the Mau Mau, from 1952, detaining at least 80,000 Kenyans in camps and forcing more than a million into fenced villages before independence in 1963; Tanzania's Julius Nyerere launched village socialism, ujamaa, with the Arusha Declaration of 1967. Ethiopia's emperor fell in 1974 to the Derg, whose war and forced resettlement deepened the famine of 1983–85; Mozambique and Angola won independence in 1975, Zimbabwe in 1980, and South Africa voted in 1994. The last person to catch smallpox in the wild was a hospital cook in Merca, Somalia, Ali Maow Maalin, in October 1977; he recovered, and died of malaria in 2013 during a polio vaccination campaign.

    Europe & the Mediterranean

    1948–2022

    The Marshall Plan (1948–52) and the Treaty of Rome (1957) bound western Europe's old enemies into a common market that grew into the European Union, while Soviet tanks crushed Hungary's rising in 1956 and a Soviet-led invasion ended the Prague Spring in 1968. Portugal's Carnation Revolution of April 1974 ended western Europe's longest-lived dictatorship and, within two years, Portugal's African empire; Greece and Spain returned to democracy in the same decade, and Poland's Solidarity union (1980) opened the road to 1989. The Yugoslav wars brought genocide back to Europe at Srebrenica in July 1995, and Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine on 24 February 2022 began the largest war on the continent since 1945.

    Central Asia & the Steppe

    1949–2022

    Khrushchev's Virgin Lands campaign sent some 300,000 young volunteers in 1954 alone to plough the northern Kazakh steppe for wheat, and Sputnik and Gagarin both lifted off from Baikonur, in Kazakhstan. Cotton irrigation shrank the Aral Sea, and 456 Soviet nuclear explosions shook Semipalatinsk until Kazakhstan closed the site in 1991, the year five Central Asian republics became independent; Mongolia's peaceful democratic revolution came in 1990. Afghanistan lived through the Soviet war (1979–89), civil war, a twenty-year US-led war, and the Taliban's return in August 2021, after which girls were barred from school beyond the sixth grade; in Xinjiang, China has held large numbers of Uyghurs and other Muslims in camps since about 2017, which a 2022 UN human-rights report said may amount to crimes against humanity.

    South Asia

    1947–2023

    Partition in 1947 was followed by Ceylon's independence in 1948 and, in 1960, the world's first woman prime minister, Sirimavo Bandaranaike. East Pakistan's war of independence in 1971, fought amid mass killings by the Pakistani army and with some 10 million refugees fleeing to India, created Bangladesh. The gas leak at Union Carbide's pesticide plant in Bhopal in December 1984 is usually counted the worst industrial disaster on record; India's 1991 reforms opened its economy, and in 2023 India passed China as the most populous country while Chandrayaan-3 made the first landing near the Moon's south pole.

    East Asia

    1950–2022

    The Korean War (1950–53) ended in an armistice close to where it began, leaving the peninsula divided. Japan rebuilt so fast that the Shinkansen opened for the 1964 Tokyo Olympics and its economy was the world's second-largest by 1968; South Korea and Taiwan followed as export economies, and both began their turn to democracy in 1987. In China the Great Leap famine and the Cultural Revolution gave way to Deng's reforms, the democracy movement was crushed in and around Tiananmen Square on 4 June 1989, and Hong Kong returned to Chinese rule in 1997. The region now has the lowest fertility on Earth, and China's population has been falling since 2022.

    Southeast Asia

    1945–2021

    Sukarno proclaimed Indonesia's independence on 17 August 1945, two days after Japan's surrender, then fought the Dutch until 1949; the Philippines (1946), Burma (1948), and Malaya (1957) followed, and Singapore became a city-state in 1965. Wars in Indochina ran, with pauses, from 1946 to 1975, and the Khmer Rouge ruled Cambodia until 1979; the Philippines' People Power revolution ended Marcos's rule in 1986, and East Timor voted for independence in 1999 after twenty-four years of Indonesian occupation. The Indian Ocean tsunami of 26 December 2004 killed about 170,000 people in Indonesia alone, most of them in Aceh; in Myanmar the army's 2017 campaign drove more than 700,000 Rohingya into Bangladesh, and its coup of February 2021 reopened civil war.

    Australia

    1949–2020

    Post-war Australia grew by immigration: more than 100,000 people from over 30 countries built the Snowy Mountains Scheme (1949–74), and the White Australia policy was taken apart step by step until 1973. Britain's atomic tests at Emu Field and Maralinga contaminated Anangu country, while the Gurindji walk-off (1966), the 1967 referendum, and the Mabo judgment (1992) began the return of land, and the 1997 Bringing Them Home report documented the Stolen Generations. In 2019 Budj Bim, the Gunditjmara's landscape of eel traps and channels, became World Heritage; in May 2020 Rio Tinto blasted the Juukan Gorge rock shelters in the Pilbara, lived in for more than 46,000 years.

    Oceania & the Pacific

    1959–2025

    Western Samoa became the first Pacific island colony to win independence, in 1962, followed by Nauru (1968), Fiji (1970), and Papua New Guinea (1975); Hawaiʻi became a US state in 1959, and New Caledonia and French Polynesia stayed French. French agents sank Greenpeace's Rainbow Warrior in Auckland harbour in July 1985, weeks before the Treaty of Rarotonga declared the South Pacific a nuclear-free zone. Bougainville's war over the Panguna copper mine (1988–98) ended in a 2019 referendum in which more than 98% voted for independence, and a campaign begun by 27 Pacific law students in 2019 led Vanuatu to seek, and the International Court of Justice to give in July 2025, a unanimous advisory opinion that states have a legal duty to prevent climate harm.

    The Arctic

    1953–2025

    The Cold War militarised the north. In 1953 Denmark moved about 130 Inughuit 100 km north to Qaanaaq, within four days, to make way for the US air base at Thule, and Canada shipped Inuit families from Inukjuak in Nunavik to Grise Fiord and Resolute in the High Arctic; in 1961 the Soviet Union exploded its largest bomb, the Tsar Bomba, over Novaya Zemlya. From the 1970s Arctic peoples won self-government, through Alaska's land-claims settlement (1971), the Inuit Circumpolar Conference (1977), Greenland home rule (1979), Norway's Sámi Parliament (1989), and Nunavut (1999), while September sea ice shrank from about 7.1 to 4.8 million km² (1979–2025). In 2025 Greenland's future became open geopolitics when the United States pressed to acquire it.

    North America

    1956–2015

    The United States and Canada came out of the war as the richest large societies on Earth: the 1956 Interstate Highway Act and mass car ownership built the suburbs, and the civil rights movement ended legal segregation by 1965. Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962) helped bring the Environmental Protection Agency (1970); the American Indian Movement held Wounded Knee for 71 days in 1973, and in Canada, where the last federally run residential school closed in 1996, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission found in 2015 that the system amounted to cultural genocide. The attacks of 11 September 2001 killed nearly 3,000 people and opened two decades of war in Afghanistan and Iraq.

    Mesoamerica

    1960–2024

    Mexico's ruling party presided over rapid growth until troops fired on students at Tlatelolco on 2 October 1968, ten days before the Mexico City Olympics opened. Guatemala's war and the army's acts of genocide against Maya communities, Nicaragua's Sandinista revolution (1979) and the US-backed Contra war, and El Salvador's civil war, which killed more than 75,000 people between 1979 and 1992 and in which Archbishop Óscar Romero was shot while saying Mass in March 1980, made the isthmus a Cold War battlefield. On 1 January 1994, the day NAFTA took effect, the Maya Zapatistas rose in Chiapas; Mexico's official register of missing people passed 100,000 in 2022, and in October 2024 Claudia Sheinbaum became its first woman president.

    The Caribbean

    1948–2021

    The Empire Windrush carried West Indians to Britain in 1948 as the islands moved toward independence: Jamaica and Trinidad and Tobago in 1962 and Barbados in 1966 (a republic since 2021), with CARICOM (1973) as their common market. Cuba's revolution of 1959 and Haiti's Duvalier dictatorship (1957–86) ran side by side; Haiti's January 2010 earthquake killed more than 100,000 people, and after President Jovenel Moïse's assassination in July 2021 armed gangs came to control most of Port-au-Prince. Puerto Rico, a US commonwealth since 1952, lost an estimated 2,975 people in the aftermath of Hurricane Maria in 2017, and Bob Marley's reggae carried Kingston's sound around the world.

    South America

    1946–2025

    Perón's Argentina (1946–55), Bolivia's 1952 revolution, which gave the vote to the Indigenous majority and broke up the great estates, and Brazil's new capital at Brasília (1960) announced a continent remaking itself. Coups in Brazil (1964), Chile and Uruguay (1973), and Argentina (1976) brought the Condor dictatorships; Peru's war with the Shining Path (1980–2000) killed some 69,000 people by its truth commission's count, three-quarters of them speakers of Quechua or another Indigenous language, and Colombia signed a peace accord with the FARC in 2016. Evo Morales became Bolivia's first Indigenous president in 2006, Ecuador wrote the rights of nature into its constitution in 2008, and more than 7.9 million Venezuelans have left their country as its economy collapsed.

    Ordinary lives

    Four lives the record nearly lost

    Even the best-documented age in history loses most ordinary lives. These four survive because something was written down: a court's files, a testimony to judges, a diary kept on salvaged paper, a contract signed in secret. Often it was written against the wishes of the powerful.

    Kenya · the Emergency, 1952–60 · and London, 2009–13

    Jane Muthoni Mara, who made Britain open its files

    Britain's war on the Mau Mau was fought as much against civilians as fighters. The colonial government admitted holding 80,000 Kenyans in detention camps (the historian Caroline Elkins estimates 160,000 to 320,000), and more than a million Kikuyu were herded into fenced villages. Jane Muthoni Mara was one of five elderly survivors, chosen from 42 interviewed, who sued the British government in 2009; she testified that she had been sexually tortured in detention. The Foreign Office told the court it held no relevant files, then admitted in 2011 that 294 boxes of Kenyan colonial records had sat at Hanslope Park since the 1990s. In June 2013 the government settled with 5,228 claimants for £19.9 million, costs included, and said it "sincerely regrets that these abuses took place", while still denying liability.

    How we know The claimants' witness statements in Mutua and others v Foreign and Commonwealth Office (High Court, London, 2011–12); the colonial files themselves, opened at the UK National Archives as series FCO 141 in 2012–13; the Foreign Secretary's statement to Parliament on 6 June 2013.

    Rongelap Atoll, Marshall Islands · 1 March 1954

    Lijon Eknilang, eight years old when the ash fell

    On her eighth birthday, fallout from Castle Bravo, a US hydrogen bomb some two and a half to three times as powerful as its designers expected, drifted 120 km from Bikini and fell on Rongelap like snow, up to 2 cm deep. The 82 people exposed were not taken off for about two days; they suffered skin burns, vomiting, and diarrhoea, and within a week a secret US study of their bodies had begun. In 1957 they were returned to an atoll declared "clean and safe". Thyroid tumours and miscarriages followed, and Eknilang had eight miscarriages and no children. In 1985 Greenpeace's Rainbow Warrior carried some 350 Rongelapese to new homes on Kwajalein Atoll; in 1995 she told the International Court of Justice what the bomb had done.

    How we know Her statement to the International Court of Justice in The Hague, November 1995, in the advisory proceedings on the legality of nuclear weapons; the US government's own medical monitoring of the exposed islanders (Project 4.1), begun six days after the test.

    Canindé favela, São Paulo · 1955–60

    Carolina Maria de Jesus, who wrote on paper she collected

    Born in 1914 to illiterate sharecroppers in Sacramento, Minas Gerais, with two years of schooling, she came to São Paulo in 1937, worked as a maid, and from 1948 lived in a shack she built herself in the Canindé favela, on the banks of the Tietê. She raised three children on what she earned collecting paper and scrap, and in notebooks salvaged from the rubbish she kept a diary of hunger, prices, neighbours, and politicians. The journalist Audálio Dantas met her in April 1958. Published in August 1960 as Quarto de Despejo ("junk room"), the year Brasília was inaugurated, it sold out its first printing of 10,000 in a week and was translated into thirteen languages. The favela was cleared in 1961 for a riverside expressway; her fame faded, and she died in 1977.

    How we know Her own handwritten notebooks, more than twenty of them; the 1960 book was a selection from them, heavily cut by Dantas, and her daughter Vera Eunice's later interviews describe the household behind it.

    Xiaogang, Fengyang County, Anhui · 24 November 1978

    Eighteen farmers who divided the fields in secret

    Fengyang had been among the Anhui counties worst hit by the Great Leap famine, and 1978 brought a severe drought. That November eighteen farmers of Xiaogang's production team signed a pledge to split the collective's land into household plots, which was then forbidden. They promised to deliver the state's grain quota and ask it for nothing more; if the team's cadres were jailed or executed for it, the others would raise their children to the age of eighteen. The next harvest equalled the team's whole grain output of 1966–70, and it sold grain to the state for the first time since 1957. Anhui's party chief, Wan Li, backed such experiments, and by 1984 almost every production team in China had adopted the "household responsibility system".

    How we know The pledge itself, one sheet bearing eighteen red thumbprints, now in the National Museum of China in Beijing; official statistics on the team's harvests; the signatories' own later accounts, which official histories have made a founding story of the reform era and which are worth reading with that in mind.

    In their own words

    Speaking for themselves

    A president opening a conference, a prisoner in the dock, a miner's wife, a poet, and a survivor: five voices from five regions, in their own English or a standard translation.

    Seen whole

    Seven ways to hold a single lifetime

    How we know

    • The instrument record, continuous and dated to the day. Charles Keeling's CO₂ measurements at Mauna Loa have run almost continuously since March 1958 (a short funding gap in early 1964 and the 2022–23 eruption, when the instruments moved to Maunakea for seven months, are the main interruptions) — 315 ppm then, 427 in 2025 (the May 2026 monthly mean passed 432). Landsat has imaged land cover since 1972, satellite passive microwave has measured sea-ice extent since October 1978, and GRACE has literally weighed the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets from orbit since 2002. This is the only chapter of the tour whose evidence problem is a surfeit of data.
    • Ice cores that overlap the instruments. Vostok and EPICA Dome C give 800,000 years of trapped air — a record the Beyond EPICA core, drilled to bedrock at Little Dome C in January 2025, is expected to push past 1.2 million years — while the fast-accumulating Law Dome cores overlap the Keeling curve, which is how the two records cross-check each other rather than merely agreeing.
    • Official statistics, and their politics. The Great Leap famine toll (15–45 million) is reconstructed from later censuses and provincial grain records, not from anything reported at the time. COVID-19 is quoted as excess mortality (18–27 million) precisely because confirmed deaths were undercounted nearly everywhere. A number's method matters more here than its precision.
    • Declassified archives and sworn testimony. Operation Condor is documented from Paraguay's "Archives of Terror", found in a suburban police station in 1992; apartheid from the Truth and Reconciliation Commission transcripts; the Stolen Generations from the 1997 Bringing Them Home inquiry.
    • Palaeogenomics — this chapter's tools, pointed backwards. The sequencing built for the Human Genome Project is what recovered a Neanderthal genome and an entire unknown population from one finger bone. See how that was worked out.
    • And the failure mode unique to this era: link rot. The best-documented decades in human history sit on the least durable medium anyone has ever used. Roughly a quarter of web pages published in the last decade are already unreachable, archiving is voluntary and incomplete, and no format on this page is guaranteed readable in a century. An Acheulean handaxe from Chapter I will outlast every source cited in Chapter IX.
    • Bones, and the paper the killers kept. In Guatemala the Forensic Anthropology Foundation has recovered more than 12,000 victims from exhumations and identified nearly 4,000 of them by name, and a National Police archive of some 80 million pages, found by chance in an abandoned munitions depot in 2005, documents disappearances from the inside. The truth commission's figure of 200,000 dead is a statistical projection from the 42,275 victims it documented by name — a method its critics still dispute.
    • Counting deaths nobody registered. Where deaths went unrecorded, researchers survey households about who died and when, then subtract an assumed "normal" death rate; the Congo's 5.4 million rests on that baseline, and moving it moves the total by millions. For Indonesia in 1965–66, thirty-nine serious estimates were attempted in twenty years, from the army's 78,500 to the Communist Party's two million.
    • Cables released decades late. US embassy files from Jakarta, declassified in 2017, show day-by-day knowledge of the killings; declassification is selective, though, and what surfaces depends on which government holds the archive and when it chooses to open it.
    • The bomb pulse. Atmospheric tests almost doubled the carbon-14 in the air by 1963, and its steady decline since the test-ban treaty is a clock written into every living thing. It has shown that neurons in the human cerebral cortex are as old as the person who carries them, dated deaths in court cases, and helped identify victims of the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami from their teeth — a clock that will fade into the natural background around 2030.
    • Methods in full: How We Know — and where the claims here are genuinely disputed, the register of live debates.

    In the hand

    Objects of acceleration

    Point-contact transistor

    1947

    A sliver of germanium that amplified a current without a vacuum tube — the seed of every chip, phone, and neural network that followed.

    A grain of IR8 rice

    1966

    The "miracle rice" bred at IRRI in the Philippines: short-strawed and heavy-yielding, it helped turn famine-prone Asia into a food exporter.

    The last smallpox vial

    1980

    Mass vaccination erased smallpox from the wild by 1980 — the only human disease ever eradicated, and the emblem of a health revolution that halved child mortality.

    The Blue Marble

    1972

    Photographed by Apollo 17 — the whole lit Earth in one frame, no borders visible — an image that helped launch the modern environmental movement.

    The smartphone

    2007

    A pocket computer, camera, map, bank, and library at once; within fifteen years it put the Internet into most human hands, from Seoul to Nairobi.

    A literacy brigade's lantern

    Cuba · 1961

    Volunteers sent into the Cuban countryside in the 1961 Year of Education were issued a uniform, a hammock, two textbooks, and a lantern, so that lessons could run at night after the day's field work — some 268,000 teachers, 707,000 new readers.

    A file from Guatemala's police archive

    1882–1997 · found 2005

    One sheet among some 80 million, found by chance in a derelict munitions depot in Guatemala City — the paperwork of a war's disappearances, now used as evidence in trials.

    A label from Rana Plaza

    Savar, Bangladesh · 2013

    Labels and order papers found in the ruins helped identify the 29 international brands that had sourced from the factories where 1,134 people died — the global supply chain, traced backwards from a stitched tag.

    A petrol ration coupon

    1974

    Printed in a hurry from Rotterdam to Washington after the OPEC embargo, and in the American case never issued — the month the industrial world discovered that the cheapest input to everything it made belonged to somebody else.

    Hōkūleʻa, the voyaging canoe

    1976

    A double-hulled canoe sailed from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti with no instruments, navigating by stars, swell, and birds — proof that Oceania was settled deliberately, and the spark of a Pacific cultural revival still running.

    The Denisova finger bone

    2008 · sequenced 2010

    A fragment the size of a lentil, from a cave in the Altai, that turned out to belong to an entirely unknown human population — the first branch of our family tree ever discovered by genome rather than by anatomy.
