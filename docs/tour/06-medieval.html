    Chapter VI  ·  500 – 1450 CE

    The Post-Classical
    & Medieval World

    500 CE – 1450 CE · a polycentric millennium

    The so-called Dark Age was the golden age of Baghdad, Kaifeng, and Angkor — until the very trade roads that carried paper and algebra also carried the Black Death.

    Scroll to begin, ~500 CE

    The chapter before us

    For a thousand years the West would remember this age as "dark" — but the light was simply somewhere else. The planet's centres of learning, wealth, and invention sat in Baghdad, Kaifeng, Constantinople, Cairo, Angkor, and Tenochtitlan, not in Paris or Rome. This is the era of a genuinely polycentric world, in which no single civilization held the lead for long.

    The previous chapter ended with the classical empires coming apart at different speeds. The Han had fallen in 220, the last western Roman emperor was deposed in 476, and the Gupta realm dissolved around 550 — but Byzantium still held Constantinople, Sasanian Persia still held Ctesiphon, and the Silk Road, the codex, cast iron, coined money, and three expanding religions all crossed the break intact. What follows is not a collapse but a redistribution. The centre of gravity moves east and south, and it does not move back for eight hundred years.

    Paper, printing, gunpowder, the compass, algebra, and hospital medicine all emerged or matured in Asia and the Islamic world, then flowed westward along the Silk Road and the Indian Ocean monsoon lanes. While Afro-Eurasia traded and translated, Polynesian navigators completed humanity's settlement of the planet, and civilizations in the Americas — the astronomer city-states of the Maya, the Mississippian metropolis of Cahokia on the middle river, the Toltec and the rising Mexica of the Valley of Mexico, and the Andean heirs of Tiwanaku and Wari — built some of Earth's largest cities in total isolation from the other hemisphere.

    Faiths travelled with the goods. Islam spread from al-Andalus to the Niger bend and the Swahili coast; Buddhism rode the Silk Road and was copied into the world's oldest dated printed book; Christianity anchored itself at Constantinople and radiated north into Europe. And the era's connective tissue — caravan roads, sea lanes, and above all the Mongol-secured steppe highway — proved to be its undoing: the same network that moved silk, scholars, and ideas carried Yersinia pestis, the Black Death, a catastrophe that cracked open the old order and set the stage for the early modern world.

    People alive, 500 CE

    250M

    About 253 million in HYDE 3.3, 206 million in Biraben, 190 million in McEvedy & Jones. No census covered more than one state. McEvedy & Jones 1978 · Biraben 1979 · HYDE 3.3

    People alive, 1300 CE

    400M

    Perhaps 360–460 million: each reconstruction roughly doubles its own figure for 500 CE. Then came the Black Death, and every reconstruction shows fewer people in 1400. McEvedy & Jones 1978 · Biraben 1979 · HYDE 3.3

    People in Greater Angkor, 1200s

    800K

    A modelled 700,000–900,000 across a low-density city of canals, rice fields, and reservoirs. It is built from lidar, excavation, and radiocarbon, not a head count. Klassen et al. 2021, Science Advances

    Mongol Empire at its height

    24M km²

    About 24 million km² around 1270, roughly a sixth of Earth's land: the largest contiguous land empire there has ever been. Taagepera 1997 · Turchin et al. 2006

    01 · Monsoon

    The ocean's engine

    Twice a year the monsoon reverses, and lateen-sailed dhows ride it between the East African coast and India as reliably as a ferry. Kilwa, Mombasa, and Mogadishu grow rich as the western hinges of a trade world that runs from Sofala to Guangzhou.

    02 · Gold & stone

    Gold for porcelain

    Coral-stone Swahili cities trade African gold, ivory, and iron for Chinese porcelain and Indian cloth. Inland, on the profits of the gold routes, the granite towers of Great Zimbabwe rise — a mortarless African capital of some thousands: no more than 10,000 on a 2017 re-estimate, though older figures ran to 18,000.

    03 · Treasure fleets

    The largest ships afloat

    Ming China's admiral Zheng He crosses this ocean seven times with fleets of hundreds of vessels, calling at Malindi and Mogadishu and carrying a giraffe home to the emperor — the grandest voyages of the age, made a lifetime before any European rounded Africa.

    04 · Wayfinding

    Into the open Pacific

    Eastward, Polynesian navigators cross thousands of kilometres of empty ocean in double-hulled canoes, steering by stars, swells, and the flight of birds with no instruments at all — reaching Hawaiʻi and, far to the southeast, Rapa Nui.

    05 · Aotearoa

    The migration closes

    Around 1250 the waka reach Aotearoa / New Zealand — the last major habitable landmass humans ever found. A journey begun in Africa tens of thousands of years earlier ends here. (A tiny Norse knarr had brushed Newfoundland c. 1000 — a footnote on the far side of the world.)

    Kilwa is one of the most beautiful and well-constructed towns in the world. — Ibn Battuta, on the Swahili Coast, 1331

    01 · 1206

    A spark on the steppe

    On the Onon River the tribes proclaim Temujin as Genghis Khan. From this single point a cavalry state explodes outward, taking Beijing in 1215, Samarkand in 1220, and Baghdad in 1258.

    02 · The empire

    One road, ocean to ocean

    At its height the Mongol Empire covers roughly 24 million square kilometres from Korea to Hungary — the largest contiguous land empire ever assembled. Across it the Pax Mongolica and the yam relay-post system make a single, guarded highway of the whole steppe.

    03 · The relay

    The hinge of the world

    Along these secured roads move merchants like Marco Polo, missionaries, artisans, and technologies — gunpowder, the compass, printing, and paper flowing steadily westward. For a few decades Eurasia is effectively one connected market.

    04 · The shadow

    What else the road carried

    In 1346 a black pulse enters the same network: from a Central Asian reservoir the plague reaches the Crimean port of Kaffa; by the summer of 1347 it is in Constantinople, and Genoese galleys carry it to Messina that October. Alexandria, Cairo, and Genoa darken in dated sequence.

    05 · Aftermath

    A world cracked open

    The Black Death kills perhaps a third to a half of Europe and perhaps a third of the Middle East and North Africa — several million more, on far thinner records. Yet from the wreckage: scarce labour raises wages, serfdom loosens, and a shaken world begins to reconnect — this time by sea. "O happy posterity, who will not experience such abysmal woe." — Petrarch, c. 1348.

    01 · The Arctic

    Two peoples meet at the top of the world

    Thule whale hunters, grown out of the Birnirk tradition at Point Barrow in northern Alaska by about 1000, carried the toggling harpoon, the skin-hulled umiak, and the dog sled east across Arctic Canada and reached northwest Greenland in the 1200s; ancient DNA shows they replaced the Dorset people already there rather than descending from them. From the other side came the Norse, sailing on from Iceland: Erik the Red's farm at Brattahlíð in Greenland from c. 985, and a camp at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland, where tree rings carrying the spike of the 993 solar storm date felled wood to 1021 — the first exact year for Europeans in the Americas, at the only Norse site yet confirmed beyond Greenland. The two met: dozens of Norse objects, chain mail among them, turn up in Thule houses of c. 1250–1400 on Ellesmere Island's Bache Peninsula and nearby Skraeling Island, though whether they came by visit, trade, or shipwreck is unknown.

    02 · River & canyon

    Cahokia and Chaco

    Around 1050 Cahokia, on the Mississippi floodplain opposite modern St. Louis, was rebuilt almost at once around a great plaza and Monks Mound, the largest earthen mound north of Mexico; at its height, c. 1050–1200, it held perhaps 10,000–20,000 people (estimates range wider, and are contested), the largest centre of a maize-farming, mound-building Mississippian world that reached Moundville in Alabama. In Chaco Canyon, ancestral Puebloans raised multistorey great houses between c. 850 and 1150, and cacao residue in Pueblo Bonito's cylinder jars and the bones of scarlet macaws show goods arriving from Mesoamerica, at least 1,900 km to the south. Chaco's building stopped in the droughts after about 1130; Cahokia was empty by about 1350.

    03 · Maya & Mexica

    From Tikal to Tenochtitlan

    The Classic Maya kingdoms fought for generations over hegemony — Calakmul's Snake dynasty against Tikal, which defeated its rival's king in 695 — and airborne lidar of northern Guatemala, published in 2018, mapped more than 60,000 structures under the forest. After c. 800 the southern cities emptied amid war and repeated droughts recorded in lake sediments; Tikal's last dated monument was raised in 869, while in the north Chichén Itzá flourished until about 1100. In the central highlands the Toltec capital, Tula, dominated c. 900–1150, and around 1325 the Mexica founded Tenochtitlan on an island in Lake Texcoco — the city whose Triple Alliance of 1428 would build the Aztec empire.

    04 · The islands

    Caciques and bateyes

    The island farmers of the Caribbean descended from Saladoid potters who paddled up the Lesser Antilles from the lower Orinoco from c. 500 BCE, and ancient DNA published in 2020–21 traces them to a migration from mainland South America. By c. 1200 the Taíno of Hispaniola, Puerto Rico, and eastern Cuba lived in chiefdoms ruled by caciques, grew cassava in mounded fields, honoured ancestral zemis, and played a rubber-ball game on stone-lined plazas called bateyes — Caguana in Puerto Rico, built c. 1270, has more than ten. In the Lesser Antilles the Kalinago are linked to Cayo pottery, which appears from Grenada to Guadeloupe from c. 1250; Spain's use of "Carib" to mean cannibal later licensed their enslavement, and how sharply the two peoples differed is debated — Kalinago descendants still hold their own territory on Dominica.

    05 · The Andes

    Tiwanaku, Wari, Chimor, the Inca

    Two highland states shared the Andes c. 600–1000: Tiwanaku, beside Lake Titicaca, fed by raised fields and llama caravans, and Wari, near modern Ayacucho, which planted walled administrative centres along roads, some of which the Inca would later reuse. Both faded around 1000, Tiwanaku perhaps under prolonged drought, though that is argued. On the desert coast Chimor's capital, Chan Chan, grew from c. 900 into some 20 km² of adobe compounds; and from Cusco, after Pachacuti's victory over the Chanka, traditionally dated 1438, the Inca began an expansion that swept south over the Titicaca basin, took Chimor c. 1470, and within a century ran from Ecuador to central Chile.

    06 · The forest

    Towns of earth in Amazonia

    Airborne lidar has overturned the picture of Amazonia as untouched wilderness. In Bolivia's Llanos de Mojos the Casarabe culture (c. 500–1400) raised platforms and pyramids up to 22 m high at Cotoca and Landívar, ringed by banks and joined by kilometres of straight raised causeways, with canals and reservoirs — the low-density urbanism Heiko Prümers's team published in 2022. In Brazil's Upper Xingu, Michael Heckenberger, working with the Kuikuro, mapped ditch-ringed towns such as Kuhikugu linked by straight roads, at their largest c. 1250–1650; and in Ecuador's Upano valley, lidar published in 2024 revealed some 6,000 earthen platforms joined by roads, a landscape so old (c. 500 BCE to 300–600 CE) that it had emptied before this chapter began.

    Milestones

    Forty-six turning points

    c. 500–1000 CE

    Austronesians settle Madagascar

    Borneo to the Mozambique Channel

    Malagasy is a Barito language of southern Borneo, and the island's rice, bananas, taro, and outrigger canoes arrived with it — a crossing of some 7,000 km of open Indian Ocean, the longest migration of the prehistoric world, made not by drift but by design; the firm archaeology dates settlement to c. 700–1000, and the older claims remain contested.

    c. 500–1450 CE

    The continental exchange network

    Aboriginal Australia

    Australia's roads carried no wheels and crossed no frontiers, yet engraved pearl shell from the Kimberley coast reached desert communities 2,000 km inland, greenstone axeheads from the Mount William quarry in Victoria travelled 800 km, and the Gunditjmara eel channels at Budj Bim — first cut some 6,600 years ago — were still being maintained: an exchange system running on ceremony and obligation rather than markets or states.

    536–549 CE

    The dust veil & the Justinianic Plague

    Eurasia & the Mediterranean

    Volcanic sulfate in Greenland ice and the narrowest tree rings of two millennia date a hemispheric cooling that Ulf Büntgen's team named the Late Antique Little Ice Age (536–660); five years into it the first plague pandemic arrived, confirmed in 2013 when Michaela Harbeck and Johannes Krause recovered Yersinia pestis DNA from teeth in a grave at Aschheim in Bavaria — settling what the pestilence was, though how many it killed is now sharply disputed.

    537 CE

    Hagia Sophia & Justinian's Byzantium

    Constantinople

    Justinian's vast domed cathedral — the largest cathedral on Earth for nearly a millennium, until Seville's was finished in the early 1500s, and on completion the largest interior ever enclosed — declared that Rome had not fallen but moved east; Byzantium kept Roman law, Greek learning, and city life alive for a thousand years.

    c. 550 CE

    Teotihuacan burns

    Valley of Mexico

    A grid-planned city of perhaps 100,000 or more at its height, with apartment compounds housing Maya, Zapotec, and Gulf-coast residents, had the temples and palaces along its Street of the Dead deliberately put to the torch — the burning concentrated on the elite core, which argues for revolt from inside rather than conquest from outside — and Mesoamerica went the next eight centuries without a single dominant capital, passing the initiative to Tula, Chichén Itzá, and Mayapán.

    c. 600–900 CE

    Maya Classic zenith

    Mesoamerica

    Tikal, Calakmul, and Copán perfected glyphic writing, the Long Count calendar, positional zero, and eclipse-accurate astronomy — with no contact whatsoever with the Old World — before drought and war unravelled the southern lowland cities.

    c. 600–1000 CE

    Tiwanaku & Wari

    Andes (Bolivia & Peru)

    Twin highland states pioneered raised-field farming, terracing, llama caravans, roads, and administrative colonies knitting coast to mountains — the state-building toolkit the Inca would later inherit whole.

    622–750 CE

    The rise of Islam

    Arabia to Iberia & the Indus

    From Muhammad's hijra to Medina in 622 — year one of a new calendar — Rashidun armies broke the Byzantines at Yarmouk and the Sasanians at al-Qadisiyyah in 636, took Egypt in 641, extinguished the Sasanian Empire by 651, and crossed into Iberia in 711; the Umayyads ruled from Damascus until the Abbasid revolution of 750 moved the centre east to a new round city, Baghdad, founded in 762 and soon the largest on Earth — and Arabic became the working language of science from the Atlantic to Samarkand.

    645–1281 CE

    Nara, Heian & the first novel

    Japan

    The Taika reforms of 645 rebuilt Japan on a Tang blueprint of censuses, land grants, and law codes; three centuries later, at a Heian court where classical Chinese was the men's language, Murasaki Shikibu wrote The Tale of Genji (c. 1010) in the kana women used — a psychologically interior work of prose fiction with a serious claim to being the world's first novel — and the warrior government established at Kamakura in 1185 twice survived Mongol invasion, in 1274 and 1281.

    652–1270 CE

    The baqt & the rock churches of Lalibela

    Nubia & the Ethiopian highlands

    After two failed sieges of Dongola, Arab Egypt signed the baqt with Christian Makuria in 652 — a non-conquest pact, paid partly in enslaved people sent north each year, that held for roughly six centuries and kept Nubia independent longer than almost any medieval state — while further south the Zagwe kings cut eleven churches downward into living rock at Lalibela around 1200, and the Solomonic restoration of 1270 founded a line that ruled Ethiopia until 1974.

    c. 700–1235 CE

    Djenné-Djenno, Ghana & the founding of Mali

    Sahel & West Africa

    Roderick and Susan McIntosh's excavations at Djenné-Djenno from 1977 revealed a city of perhaps 20,000 at its c. 800 peak with no palace, no citadel, and no trace of a king — evidence that urbanism need not require a state — while to the north-west, between the upper Senegal and the upper Niger, the Ghana Empire taxed the gold-for-salt caravans until Sundiata Keita's victory at Kirina around 1235 replaced it with Mali, whose constitution was recited by griots rather than written down.

    732–735 CE

    The Orkhon inscriptions

    Central Asia & the Steppe (Mongolia)

    Memorial stelae raised in Mongolia's Orkhon valley for the Türk prince Kül Tigin (732) and his brother Bilge Qaghan (735) carry texts in Chinese and in Old Turkic — with the nearby Tonyukuk stele, the oldest substantial writing in any Turkic language. In them a steppe empire tells its own history of conquest, submission to the Tang, and revival, and warns its people against the sweet words and soft silks of China; Vilhelm Thomsen deciphered the runiform script in 1893.

    735–737 CE

    Smallpox strikes Nara Japan

    East Asia (Japan)

    Recorded at Dazaifu in northern Kyushu in 735, smallpox reached the capital by 737 and killed all four Fujiwara brothers who dominated the court; working from surviving provincial fiscal reports, the historian William Wayne Farris estimated that a quarter to a third of Japan's people died. The state remitted taxes across the country, and Emperor Shōmu answered with Buddhism on a monumental scale — the Great Buddha of Tōdai-ji, dedicated in 752.

    751 CE

    Paper goes west after Talas

    Central Asia to the Middle East

    After Abbasid and Tang armies met at Talas, Chinese papermaking spread through Samarkand into the Islamic world (the story that captured papermakers carried it there is told only later, and is contested); cheap paper replaced parchment and papyrus and fuelled Baghdad's bureaucracy, book markets, and translation movement.

    800 CE

    Charlemagne & the feudal order

    Western Europe

    Crowned by the pope on Christmas Day 800, Charlemagne revived the idea of a Western empire and a fragile renewal of learning; after it splintered, Europe reorganised into the lord-and-vassal, manor-and-serf web of feudalism.

    c. 800–1293 CE

    Borobudur, Bach Dang & the mandala states

    Java, the Irrawaddy & the Red River

    Sailendra Java raised Borobudur — 2,672 relief panels and 504 Buddhas in cut andesite — around 800; Ngo Quyen's iron-tipped stakes in the Bach Dang estuary ended a thousand years of Chinese rule over Dai Viet in 938; Bagan covered the Irrawaddy plain with more than 10,000 temples between 1044 and 1287; and Srivijaya, then Majapahit after 1293, held the straits that every ship between China and India had to pass through.

    c. 800–1130 CE

    A line of mothers at Pueblo Bonito

    North America (Chaco Canyon)

    Nine people buried over some 330 years in Room 33, the richest burial chamber known in the ancient Southwest, share one mitochondrial genome, and nuclear DNA links them as mother and child, grandparent and grandchild — the strongest evidence yet for hereditary leadership in the Southwest, passed down a female line, as descent is reckoned among the Hopi and Zuni today. Ancient DNA and radiocarbon dates on the burials (Kennett et al. 2017) make the case; how far one crypt speaks for all of Chaco is still argued.

    c. 800–1200 CE

    The bronze-casters of Igbo-Ukwu

    West Africa (south-eastern Nigeria)

    Thurstan Shaw's excavations of 1959–60 and 1964 opened a shrine store, a richly furnished burial, and a disposal pit holding copper-alloy vessels and regalia cast by lost wax in intricate detail, woven textiles, and more than 150,000 glass and stone beads. Lead isotopes trace most of the metal to ores in south-eastern Nigeria, while much of the glass came from Egypt, Mesopotamia, or the eastern Mediterranean — the forest zone was already inside long-distance trade; radiocarbon dates spread from the ninth century into the twelfth, and the exact age is still argued.

    c. 813–833 CE

    The House of Wisdom

    Baghdad & the Islamic world

    Abbasid scholars translated Greek, Persian, and Sanskrit science into Arabic then surpassed it: al-Khwarizmi's al-jabr named algebra (and his name gave us "algorithm"), while Ibn Sina's Canon taught European doctors into the 1600s.

    c. 850–1350 CE

    Chaco, Cahokia & the megadrought

    North America

    Pueblo Bonito's 650 rooms anchored a Chacoan world of great houses linked by more than 300 km of engineered road and framed with 200,000 timbers hauled 75 km from the Chuska mountains, while on the Mississippi floodplain Cahokia was refounded around 1050 and raised Monks Mound, the largest earthwork in the Americas — Chaco's building stopped around 1130, in a fifty-year drought, and the canyon was largely deserted after 1150; the later 1276–1299 megadrought emptied the Four Corners cliff dwellings that had succeeded it, and Cahokia was empty by about 1350, though a 2024 soil-isotope study found no sign of the drought-driven crop failure long blamed for it. The droughts are dated to the year because A. E. Douglass, chasing sunspot cycles through tree rings, bridged the Southwest's floating chronology to the calendar in 1929.

    868–1048 CE

    The print revolution

    Tang & Song China

    The 868 Diamond Sutra is the world's oldest dated printed book; by the Song, woodblock printing made books cheap and abundant, and the artisan Bi Sheng cut movable ceramic type — four centuries before Gutenberg.

    869–883 CE

    The Zanj revolt

    West Asia (southern Iraq) & the East African trade

    Workers shipped from East Africa to clear the salt-crusted marshes near Basra rose under Ali ibn Muhammad, built their own capital at al-Mukhtara, sacked Basra in 871, and held off the Abbasid caliphate for fourteen years — often called the largest slave revolt of the medieval world. Almost everything known comes from the chronicler al-Tabari, and how many of the rebels were enslaved Africans rather than free Africans, Arabs, and the marsh poor is argued.

    c. 890 CE

    Ohthere describes the Sámi

    The Arctic (Hålogaland, northern Norway)

    A chieftain who said no Norwegian lived farther north than he did told King Alfred's court in England how he had sailed round the North Cape into the White Sea, kept 600 tame reindeer (six of them decoys for catching wild ones), and lived on tribute from the Finnas — the Sámi — paid in marten and reindeer skins, bearskins, feathers, and ship's ropes of whale and seal hide. Preserved in the Old English version of Orosius's history, it is the earliest detailed eyewitness account of Sámi life and the first written mention of reindeer herding in Norway.

    c. 900–1470 CE

    Chan Chan & the Inca explosion

    Andean coast & highlands

    Chimor's capital at Chan Chan sprawled across roughly 20 km² of adobe — the largest mud-brick city ever built, watered by canals cut between river valleys — until the Inca absorbed it around 1470, a generation or so after Pachacuti's victory over the Chanka — traditionally dated 1438, though radiocarbon from Machu Picchu and the empire's frontiers runs earlier — launched an empire that would lay 40,000 km of road, tax in rotated mit'a labour instead of money, and keep its accounts in knotted khipu.

    900 CE

    The Laguna Copperplate

    Southeast Asia (Luzon, the Philippines)

    A thin copper sheet dated to the year 822 of the Saka era — 21 April 900 — grants a woman named Angkatan and her relative Bukah, the child of one Namwaran, a document of full acquittal, clearing all Namwaran's descendants of a debt of 1 kati and 8 suwarna of gold, the better part of a kilogram. Written in Old Malay in the Javanese Kawi script with Sanskrit legal terms, it is the oldest known dated document from the Philippines, proof that the archipelago's polities kept written records in the Malay world's shared language six centuries before Spain arrived; a labourer dredging sand near the mouth of the Lumbang River found it in 1987.

    918–1377 CE

    Goryeo, the Tripitaka Koreana & the Jikji

    Korea

    After Mongol armies burned the first set in 1232, Goryeo re-carved the entire Buddhist canon onto 81,258 woodblocks between 1236 and 1251 — still complete at Haeinsa, and still all but free of error — and in 1377 the monks of Heungdeok temple at Cheongju printed the Jikji with movable metal type, the oldest such book surviving anywhere, seventy-three years before Gutenberg's press of c. 1450.

    c. 985–1450 CE

    The Thule cross the Arctic

    Alaska to Greenland

    Carrying the toggling harpoon, the skin-covered umiak and kayak, and dog traction, Thule whalers moved east from the Bering Strait from about 1200 and reached Greenland within about a century — re-dating in 2008 moved the start two centuries later than the c. 1000 once read from sea-mammal samples, which give radiocarbon ages that are too old. Ancient DNA shows they replaced rather than descended from the Dorset people already there, while the Norse colony Erik the Red founded in 985 held on until about 1450 and then failed, for reasons still argued over.

    c. 1000–1450 CE

    The Swahili Coast, Kilwa & Great Zimbabwe

    East & Southern Africa

    Monsoon dhows wove Africa into the Indian Ocean economy: coral-stone Kilwa (which Ibn Battuta ranked among the world's loveliest towns) traded gold and ivory for Chinese porcelain, while inland the granite enclosures of Great Zimbabwe rose on the gold routes. Ancient DNA from 80 people buried in six coastal towns (Brielle et al. 2023) shows African and Asian ancestries mixing from about 1000, as Islam spread — more than half of it from African women, most of the rest, before 1500, from Persian men — much as the Kilwa Chronicle's story of Shirazi founders claims.

    1010–1025 CE

    The Chola maritime empire

    South India & the Bay of Bengal

    Rajaraja I finished the Brihadeeswarar temple at Thanjavur in 1010 — a 66 m granite tower under a capstone of some 80 tonnes — and his son Rajendra sent a fleet across the Bay of Bengal in 1025 to raid Srivijaya, one of the very few premodern amphibious campaigns fought across open ocean; the same dynasty's inscriptions record village assemblies choosing their committees by drawing lots.

    c. 1021–1437 CE

    The Book of Optics & the observatory

    Cairo, Maragha & Samarkand

    Ibn al-Haytham's Kitab al-Manazir, written in Cairo, proved by experiment that we see because light enters the eye, and made the controlled test — not authority — the arbiter of a claim, the method Kepler and Newton inherited; the Ilkhanid observatory at Maragha (1259) built mathematical devices that reappear in Copernicus, and Ulugh Beg's at Samarkand catalogued 1,018 stars and measured the year to within about 25 seconds.

    c. 1024–1119 CE

    Song China's economic revolution

    Song dynasty China

    In a single century Song China issued the first government paper money, wrote down the first gunpowder formulas, and sent the magnetic compass to sea — with blast furnaces and million-strong cities unmatched anywhere on Earth.

    1088–1220 CE

    Universities & Gothic cathedrals

    Western Europe

    Bologna, Paris, and Oxford invented the self-governing degree-granting university around an Aristotle recovered from Arabic; pointed arches, ribbed vaults, and flying buttresses dissolved cathedral walls into towering glass and light.

    1095–1291 CE

    Crusade, counter-crusade & Ain Jalut

    Eastern Mediterranean

    Two centuries of holy war shifted little territory permanently but moved sugar, cotton, paper, and Arabic learning into Latin Europe; the Fourth Crusade's sack of Constantinople in 1204 crippled the very state it had set out to relieve, and in 1260 at Ain Jalut in Galilee the Mamluks — Turkic and Circassian boys bought as slaves, trained, freed, and raised into the ruling caste of Egypt — dealt the defeat that fixed the Mongol frontier at the Euphrates — Ilkhanid armies would return to Syria in 1281, 1299 (briefly taking Damascus) and 1303, but never hold it.

    c. 1113–1150 CE

    Angkor Wat & the Khmer hydraulic city

    Khmer Empire (Cambodia)

    Suryavarman II's temple-mountain is still the largest religious monument ever built, heart of a low-density urban sprawl of canals, paddies, and vast baray reservoirs covering some 1,000 km² — plausibly the most extensive preindustrial settlement on Earth, and on a 2021 model built from lidar, excavation, and radiocarbon dates home to some 700,000–900,000 people at its thirteenth-century peak.

    c. 1180–1200 CE

    Nan Madol rises from the reef

    Oceania (Pohnpei, Micronesia)

    Uranium–thorium dates on coral built into the site, with geochemical sourcing of its basalt, show columns from a volcanic plug on the far side of Pohnpei being set into the lagoon by c. 1180–1200 — the earliest firmly dated monument building in the Pacific islands (McCoy et al. 2016). Its 92 artificial islets of stacked basalt and coral, built without metal tools, draught animals, or the wheel, became the seat of the Saudeleur dynasty, which united the island until the 1500s by oral tradition, or until about 1628 on the archaeology.

    c. 1200 CE

    Polynesians and Native Americans meet

    Eastern Polynesia & the Pacific coast of South America

    Genomes of 807 people from 17 Pacific islands and 15 Native American coastal groups show a single contact, dated to around 1200, between eastern Polynesians and a people most closely related to today's Indigenous inhabitants of Colombia (Ioannidis et al. 2020); ancient genomes from Rapa Nui carry about a tenth Native American ancestry from a mixing dated 1250–1430 (Moreno-Mayar et al. 2024). It is one of the very few contacts between the hemispheres before 1492 that can be dated, and a likely route for the South American sweet potato into Polynesia — though who sailed to whom is inferred, not shown.

    1206–1279 CE

    Genghis Khan & the Mongol Empire

    Eurasia, Korea to Hungary

    From the steppe the Mongols forged the largest contiguous land empire in history — roughly 24 million km² — and, paradoxically, the Pax Mongolica that secured its roads for merchants, missionaries, technologies, and ultimately pathogens.

    1206–c. 1400 CE

    The Delhi Sultanate, Vijayanagara & the Kerala school

    South Asia

    Founded in 1206 by a former military slave and briefly ruled by Razia Sultana (1236–40), the Delhi Sultanate absorbed Persian administration and turned back repeated Mongol invasions; the rival southern capital at Vijayanagara, founded in 1336 on the Tungabhadra, grew into one of the largest cities on Earth; and in Kerala, Madhava of Sangamagrama's school derived infinite series for π and the sine some 250 years before Newton and Leibniz.

    c. 1220–1300 CE

    Mapungubwe, the hilltop kingdom

    Southern Africa (the Limpopo valley)

    Around 1220 the rulers of the middle Limpopo moved up onto Mapungubwe Hill, leaving commoners on the plain below — long regarded as the first capital in southern Africa to set rulers physically above the ruled — and a town of perhaps 5,000 sent gold and ivory to the coast for glass beads from across the Indian Ocean, with Chinese celadon reaching the royal quarter. Royal graves opened in the 1930s held gold-foil regalia, among them the famous rhinoceros; when the valley emptied around 1300, Great Zimbabwe took up the succession.

    c. 1250–1300 CE

    Polynesians settle Aotearoa

    Oceania / the Pacific

    Steering by stars, swells, and birds without instruments, Polynesians reached Hawaiʻi (c. 1000–1200), Rapa Nui (c. 1200), and finally New Zealand around 1250 — the last great habitable landmass humans ever settled.

    c. 1270 CE

    Caguana and the Taíno chiefdoms

    The Caribbean (Puerto Rico & Hispaniola)

    In the mountains of Utuado the Taíno laid out about a dozen stone-lined ball courts and plazas, their slabs carved with the largest concentration of petroglyphs in the Antilles — the ceremonial face of the cacicazgos, the chiefdoms Columbus would meet in 1492. Ancient DNA from 174 people across the islands (Fernandes et al. 2021) shows a close-knit population descended from Arawak-speaking migrants out of north-eastern South America, and implies that Puerto Rico and Hispaniola together held tens of thousands of people at most, not the hundreds of thousands of older estimates — a figure still much debated.

    1324 CE

    Mansa Musa's hajj & golden Timbuktu

    Mali Empire, West Africa

    Mali's emperor gave away so much gold on his pilgrimage that Cairo's markets were said to slump for years; the 1375 Catalan Atlas drew him enthroned with a nugget, while Timbuktu's mosques and Sankore scholars made the Niger bend a capital of Islamic learning.

    c. 1325 CE

    Tenochtitlan founded on Lake Texcoco

    Valley of Mexico

    The Mexica raised an island capital of causeways, aqueducts, and chinampa raised-field gardens that by 1500 ranked among the largest cities on Earth, rivalling anything in Europe — built in complete isolation from the Old World.

    1338–1339 CE

    The pestilence graves of Issyk-Kul

    Central Asia (Kyrgyzstan)

    Syriac-inscribed tombstones in two Christian cemeteries near Lake Issyk-Kul record a spike of deaths from pestilence in 1338–39; in 2022 Maria Spyrou, Philip Slavin, Johannes Krause and colleagues sampled seven of the dead, found Yersinia pestis DNA in three of them, and the two genomes they rebuilt sit at the branching point from which the Black Death's lineages radiate. The second pandemic now has a place and a date eight years before Kaffa, in the heart of the Mongol trading world, near the Tian Shan, where the closest living plague strains still circulate in wild rodents.

    1347–1351 CE

    The Black Death

    Central Asia to the Atlantic

    Yersinia pestis rode the era's own trade arteries from Central Asia to Kaffa to Messina in 1347, killing an estimated 30–60% in the best-recorded parts of Europe (pollen cores show the toll was patchy, catastrophic in some regions and light in others) and perhaps a third of the Middle East and North Africa — loosening serfdom, raising wages, and closing the medieval order.

    1370–1405 CE

    Timur, and the last steppe conquest

    Transoxiana to Delhi & Anatolia

    Claiming Genghisid legitimacy he could not inherit, Timur (Tamerlane) sacked Delhi in 1398 and shattered the Ottomans at Ankara in 1402 — a defeat that captured Sultan Bayezid and bought Constantinople another fifty years — while hauling the captured artisans of half of Asia back to rebuild Samarkand; he died in 1405 marching on Ming China, and the empire he left behind spent its energy on Ulugh Beg's observatory instead. He is the last steppe conqueror on the Genghisid scale — though mounted archers would still capture a Ming emperor at Tumu in 1449 and burn Moscow in 1571; only slowly did gunpowder fortification and firearms erode their advantage.

    Why it happened

    Weather, contagion, and the roads between

    Almost nothing in this millennium moved without a road, and nothing on the road mattered more than what nobody chose to carry. Two climate swings opened and then closed the era's frontiers; two pandemics reset its demography; and the societies that came through were not the ones that won battles but the ones whose institutions could absorb a shock.

    The chapter opens in the cold. Sulfate layers in Greenland and Antarctic ice date volcanic eruptions to 536, 540, and 547, and the narrowest tree rings of the last two thousand years follow immediately; Procopius wrote that the sun gave its light without brightness, and Chinese chroniclers recorded yellow dust falling like snow. The cooling that followed — the Late Antique Little Ice Age, roughly 536 to 660 — sits underneath the whole first century of this chapter. Four hundred years later the swing reversed. The Medieval Climate Anomaly of about 950–1250 brought warmer North Atlantic summers and reliable Southwest rainfall, and almost every frontier this chapter celebrates opened inside that window: Iceland in 874, Norse Greenland in 985, the Chacoan great houses, Cahokia around 1050, the Thule push east across the Arctic, and the last long Polynesian voyages to Hawaiʻi, Rapa Nui, and Aotearoa. When the window shut, they shut with it. The Colorado Plateau emptied during the 1276–1299 megadrought, Cahokia was abandoned by about 1350, the Greenland Norse were gone by roughly 1450, and northern Europe lost perhaps a tenth of its people to the rain-drowned harvests of the Great Famine of 1315–17. What closed the window may have been volcanic. In 1257 Samalas, on the Indonesian island of Lombok, threw out at least 40 km³ of rock in one of the largest eruptions of the last ten thousand years — identified only in 2013, when glass in its pumice was matched to shards in both Arctic and Antarctic ice — and ice-cap and lake records show cold summers and spreading ice setting in abruptly between 1275 and 1300, after a further run of eruptions. That these eruptions tipped the North Atlantic into the Little Ice Age is a leading hypothesis, not a settled one. The tour's climate spine runs beneath all of it.

    Population is the other spine. Depending on whose reconstruction you follow, the world held some 190–250 million people in 500 CE and 250–320 million by 1000; by 1300 it had reached perhaps 360–460 million — and then it fell, by a few per cent in some reconstructions and by more than a tenth in others, the one sustained global decline of the millennium on which every reconstruction agrees. The largest single engine of the rise stood in China, where early-ripening Champa rice, from what is now central Vietnam — already grown in Fujian, and distributed to the lower Yangzi by imperial order in 1012 — allowed two harvests a year from one paddy and helped push Song population past 100 million: about a third of humanity inside one state, transacting with the world's first government paper money. The long shape is plotted in the population series.

    Disease did the falling. The first plague pandemic, from 541, is genomically confirmed but demographically disputed — the chroniclers describe catastrophe, while papyri, coin finds, pollen cores, and burial counts have been read to argue for something far milder. Between the two plagues, smallpox showed what a crowd disease could do to an island population with little immunity: entering Japan at Dazaifu in 735, it killed perhaps a quarter to a third of the country within three years, on the evidence of provincial fiscal reports, and the court's tax base with it. The second plague pandemic is not in doubt, and since 2022 it has a birthplace: tombstones by Lake Issyk-Kul in Kyrgyzstan record deaths from pestilence in 1338–39, and plague genomes from those graves sit at the root of every Black Death lineage. English manorial court rolls, wills, and the poll tax of 1377 let historians count rather than guess, and 30–60% mortality in the best-recorded parts of Europe in 1347–51 rests on documents — but pollen from 261 radiocarbon-dated sites in 19 countries shows fields abandoned to scrub in some regions and farmed on almost undisturbed in others, so no single figure fits the continent. Egypt and Syria lost something like a third. What followed was not only grief but a change in the price of a person. Labour turned scarce, English wages roughly doubled within a generation, and landlords legislated against the rise and failed. West of the Elbe serfdom dissolved; east of it, landlords eventually answered by binding peasants harder — the "second serfdom", which crystallised only in the late fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Same scarcity, opposite institutions over the following two centuries — the clearest evidence this chapter offers that demography sets the problem and politics chooses the answer. Institutions could also learn: in 1377 Ragusa (Dubrovnik) made arrivals from plague-struck places wait thirty days before entering the city, and when Venice stretched the wait to forty in 1448 the practice took its name — quarantine.

    Ecology set limits that engineering could only postpone. Angkor's canals and baray fed a region of perhaps 700,000–900,000 people at its thirteenth-century peak, but tree rings from southern Vietnam record decades-long droughts in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries broken by monsoons violent enough to wreck its water works, and sediment cores from the moat of Angkor Thom show burning, clearance, and erosion falling away from the early 1300s and the moat itself unmaintained by 1400 — a drift of people and power away from the capital, not the single catastrophe of the Ayutthayan sack of 1431. Cahokia's waste tells a parallel story: faecal stanols, molecules made in the human gut and washed into Horseshoe Lake, track its population, and they fall after about 1150, when Mississippi floods and warm-season droughts struck together.

    Coercion was structural in this world, not incidental to it. The trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean trades moved millions of enslaved people across the era; the baqt sent captives north out of Nubia annually for centuries; and in 869 enslaved East Africans draining the salt marshes of southern Iraq — the Zanj — rose and held Basra's hinterland for fourteen years. Nor was unfreedom confined to the great routes: Domesday Book, William the Conqueror's survey of England in 1086, lists some 28,000 slaves, roughly a tenth of the people it counts, and the temples, manors, and irrigation states of the age ran largely on labour owed rather than sold — the temple servants listed in Angkor's inscriptions, the Andean mit'a, the serf's week-work on the lord's demesne. Some slave systems inverted entirely: mamluks bought as boys became the sultans of Egypt, and Shajar al-Durr, herself once enslaved, ruled it outright in 1250. Women hold power and authorship across the era wherever the sources allow us to see it — Wu Zetian, the only woman to reign as emperor of China; Theodora at Constantinople; Murasaki Shikibu and Sei Shonagon at Heian; Hildegard of Bingen in the Rhineland; Razia Sultana on the Delhi throne — and the Inca aqllakuna, women taken from their communities to weave and brew for the state, show power and coercion housed in the same institution. Where no one wrote women down, their bones now speak. At Pueblo Bonito nine elite burials spanning some 330 years share one mitochondrial genome, a ruling line traced through mothers; on the Swahili coast, ancient DNA shows the stone towns' lineages forming from about 1000 out of African mothers and, mostly, Persian fathers — marriage, not only trade, built the Indian Ocean world.     Competing explanations

    • How lethal was the first plague pandemic? The written sources describe a world emptied; a recent reading of papyri, coinage, pollen, and burials argues the demographic dent was modest. Genuinely unresolved — the contested-claims register holds the site's other open cases.
    • Where did the Black Death begin, and how did it reach Europe? The 2022 Issyk-Kul genomes put the strain's last common ancestor in Central Asia by 1338, but not how long it had lurked in rodent reservoirs or how it travelled west. The famous story of plague corpses catapulted into Kaffa rests on one Italian writer who was not there; Hannah Barker (2021) argues instead that embargoes halted the Black Sea grain trade in 1346 and that plague resumed its spread with the grain ships, rats and fleas aboard, from Tana as well as Kaffa, once trade reopened in 1347.
    • Did the Black Death end serfdom, or merely finish it? Post-plague wage rises in England are well attested; whether the pandemic caused western serfdom's collapse or accelerated a decline already under way is argued, and the second serfdom east of the Elbe, which hardened a century and more later, shows the same scarcity of labour ending in the opposite institution.
    • What emptied Cahokia? Faecal-stanol and flood records tie its decline to Mississippi floods and summer droughts around 1150 (White et al. 2019); a 2024 study of soil carbon isotopes found no sign that drought turned its farmland to prairie; others stress politics and violence — a palisade first thrown round the central precinct around 1175 and rebuilt several times. Estimates of its peak population run from about 6,000 to 40,000.
    • When did the Inca empire begin? The date 1438 comes from Spanish-recorded king-lists. Radiocarbon puts Machu Picchu, supposedly built for Pachacuti after that date, in use by c. 1420 (Burger et al. 2021), and Bayesian models of radiocarbon dates place the Inca in Argentina's Mendoza region somewhere between c. 1350 and 1440 (Marsh et al. 2017). Either the king-list is compressed or the dates need another reading.
    • Did Angkor fall, or drift? The 1431 Ayutthayan sack is the textbook ending; tree-ring droughts and flood damage (Buckley et al. 2010) and moat sediments showing a slow fall-off from the early 1300s (Penny et al. 2019) point to a long relocation of people and power, with climate as one pressure among several.
    • Why did Song China not industrialise? It had coke-smelted iron, water-powered spinning, paper money, and hundred-million-person markets. Explanations split between resource geography (coal far from the delta cities), a high-level equilibrium in which cheap labour made machines unattractive, and the political rupture of the Mongol and Ming centuries.
    • What ended Norse Greenland? Cooling, soil erosion, the collapse of the walrus-ivory market once elephant ivory returned to Europe, conflict with the Thule, or simple emigration — the settlements look abandoned rather than starved, and the case is open.
    • Why did the treasure fleets stop? Court factionalism and the cost of the northern frontier are better documented than any grand decision to "turn inward" — private Chinese maritime trade went on growing after 1433.

    When the sea lanes reopened after the plague, the initiative had moved to the ocean's edge. The next chapter belongs to the ships: Iberian caravels working down the African coast, Ming junks already withdrawn from the Indian Ocean, and an Atlantic crossing that would join two hemispheres which had never once met.

    Meanwhile, everywhere

    Sixteen regions, one millennium

    No region waited on another. Between 500 and 1450 every inhabited part of the planet was building, trading, and reorganising on its own terms; where the record is thin, the card says so.

    West Asia

    622–1402

    Muhammad's hijra of 622 began a new calendar; within a century the caliphs ruled from Iberia to the Indus, and from 762 they ruled from the round city of Baghdad, whose House of Wisdom put Greek, Persian, and Sanskrit science into Arabic. Seljuk Turks broke Byzantium's army at Manzikert in 1071, Crusader states held the Levant coast from 1099 to 1291, and the Mongols sacked Baghdad in 1258. From about 1300 a small Anatolian emirate, the Ottomans, grew fast enough to cross into Europe in 1354 and to survive Timur's rout at Ankara in 1402.

    North Africa & the Nile

    641–c. 1400

    Arab armies took Egypt in 641. By tradition a merchant's daughter, Fatima al-Fihri, founded the mosque of al-Qarawiyyin at Fez in 857–859, later a great centre of learning. The Fatimids built Cairo beside Fustat in 969, and under the Mamluks it became perhaps the largest city of the Islamic world. The Almoravids, who founded Marrakesh c. 1070, and then the Almohads ruled from the Sahara to al-Andalus, while up the Nile Christian Makuria, shielded by the baqt, painted its cathedral at Faras — murals rescued in 1960–64, before Lake Nasser drowned the site.

    West & Central Africa

    c. 800–1400

    Djenné-Djenno (Chapter V's Jenne-jeno) on the inland Niger delta peaked around 800 without palace or citadel, and in 1068 al-Bakri described Ghana's capital as two towns, one of them Muslim with twelve mosques. In the forest to the south, Igbo-Ukwu's smiths were casting bronzes from the ninth century, and Ife made glass beads traded as far as Mali and cast naturalistic copper-alloy heads of its rulers, probably in the 1300s–1400s. Kanem, near Lake Chad, had Muslim kings by the late 1000s; Mali rose after c. 1235; and by tradition around 1390 a marriage alliance founded the kingdom of Kongo.

    East & Southern Africa

    c. 700–1450

    In the Ethiopian highlands the Zagwe kings cut Lalibela's churches into the rock around 1200, and the Solomonic dynasty took the throne in 1270. Swahili towns of coral stone from Mogadishu to Kilwa minted their own coins, and ancient DNA shows their lineages forming from about 1000 out of African mothers and, mostly, Persian fathers. Inland, Mapungubwe's hilltop rulers (c. 1220–1300) gave way to Great Zimbabwe, whose Great Enclosure rose in the 1200s–1300s over a town of some thousands (no more than 10,000 on a 2017 re-estimate, though older figures ran to 18,000); and Austronesian settlers, whose language came from southern Borneo, were farming Madagascar by c. 700–1000.

    Europe & the Mediterranean

    537–1450

    Byzantium held Constantinople and Justinian's Hagia Sophia through the millennium, save the crusaders' Latin Empire of 1204–61, while in the west Charlemagne was crowned emperor in 800. Norse sailors raided and traded from Lindisfarne (793) to the Volga, settled Iceland from about 870 — its Althing first met in 930 — and the Rus' of Kiev took Byzantine Christianity in 988. Córdoba became a caliphal capital in 929; universities and Gothic cathedrals spread from the 1100s; and the Black Death of 1347–51 killed perhaps a third to a half of Europe's people, very unevenly.

    Central Asia & the Steppe

    552–1449

    The first Türk khaganate, founded in 552, ruled the steppe from Manchuria to the Black Sea; its successors' Orkhon stelae (732–735) are the oldest substantial Turkic writing, and Sogdian merchants of Samarkand ran the caravans to China. The Uyghurs made Manichaeism their state religion in the 760s, and Tibetan troops held Chang'an for fifteen days in 763. Genghis Khan's Mongols united the steppe in 1206 and wrecked Khwarazm's cities in 1219–21; Timur rebuilt Samarkand with captured craftsmen, and his grandson Ulugh Beg built its observatory in the 1420s.

    South Asia

    606–c. 1425

    Harsha ruled the north from Kannauj (606–647) while the pilgrim Xuanzang studied at Nalanda; under the Pala kings (750–1161), Nalanda and Vikramashila trained the teachers who took Buddhism to Tibet. The Rashtrakuta king Krishna I had the Kailasa temple at Ellora carved downward out of a basalt cliff (c. 756–773), and the Cholas sent fleets across the Bay of Bengal. Mahmud of Ghazni sacked Somnath in 1025–26, the Delhi Sultanate followed in 1206, Vijayanagara was founded in 1336, and in Kerala Madhava's school was deriving infinite series by about 1400.

    East Asia

    589–1443

    The Sui reunited China in 589 and dug the Grand Canal. After the An Lushan rebellion of 755–763, the Tang registers fell from 53 million people to 17 million — a collapse of the tax rolls as much as of lives. The Song (960–1279) ran on examinations, paper money, and Champa rice; the Mongol Yuan ruled from 1271 until the Ming drove them out in 1368. Silla unified most of Korea in 668, and Joseon's King Sejong had the Hangul alphabet devised in 1443; Japan built Tang-style capitals at Nara and Heian, and its Kamakura warrior government (1185) outlasted two Mongol invasions.

    Southeast Asia

    682–c. 1400

    Srivijaya, first named in a Palembang inscription of 682, controlled the Straits for some three centuries; Sailendra Java raised Borobudur around 800; and Angkor's kings, from 802, built one of the largest settlement complexes of the pre-industrial world. Dai Viet won independence at Bach Dang in 938, Bagan covered the Irrawaddy plain with temples, and the Laguna Copperplate of 900 shows Luzon's polities keeping records in Old Malay. From 1238 Tai kingdoms rose at Sukhothai and then Ayutthaya, Majapahit (1293) ruled from eastern Java, and Melaka, founded c. 1400, became the new hinge of the Straits.

    Australia

    c. 500–1450

    Few sites from these centuries are closely dated, but the record shows continuity, not stasis. At Mount William, in Wurundjeri country, greenstone was dug from what are now 268 quarry pits, some several metres deep, and flaked on at least 34 working floors; its axes, and engraved pearl shell from the Kimberley coast travelled hundreds to thousands of kilometres along ceremonial routes; and country was managed with deliberate burning. Five Kilwa sultanate coins, found on Marchinbar Island off Arnhem Land in 1944 beside Dutch coins dated 1690 to the 1780s, hint at an Indian Ocean contact — but no one knows when or how they arrived.

    Oceania & the Pacific

    c. 1025–1450

    Radiocarbon on short-lived samples puts the first settlers in the Society Islands c. 1025–1120 and on Hawaiʻi, Rapa Nui, and Aotearoa in a rapid pulse c. 1190–1290 (Wilmshurst et al. 2011), though some Hawaiian dates run earlier; Rapa Nui's moai were carved c. 1250–1500. In Micronesia, Nan Madol's basalt islets, later the Saudeleur seat on Pohnpei, were rising from c. 1180; in Tonga the Tuʻi Tonga line built a seagoing chiefdom whose Haʻamonga ʻa Maui trilithon dates to the 1200s. Genomes show eastern Polynesians meeting a Native American people around 1200.

    The Arctic

    c. 890–1408

    From the far north of Norway, the chieftain Ohthere told King Alfred's court c. 890 of his reindeer and of the tribute the Sámi paid him in furs and hides. In the Canadian Arctic the Dorset (Tuniit) hunted seals at breathing holes into the 1300s, when Thule whalers moving east from Alaska after c. 1200 replaced them. Norse Greenlanders farmed two fjord settlements from 985, shipped walrus ivory to Europe, and left a runestone of c. 1250–1333 near Upernavik; their last written record is a wedding at Hvalsey church in September 1408.

    North America

    c. 850–1450

    Around 900, productive local maize spread across the Eastern Woodlands, displacing the old seed crops of the Eastern Agricultural Complex and feeding the Mississippian world of Cahokia, refounded c. 1050, and palisaded Moundville in Alabama (c. 1000–1450). In the Southwest the Hohokam watered the Phoenix basin with the most elaborate canal network in the pre-contact Americas, Chaco's great houses rose c. 850–1150, and Mesa Verde's cliff dwellings were built and left within a century after c. 1200. Norse visitors felled wood at L'Anse aux Meadows, Newfoundland, in 1021.

    Mesoamerica

    c. 600–1441

    The Classic Maya kingdoms of Tikal and Calakmul fragmented after c. 800 — Tikal's last dated monument is from 869 — while Chichén Itzá flourished until about 1100, and walled Mayapán, a city of perhaps 15,000–17,000, dominated Yucatán from the 1220s until its sack c. 1441. In Oaxaca the Mixtec lord Eight Deer Jaguar Claw (1063–1115) fought and married his way to power, a life painted in screenfold codices centuries later. Tula dominated central Mexico c. 900–1150; the Mexica founded Tenochtitlan c. 1325 and joined the Triple Alliance of 1428.

    The Caribbean

    c. 500–1450

    Canoe-borne settlers from Hispaniola or Cuba reached the Bahamas between 500 and 800 and spread island by island to become the Lucayans. By about 1200 the Taíno of Hispaniola, Puerto Rico, and eastern Cuba lived in chiefdoms ruled by caciques, grew cassava in mounded fields, and laid out stone-lined ball courts — Caguana, c. 1270, has about a dozen. In the Lesser Antilles, Cayo pottery from c. 1250 is linked to the Kalinago. Ancient DNA suggests Hispaniola and Puerto Rico held tens of thousands of people, not the hundreds of thousands or millions once claimed — a figure still argued.

    South America

    c. 500–1450

    Tiwanaku and Wari shared the highlands c. 600–1000; on the north coast the Sicán lords of Batán Grande (c. 750–1375) smelted arsenical copper on a large scale, and Chimor's Chan Chan grew from c. 900 into the largest adobe city ever built. East of the Andes the Casarabe culture of Bolivia's Llanos de Mojos (c. 500–1400) raised platform towns joined by causeways, mound-builders flourished on Marajó island at the Amazon's mouth (c. 400–1400), and the Tairona founded Ciudad Perdida around 800. From Cusco the Inca began to expand in the early 1400s — traditionally from 1438.

    Ordinary lives

    Four lives kept by accident

    Most people in this millennium farmed, paid, and left no trace. These four are known because something kept them by accident: volcanic ash, a register reused as scrap paper, a synagogue storeroom, and waterlogged clay.

    Joya de Cerén, El Salvador · c. 600 CE

    A village that ran from the ash

    One evening around 600, the families of a Maya farming village of perhaps 200 people left in a hurry — hearths still lit, digging tools stacked by the houses, sleeping mats not yet unrolled — as the nearby Loma Caldera vent buried everything under metres of ash within hours. No bodies have been found.

    What they left is a working village: wattle-and-daub houses with storerooms and kitchens holding some 70 pots per household, a sweat bath, a building for communal feasts and another perhaps used by a diviner, and maize fields, kitchen gardens, and a field of manioc beside the houses. The villagers made their own grinding stones, agave cord, and pottery, and obtained obsidian blades, jade, and fine painted ware from Copán through markets beyond the village.

    How we know Excavation, led by Payson Sheets, after a bulldozer driver uncovered the site in 1976; plaster poured into hollows left by rotted plants in the ash — ripe maize and stored beans put the eruption in August or September.

    Hanyū village, Mino province, Japan · 702 CE

    Every name in a village, free and unfree

    In 702 the officials of Mino province wrote down every household of Hanyū village for the capital: each person by name and age, three to a line, the free listed before the unfree and men before women. Households were graded by their number of adult men and by their wealth, and tied into groups of five that answered together for taxes and for anyone who ran away. The register counted 1,119 people, slaves among them, and existed so that the state could allot rice land and levy tax, labour, and soldiers.

    It was meant to be scrapped after thirty years. Instead, in 748, clerks at the sutra-copying office of Tōdai-ji turned the sheets over and used the backs for the accounts of a project to copy the Lotus Sutra a thousand times — which is why the register survives, almost entire, among the Shōsōin papers at Nara.

    How we know The register itself, now a designated Important Cultural Property; pit-houses of the sixth to eighth centuries, excavated in 1977 at Tomika in Gifu, may be the village it describes.

    Mangalore, Malabar coast · 1132 CE

    Ashu, freed; Bomma, sent to sea

    On 17 October 1132, in the trading port of Mangalore, the Tunisian-born Jewish merchant Abraham Ben Yiju freed an enslaved woman named Ashu, and a second document confirmed it. By 1135 he had a son, and the historian S. D. Goitein inferred that Ashu had become his wife; the letters mention a brother-in-law called Nair, a hint that her family belonged to the Nair community of the coast.

    Another man Ben Yiju held — his name survives only as the Hebrew letters B-M-T, which the writer Amitav Ghosh reads as the Tulu name Bomma — sailed to Aden as his agent in 1135, and appears to have followed him to Egypt, where Ben Yiju's accounts record money owed to him. Neither Ashu nor Bomma left a word of their own; we see them only through their owner's papers.

    How we know The Cairo Geniza: deeds and letters that ended up in a Fustat synagogue storeroom, where writing that might bear the name of God was set aside rather than destroyed — read by Goitein and retold in Ghosh's In an Antique Land (1992).

    Novgorod, northern Rus' · c. 1240–1260 CE

    Onfim, about seven, at his letters

    A boy named Onfim, probably six or seven years old, practised writing on strips of birch bark: the alphabet in rows, syllables, snatches of psalms, and a prayer — "Lord, help your servant Onfim." In the margins he drew what was on his mind: himself on horseback spearing an enemy, his name written beside the rider; a four-legged beast captioned "I am a wild beast"; a greeting to a friend, Danilo. His stick figures have anything from three to eight fingers.

    Seventeen of his pieces survive. They come from a town where writing was not confined to clergy and nobles: Novgorod's birch-bark letters show it in use across social classes, by women as well as men.

    How we know Birch bark survives in Novgorod's waterlogged clay: more than 1,100 inscribed pieces have been dug up since 1951, and Onfim's are dated by the layer they lay in, conventionally 1240–1260.

    Seen whole

    Seven ways to hold a thousand years

    How we know

    • Ancient DNA from teeth. Yersinia pestis genomes recovered from the East Smithfield plague cemetery in London (1348–50) and from a grave at Aschheim in Bavaria (c. 541) ended a long argument over whether these pandemics were plague at all — and showed the two were caused by related but distinct lineages.
    • Tree rings and ice cores. Sulfate layers in Greenland and Antarctic ice date the eruptions of 536, 540, and 547; Alpine and Altai larch rings measure the cooling that followed. The same method, built by A. E. Douglass in the 1920s, dates Chacoan roof beams and the 1276–1299 Southwest megadrought to the calendar year.
    • Airborne lidar. Surveys led by Damian Evans in 2012 and 2015 stripped the forest canopy off Angkor and Phnom Kulen in software and revealed a planned grid of streets, ponds, and canals where the maps had shown only jungle around the temples. In 2021 a model combining those maps with excavation and radiocarbon dates put Greater Angkor at 700,000–900,000 people in the thirteenth century — an estimate built on assumptions about household size, not a count.
    • Manorial and fiscal records. English court rolls, wills, and the poll tax of 1377 turn plague mortality and post-plague wages into countable series rather than impressions; Egyptian waqf and land documents do similar work for the Mamluk lands.
    • Travellers who wrote it down. Xuanzang in India (629–645), Ibn Fadlan among the Volga Rus (922), al-Bakri on Ghana (1068), Ibn Battuta over some 120,000 km (1325–54), and Rabban Bar Sauma travelling the other way, from Beijing to Bordeaux (1287).
    • The Cairo Geniza. Roughly 400,000 discarded pages from a Fustat synagogue storeroom — invoices, letters, marriage contracts — let the Indian Ocean trade be reconstructed from the merchant's desk upward rather than the chronicler's palace downward.
    • Shipwrecks. The Belitung wreck (c. 830), an Arabian-built dhow lost off Sumatra with some 60,000 Chinese bowls aboard, is a monsoon cargo manifest preserved intact.
    • Genomes as family trees. Ancient DNA now reads kinship and ancestry where no one wrote it down: the Pueblo Bonito matriline, the African mothers and Persian fathers of the Swahili towns, the Arawak-speaking ancestry of the Taíno. Modern genomes date the Polynesian–Native American contact to c. 1200 from the lengths of the Native American DNA segments Polynesians still carry. The limits are real: a few elite burials may not speak for a society, admixture dates carry error bars of generations, and the Caribbean population figure is an effective size that has to be scaled up, by assumption, to a head count.
    • Syriac tombstones and plague genomes together. The Issyk-Kul graves pair dated inscriptions naming the cause of death with bacterial DNA from the same people — which is why the Black Death's source can be placed at 1338–39 rather than inferred. What the genomes cannot show is the route the strain took west, or how long it had been circulating before.
    • Radiocarbon, modelled. Single dates are fuzzy; Bayesian models combine many dates with the order of layers in the ground. That is how Inca sites in Ecuador and Argentina, and 2021 AMS dates from Machu Picchu's burials, came to challenge the king-list chronology that starts the empire in 1438. Sea creatures give radiocarbon ages that read too old, which is why the Thule migration once seemed to start near 1000 and now starts near 1200.
    • Uranium–thorium on coral. At Nan Madol, coral built into the islets dates by the decay of uranium in its skeleton, far more tightly than radiocarbon can, and places monument building at c. 1180–1200. The date is when the coral died, so it marks the earliest the stone could have been laid around it.
    • Pollen as a mortality proxy. Cereal and weed pollen in 261 dated lake and bog cores across Europe show where farming collapsed after 1347 and where it carried on, testing the documentary 30–60% region by region. It measures abandoned fields, not deaths, so it confirms the pattern better than the percentage.
    • Tephra and sulfate. The chemistry of volcanic glass in Arctic and Antarctic ice matched the Samalas pumice on Lombok, turning an anonymous 1250s sulfate spike into a dated eruption. How much it cooled the world is modelled from the sulfate, not measured.
    • Metal and glass fingerprints. Lead isotopes tie most of Igbo-Ukwu's copper to ores in south-eastern Nigeria, and the composition of its glass beads to Egyptian and Near Eastern furnaces. An isotope match can rule a source out more securely than it can prove one in.
    • Inscriptions, fiscal reports, and one witness. The Orkhon stelae, the Laguna Copperplate and Japan's provincial tax reports of the 730s are documents made at the time; Ohthere's account of the Sámi survives only because it was written into the Old English translation of Orosius made in Alfred's England, and nothing corroborates it line by line.
    • The methods themselves are set out in How We Know. The claims on this page that remain genuinely open — the lethality of the first plague pandemic, Cahokia's population, the end of Norse Greenland, the start of the Inca empire — are argued in the competing explanations above; the contested-claims register holds the site's other open cases.

    In the hand

    Objects from a connected world

    Diamond Sutra scroll

    868 CE · Dunhuang

    A printed Buddhist scroll from a sealed cave library — the world's oldest dated printed book, six centuries before Gutenberg.

    Brass astrolabe

    ~10th c. · Islamic world

    A hand-held star-computer for prayer times, latitude, and the heavens — perfected in Baghdad and Isfahan, then carried into Europe.

    Double-hulled waka

    ~1250 CE · Polynesia

    A voyaging canoe that crossed thousands of kilometres of open Pacific by star, swell, and bird — no chart, no compass, no instruments.

    Roped pot on a stand

    c. 9th–12th c. · Igbo-Ukwu, Nigeria

    A leaded-bronze vessel cast by lost wax, the knotted rope that cradles it rendered in metal too — excavated by Thurstan Shaw at Igbo-Ukwu, from a copper alloy whose ores lie in south-eastern Nigeria.

    Timbuktu manuscript

    from the late 13th c. · Timbuktu

    Timbuktu's family libraries hold books on law, astronomy, and medicine dating from the late thirteenth century to the twentieth — most copied after this chapter ends, but the scholarship took root under Mali.

    Laguna Copperplate

    900 CE · Luzon, Philippines

    A thin copper sheet inscribed in Old Malay, in Kawi script, releasing a family from a debt in gold — the oldest dated document from the Philippines, found in river sand in 1987.

    Maya Long Count stela

    Classic period · Mesoamerica

    A carved monument dating events to the day across millions of years — inscribed with a positional zero invented entirely in the Americas.

    A page of the Jikji

    1377 CE · Cheongju, Korea

    A Buddhist anthology set in cast metal sorts at Heungdeok temple — the oldest surviving book printed from movable metal type, seventy-three years before Gutenberg.

    Gold rhinoceros

    c. 1220–1300 · Mapungubwe

    Gold foil hammered over a carved wooden core, small enough to stand in a palm — found in a royal grave on Mapungubwe Hill, gold that elsewhere left for the coast as trade here buried with the elite.

    Toggling harpoon head

    ~1200 CE · Thule Arctic

    Antler and ground slate, cut to turn sideways under the skin so a struck whale cannot shake it — the single tool that let people cross from Alaska to Greenland.

    Engraved pearl shell

    Kimberley coast · Aboriginal Australia

    Incised with interlocking key designs and hung on hair string, coastal riji shell passed hand to hand along ceremonial routes until it reached desert country 2,000 km inland.

    Taíno duho

    c. 1200–1500 · Greater Antilles

    A cacique's carved seat of wood or stone — literally a seat of power, used in the cohoba ceremonies through which chiefs consulted their ancestral zemís.
