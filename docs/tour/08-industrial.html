    Chapter VIII  ·  1750 – 1945 CE

    The Industrial
    & Modern Age

    1750 – 1945 CE · eight generations that remade the world

    In under two centuries humanity harnessed fossil energy and rights-of-man revolutions — closing with the deadliest wars ever fought and a weapon that put civilization's survival in human hands.

    Scroll to begin in 1750

    The chapter before us

    The previous chapter left the world stitched together but nowhere near levelled. In 1750 most of the planet's manufacturing still came out of Asia — Qing China and Mughal India between them made something close to half of everything made on earth — and Europe's edge lay less in its workshops than in its ships, its chartered companies, and the Atlantic system of silver, sugar, and enslaved labour it had spent three centuries building. What carried forward into this chapter were those inheritances: American crops feeding Eurasian villages, print and Newtonian mechanics loose in the culture, the slave trade at its ghastly peak, and the habit of moving goods, people, and ideas across oceans as routine. What was new was the fuel.

    In under two centuries, humanity harnessed fossil energy, toppled kings and empires, and learned to speak across oceans in seconds — a faster transformation than in the previous ten millennia combined. Coal and steam broke the ancient ceiling set by muscle, wind, and water.

    Enlightenment ideas of reason and rights ignited revolutions from Philadelphia to Paris to Port-au-Prince to Caracas, while the new mills pulled millions out of the fields and into factory cities. The same current carried the century's great emancipations — the abolition of Atlantic slavery, the first victories for women's suffrage, and, in the laboratory, germ theory and Darwin's account of life itself — each widening, unevenly and against fierce resistance, the circle of who counted as fully human.

    But the same forces cut both ways. Railways and telegraphs knit the planet together even as industrialized empires carved up Africa and Asia; Japan's Meiji reformers and Ethiopia's army at Adwa proved the new power was not the property of Europe alone. The age closes with the two most destructive wars ever fought and a weapon — first tested in a New Mexico desert in the summer of 1945 — that placed the survival of civilization itself in human hands.

    01 · Sapere aude

    Dare to know

    The age opens not with a machine but with an idea. Diderot and d'Alembert's Encyclopédie gathered the knowledge of the world into twenty-eight volumes of text and plates (thirty-five with the later supplements and index) and made reason, evidence, and natural rights a public inheritance — the operating manual for everything that follows.

    02 · Buried sunlight

    The coal seam

    Beneath Britain lay carbon laid down more than three hundred million years earlier. Burning it released energy no waterwheel or team of oxen could match — the first time a society tapped a stock of ancient sunlight rather than the daily flow of the sun.

    03 · The tireless muscle

    Watt's engine

    James Watt's separate condenser (1769) turned the steam engine from a clanking mine-pump into an efficient, universal prime mover. Rotary power could now be set down anywhere — beside a coalfield, not just a river — and run day and night without tiring.

    04 · The factory city

    A world of cities

    Coal-fed mills pulled millions from the countryside into smoke-blackened boomtowns. Manchester multiplied roughly tenfold in a single lifetime, and for the first time in history whole societies began to live mostly in cities.

    1B

    humans alive worldwide, first past one billion around 1804 — then doubling to two billion by 1927, an acceleration with no precedent in the whole of the past.

    05 · The Rocket

    Annihilating distance

    When Stephenson's Rocket opened the Liverpool–Manchester line in 1830, journeys that had taken days collapsed into hours. Iron rails, steamships, and — soon — the telegraph began to shrink the whole planet to a fraction of its former size.

    In overthrowing me, you have cut down only the trunk of the tree of liberty — it will spring back from the roots, for they are numerous and deep.

    — Toussaint Louverture, on his arrest by French forces, 1802

    01 · Philadelphia

    All men created equal

    The American Revolution turned Enlightenment theory into a founding creed and, in 1787, built the first large modern republic. Its promise of equality — and its glaring betrayal of that promise in slavery — would drive two centuries of struggle to make the words true.

    02 · Paris

    Liberté, égalité, fraternité

    The storming of the Bastille and the Declaration of the Rights of Man destroyed a thousand-year order of monarchy and privilege. Terror and Napoleon followed, but so did a new political language — left and right, nationalism, the citizen — that the whole world still speaks.

    03 · Saint-Domingue

    The universal made literal

    Half a million enslaved people, led by Toussaint Louverture and Jean-Jacques Dessalines, defeated France, Spain, and Britain to found Haiti in 1804 — the only successful slave revolution in history. It forced the word "universal" in universal rights to be taken at last at its word.

    04 · The Andes

    A continent breaks free

    From Hidalgo's cry in Mexico to Bolívar and San Martín's campaigns across the Andes, Spanish and Portuguese America broke away within a single generation: nine new states by 1825, the Empire of Brazil among them, and more than a dozen once Gran Colombia and the Central American federation split apart. It was the largest wave of decolonization before the twentieth century.

    05 · Breaking the chain

    The first global rights movement

    Driven by the resistance of the enslaved and by Black abolitionists like Frederick Douglass, the slave trade was outlawed in 1807, then slavery itself — the British Empire in 1833, the United States in 1865, and Brazil last of all in 1888. Link by link, a system that had trafficked over twelve million Africans was struck off.

    01 · Hawaiʻi & Tonga

    Two Pacific kingdoms

    Kamehameha I brought the Hawaiian Islands under one rule between 1795, when he took Oʻahu at the battle of Nuʻuanu, and 1810, when Kaumualiʻi of Kauaʻi agreed to submit. His successors built a state on their own terms — a written constitution in 1840, independence recognised by Britain and France in 1843, and a reading public that by some counts was among the most literate on earth, which published more than a hundred Hawaiian-language newspapers between 1834 and 1948. In January 1893 a committee of businessmen, most of them American citizens or of American descent, backed by sailors and marines landed from USS Boston, overthrew Queen Liliʻuokalani in Honolulu; the Hui Aloha ʻĀina's Kūʻē petition of 1897 carried 21,269 signatures against annexation, and the United States annexed the islands anyway in 1898. From Nukuʻalofa, George Tupou I gave Tonga a constitution in 1875 that is still in force, and Tonga — a British protected state from 1900 but never annexed — kept its crown.

    02 · Aotearoa

    Te Tiriti, and the wars for the land

    Te Tiriti o Waitangi was first signed at Waitangi on 6 February 1840, and about 540 rangatira had signed by that September — almost all of them on the Māori text, which gave the Crown kāwanatanga (governance) and guaranteed tino rangatiratanga, full chiefly authority, over land and taonga. The English text speaks of ceding sovereignty, and in 2014 the Waitangi Tribunal found that the northern rangatira who signed in February 1840 had not ceded it. To stop the land sales, a gathering of tribes raised Pōtatau Te Wherowhero of Waikato as the first Māori King at Ngāruawāhia in 1858; in the New Zealand Wars (1845–72) Crown forces invaded the Waikato in 1863 to break the Kīngitanga, and the government confiscated more than a million hectares across the North Island. At Parihaka the prophets Te Whiti o Rongomai and Tohu Kākahi answered with non-violent resistance, until some 1,600 troops and volunteers marched into the village in November 1881.

    03 · Frontier & plantation

    Massacre, petition and indenture

    Australia's frontier wars ran from 1788 into the twentieth century. At Myall Creek in New South Wales, on 10 June 1838, stockmen murdered at least 28 Wirrayaraay people, most of them women, children and old men; seven of the killers were hanged — one of the very few times colonists were executed for killing Aboriginal people. Near Melbourne, the Kulin leaders Simon Wonga and William Barak chose the site of Coranderrk in 1863 and made it a self-supporting farm, defending it by petition, by deputations that walked to Melbourne, and before an 1881 inquiry whose transcript preserves their own words — until the Aborigines Protection Act of 1886 expelled younger residents of mixed descent and the reserve was closed in 1924. In Fiji, ceded to Britain in 1874, the Leonidas brought the first indentured Indian labourers to Levuka on 14 May 1879, and some 60,000 had come by 1916; in Samoa, partitioned between Germany and the United States in 1899 and seized by New Zealand in 1914, New Zealand police opened fire on a Mau procession in Apia on 28 December 1929 — Black Saturday — and killed its leader, Tupua Tamasese Lealofi III.

    04 · Removal

    From Prophetstown to the Trail of Tears

    The Shawnee brothers Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa built a confederacy of many nations from Prophetstown, on the Wabash, from 1808 to stop the land cessions; William Henry Harrison burned the town after the battle of Tippecanoe in November 1811, and Tecumseh, fighting as Britain's ally, was killed at the Thames in Upper Canada on 5 October 1813. The Indian Removal Act of 28 May 1830 then set out to clear the South-East: the Supreme Court upheld Cherokee sovereignty in Worcester v. Georgia (1832), but Georgia and President Jackson ignored it, and the Treaty of New Echota (1835), signed by a small faction and rejected by a petition carrying nearly 16,000 Cherokee signatures, became the legal pretext. In 1838 the army herded some 16,000 Cherokee into stockades and camps, and from south-east Tennessee they were forced west in 1838–39 along the Trail of Tears — most detachments overland by Nashville, across the Ohio and the Mississippi, and through Missouri — to Indian Territory, where the Nation rebuilt its capital at Tahlequah; about 4,000 is the most-cited estimate of the dead. With the Choctaw, Creek, Chickasaw and Seminole removals, something like 60,000 people were forced west of the Mississippi in little more than a decade.

    05 · The Plains

    The Black Hills, Wounded Knee, and the school

    The Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868 guaranteed the Black Hills to the Lakota — until Custer's 1874 expedition reported gold. On 25–26 June 1876 Lakota, Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho fighters, Sitting Bull and Crazy Horse among their leaders, destroyed Custer's command at the Little Bighorn; within a year Congress had taken the Hills, a seizure the Supreme Court ruled unlawful in 1980, awarding compensation the Lakota have refused to accept. On 29 December 1890, a fortnight after agency police shot Sitting Bull dead, the 7th Cavalry killed at least 150 and perhaps 300 Lakota, many of them women and children, at Wounded Knee. Taking the land came with taking the children: Carlisle Indian Industrial School in Pennsylvania, opened in 1879 with Lakota children from the Rosebud and Pine Ridge agencies among its first pupils — Luther Standing Bear, who later wrote about it, was one — became the model for hundreds of federal boarding schools.

    06 · The Métis & the treaties

    Red River, Batoche, and the numbered treaties

    When the Hudson's Bay Company sold Rupert's Land to Canada without consulting anyone who lived there, the Métis of Red River formed a provisional government under Louis Riel at Upper Fort Garry (1869–70) and won the Manitoba Act — though the land it promised Métis families was largely lost to delay and speculators, a failure the Supreme Court of Canada recognised in 2013. In Treaties 1 to 7 (1871–77) the Crown secured the prairies in return for reserves, annuities, schools and, in Treaty 6, a "medicine chest"; the nations understood them as agreements to share the land, and their oral terms are still argued in court. As the buffalo vanished, the Métis and some Cree bands rose in 1885: troops took Batoche on 12 May, Riel was hanged at Regina that November, and eight Cree and Assiniboine men were hanged together at Battleford. Canada's church-run residential schools, which more than 150,000 First Nations, Inuit and Métis children attended until the last of them closed in the late 1990s, were named a cultural genocide by the Truth and Reconciliation Commission in 2015.

    Milestones

    Forty-nine turning points

    1751–1772

    The Enlightenment & the Encyclopédie

    Europe & the Atlantic world

    Diderot and d'Alembert's Encyclopédie crystallized a movement — Locke, Montesquieu, Rousseau, Voltaire, Kant — that made reason, natural rights, and the questioning of throne and altar into public property, and became the operating manual for every revolution that followed.

    c. 1769–1830

    Steam, coal & the factory city

    Britain, then the world

    Watt's improved steam engine, Arkwright's mills, and the first passenger railways broke the ancient limits of muscle, wind, and water — creating the factory system, mass urbanization, a new working class, and the first large-scale carbon emissions.

    1775–1783

    The American Revolution

    North America

    The Declaration of Independence (1776) turned Enlightenment theory into a founding creed, and the 1787 Constitution built the first large modern republic — its example, and its contradiction of preserving slavery, shaping democratic struggle worldwide.

    1780–1783

    Túpac Amaru II & the great Andean rising

    South America — the Andes

    In November 1780 José Gabriel Condorcanqui, a kuraka near Cuzco who took the name of the last Inca, executed a Spanish corregidor and raised what is usually counted the largest Indigenous rising in colonial Spanish America, with his wife Micaela Bastidas among its commanders. Both were executed in Cuzco on 18 May 1781, but Túpac Katari and Bartolina Sisa besieged La Paz and the war ran into 1783; around 100,000 people are usually estimated to have died. Most of what we know comes from the Crown's own trial records.

    1788–1830s

    The First Fleet & the frontier wars

    Australia

    Eleven ships landed roughly 1,400 convicts, marines and officials at Sydney Cove in January 1788, opening the last great settler colonisation — and a century of frontier war, from Pemulwuy's raids around Sydney to the Black War in Tasmania, against peoples who had held the continent for at least 50,000 years, and perhaps 65,000.

    1789–1799

    The French Revolution

    France & Europe

    The Bastille's fall and the Declaration of the Rights of Man destroyed monarchy and privilege, then spiralled through terror to Napoleon — inventing modern politics: left and right, nationalism, citizen armies, and the metric system.

    1791–1804

    The Haitian Revolution

    Saint-Domingue / Haiti

    The only successful slave revolution in history: half a million enslaved people, led by Toussaint Louverture and Dessalines, defeated three European powers to found the first Black republic — forcing "universal rights" to be taken literally and terrifying every slaveholding power.

    1796–1806

    Vaccination goes round the world

    Britain, Spanish America, the Philippines & China

    Edward Jenner gave eight-year-old James Phipps cowpox in May 1796 and published his Inquiry in 1798 — the first vaccine. From 1803 the Spanish crown's Balmis expedition carried it alive across the Atlantic in 22 orphan boys, vaccinated one after another in the care of the rectoress Isabel Zendal, then on through Spanish America to Manila and Canton, vaccinating hundreds of thousands by 1806. Jenner's printed case histories and the expedition's reports to Madrid are the record.

    1804–1903

    The Sokoto Caliphate & Nana Asma'u's teachers

    West Africa — the Hausa lands (northern Nigeria & Niger)

    Usman dan Fodio's jihad of 1804 built the most populous state in nineteenth-century West Africa, ruled by scholars who wrote in Arabic — and one of the largest slave societies on earth, with at least a million enslaved people by 1900, until Lugard's forces took Sokoto in 1903. His daughter Nana Asma'u (1793–1864) left more than sixty works in Arabic, Fulfulde and Hausa and trained a network of women teachers, the yan taru; the caliphate's own manuscripts are how we know it from the inside.

    1805–1869

    Muhammad Ali's Egypt & the Suez Canal

    Egypt & North Africa

    Muhammad Ali conscripted peasants, monopolised the cotton crop and built arsenals, textile mills and a modern army — the first serious attempt outside Europe to industrialise by decree. His son Sa'id granted the concession and his grandson Isma'il opened the Suez Canal (1869), cutting the sea road from London to Bombay by two-fifths; the debt it took delivered Egypt to European creditors and, in 1882, to British occupation.

    1807–1888

    Abolition of Atlantic slavery

    Britain, Africa & the Americas

    Driven by the enslaved's resistance and Black abolitionists like Frederick Douglass, the slave trade was outlawed (1807) and then slavery itself — British Empire 1833, United States 1865, Brazil last in 1888 — the first global human-rights movement.

    c. 1810–1825

    Latin American independence

    Spanish & Portuguese America

    From Hidalgo's cry in Mexico to Bolívar and San Martín's Andean campaigns, Iberian America broke away in a generation — nine new states by 1825, the Empire of Brazil among them, and more than a dozen once Gran Colombia (1830–31) and the Central American federation (1838–41) split apart. It was the largest wave of decolonization before the twentieth century, and it ended three centuries of Iberian rule on the American mainland.

    1815–1816

    Tambora & the year without a summer

    Sumbawa (Indonesia), then the northern hemisphere

    On 10 April 1815 Mount Tambora blew itself apart in the largest eruption in recorded history, killing perhaps 10,000 people outright and tens of thousands more by famine on Sumbawa and Lombok. Its sulphur veil cooled the globe by roughly 0.4–0.7 °C: snow lay in Vermont in June 1816, and harvests failed from Ireland to Yunnan. The sulphate layer in Greenland and Antarctic ice is the proof that climate still set the harvest on the eve of the coal age (see the climate spine).

    1830–1847

    The French conquest of Algeria

    North Africa

    France invaded in 1830 and spent seventeen years breaking Abd al-Qadir's resistance, burning villages and expropriating the best land for European settlers — the template for settler colonialism in Africa, and the wound that produced the war of 1954–62.

    1830–1920

    The Cultivation System & indenture

    Java, India, China & the plantation world

    As abolition closed one supply of forced labour, others opened: Java's cultuurstelsel compelled peasants to give a fifth of their land and labour to Dutch export crops, and close to two million indentured Indians and Chinese were shipped to Mauritius, Trinidad, Guyana, Natal, Fiji and Peru under contracts that reproduced much of slavery's coercion — and built the diaspora communities of those places.

    1831–1832

    The Baptist War in Jamaica

    The Caribbean

    At Christmas 1831 Samuel Sharpe, an enslaved Baptist deacon, called a strike across western Jamaica that became the largest rising in the British Caribbean — up to 60,000 of the island's 300,000 enslaved people. It was crushed within weeks and more than 300 people were executed, Sharpe on 23 May 1832; but missionaries' evidence of the reprisals helped break the planter lobby in Parliament, and the Slavery Abolition Act followed in 1833. Trial records and the 1832 parliamentary evidence are the core sources.

    1839–1860

    The Opium Wars

    China

    When Commissioner Lin Zexu destroyed some 1,200 tonnes of British opium at Canton, Britain answered with steam gunboats; two wars later China had ceded Hong Kong, opened treaty ports, and been forced to legalise the drug — the moment a state that had set the terms of world trade for centuries lost the power to set its own.

    1844–1876

    The telegraph & telephone

    United States & Britain, then worldwide

    Morse's 1844 line and the durable 1866 transatlantic cable collapsed weeks of communication into minutes; Bell's telephone (1876) carried the voice itself — rewiring commerce, journalism, diplomacy, and war.

    1845–1902

    Famine in the age of free trade

    Ireland, India, north China & Brazil

    A million died in Ireland while grain and cattle were exported under guard (1845–52), and the El Niño droughts of 1876–79 and 1896–1902 killed perhaps 30–60 million in India, north China and the Brazilian sertão as railways carried food away to paying markets. Famine stopped being a failure of harvests and became a failure of entitlement and policy.

    1845–1912

    The poles, and the whale fishery

    Arctic & Southern Ocean

    Franklin's expedition vanished in the Canadian Arctic in 1845, and Inuit testimony about its fate — relayed by John Rae in 1854 and rejected in London — was vindicated 160 years later when it helped guide searchers to Erebus (2014) and Terror (2016). Nansen let the Fram freeze into the polar drift, Amundsen reached the South Pole in December 1911 five weeks ahead of Scott, who died returning — and Svend Foyn's explosive harpoon turned whales into an industrial crop, beginning the collapse of one whale stock after another from Finnmark to the Southern Ocean.

    c. 1847–1885

    Germ theory & modern medicine

    Europe, worldwide impact

    From Semmelweis's handwashing and Snow's cholera map to Pasteur's microbes, Lister's antiseptic surgery, and Koch's tuberculosis and cholera bacilli, disease was traced to living germs — beginning the greatest fall in human mortality ever recorded.

    1847–1901

    The Caste War of Yucatán

    Mesoamerica — the Yucatán peninsula

    Maya communities pushed off their lands by spreading sugar and henequen estates rose in 1847, and by the spring of 1848 held most of the peninsula except Mérida and Campeche; both sides massacred civilians. Rallied from 1850 by the "Talking Cross" at Chan Santa Cruz, the rebels ran an independent Maya state in the eastern forest for half a century, buying guns through British Honduras, until Mexican troops took Chan Santa Cruz in 1901. Rebel leaders' letters survive beside the government's reports.

    1850–1864

    The Taiping Rebellion

    China

    Hong Xiuquan, a failed examination candidate who believed himself Christ's younger brother, raised a peasant army, took Nanjing, and held the lower Yangzi for eleven years. The war to destroy his Heavenly Kingdom killed an estimated 20–30 million people — the deadliest civil war in human history, larger than the First World War, and almost never taught outside China.

    1851–1902

    Gold, federation & the first votes for women

    Australia

    The 1851 gold rushes multiplied the colonies' population two-and-a-half times in a decade (Victoria's nearly sevenfold) and drew tens of thousands of Chinese miners; the colonies federated in 1901; and South Australia (1894) and then the Commonwealth (1902) gave women both the vote and the right to stand for parliament, ahead of almost anywhere on earth — while the same parliament legislated White Australia and left Aboriginal people outside the franchise entirely.

    1853–1895

    The Great Game & the fall of the khanates

    Central Asia

    Russian columns took Tashkent, Samarkand, Bukhara and Khiva, ending the independence of the old Silk Road states and turning their irrigated fields over to cotton for Moscow's mills, while Britain fought two wars in Afghanistan to block them — the first ending with the destruction of a whole army on the retreat from Kabul in 1842. The boundaries their surveyors drew are still the map of Central Asia.

    1856–1924

    The discovery of human antiquity

    Germany, Java & South Africa

    Quarrymen in the Neander Valley turned up a thick-browed skullcap in 1856; Huxley placed humans among the apes in 1863 and Darwin followed with the Descent of Man (1871); Eugène Dubois dug Pithecanthropus out of a Javanese riverbank in 1891; and Raymond Dart read the Taung Child as an upright African ancestor in 1924 — while the Piltdown forgery of 1912 sent the field down a false trail for forty years. This is the chapter in which our species worked out what it was.

    1857–1858

    The Indian Rebellion of 1857

    South Asia

    Sepoys, peasants, and princes rose against the East India Company in the era's largest anti-colonial revolt; its defeat ended Company rule and put India directly under the Crown. Its memory — the siege of Delhi, Rani Lakshmibai dying in the saddle at Gwalior — became the founding story of the movement that ended the Raj ninety years later.

    1859

    Darwin's On the Origin of Species

    Britain, evidence gathered worldwide

    Darwin (with Wallace's parallel insight) explained life's diversity through natural selection, placing humanity inside nature rather than above it — biology's unifying theory, and one soon distorted into "social Darwinist" justifications for racism and empire.

    1862–1863

    The slave raids on Rapa Nui

    Oceania — Rapa Nui (Easter Island)

    Peruvian slave raiders carried more than 1,400 people off Rapa Nui in 1862–63 — between a third and a half of the islanders, by most estimates. Most died of disease before protest ended the trade in 1863, and the few sent home brought smallpox; after further epidemics and removals, a count in 1877 found 111 people. With the dead went much of the knowledge that might have let anyone read rongorongo. The historian H. E. Maude reconstructed the trade voyage by voyage.

    1864–1870

    The Paraguayan War

    South America — the Río de la Plata

    Paraguay fought Brazil, Argentina and Uruguay for more than five years, until Francisco Solano López was killed at Cerro Corá in March 1870 — the deadliest interstate war in Latin American history. How deadly is among demography's widest disagreements: Vera Blinn Reber put Paraguay's loss at no more than 18.5% of its people, Jan Kleinpenning at 43–52%, and Thomas Whigham and Barbara Potthast, from a rediscovered 1870–71 census, at 60–69%. The fight is over baselines — how many Paraguayans there were in 1864 — as much as bodies.

    1868

    The Meiji Restoration

    Japan

    After Perry's gunboats forced open its ports, Japan overthrew the shogunate and industrialized on its own terms — railways, factories, universal schooling, a modern navy — in a single generation. Its 1905 defeat of Russia electrified anti-colonial thinkers across Asia and Africa.

    1879–1890s

    Electrification

    United States & Europe, then global cities

    Edison's practical light bulb (1879) and Pearl Street station (1882), then Tesla and Westinghouse's alternating current, turned electricity into urban infrastructure — night became optional, and the grid became civilization's nervous system.

    1881–1898

    The Mahdist state in Sudan

    Northeast Africa

    Muhammad Ahmad's followers annihilated an Egyptian army, took Khartoum in 1885 and governed an independent Islamic state for thirteen years — until Kitchener's Maxim guns and gunboats killed some 11,000 Sudanese in a single morning at Omdurman, the plainest demonstration anywhere of what the industrial arms gap now meant.

    1884–1896

    The Scramble for Africa & Adwa

    Africa

    At the Berlin Conference (1884–85) European powers — with no African present — set the rules by which they carved up nearly the whole continent over the next twenty years, ruling ~90% of Africa by 1914. Resistance was constant — and at Adwa in 1896 Menelik II's Ethiopian army destroyed an Italian invasion, keeping Ethiopia free.

    1885–1908

    The Congo Free State

    Central Africa

    Leopold II held the Congo basin as personal property and extracted wild rubber by hostage-taking and mutilation, at a demographic cost usually estimated in the millions and still argued over. A Liverpool shipping clerk, E. D. Morel, noticed that ships came home loaded with rubber and went out carrying only guns; with Roger Casement's consular report he built the first modern international human-rights campaign, and forced Belgium to take the colony off its king in 1908.

    1893–1920

    Women's suffrage

    New Zealand, then worldwide

    New Zealand gave women the vote in 1893, followed by Australia and Finland and — after decades of organized struggle by suffragists and suffragettes — Britain (1918/1928) and the United States (1920). Half of humanity began, unevenly, to win a political voice.

    1896–1902

    The Philippine Revolution

    Southeast Asia

    Andrés Bonifacio's Katipunan rose against Spain in 1896, and in 1899 Emilio Aguinaldo proclaimed the Malolos Republic — the first constitutional republic in Asia. The United States then bought the islands from Spain for $20 million and spent three years destroying it, in a war that killed some 200,000 Filipinos and taught a generation of Asian nationalists what self-determination would cost.

    1896–1913

    Radioactivity, and deep time made numerical

    France, Britain & the United States

    Henri Becquerel's fogged photographic plates (1896) and Marie and Pierre Curie's radium revealed that the earth carries its own clock: Bertram Boltwood dated rocks by uranium–lead decay in 1907, and Arthur Holmes published The Age of the Earth in 1913 with figures in the billions. The deep-time dates in the first two chapters of this tour are numbers rather than guesses because of those seventeen years; radiocarbon, which anchors the last fifty thousand years and the two chapters that follow, is a child of the next chapter.

    1904–1908

    The Herero & Nama genocide

    German South West Africa (Namibia)

    After the Herero rose against land and cattle seizures, General Lothar von Trotha ordered their extermination, drove survivors into the Omaheke desert and sealed the waterholes; roughly 80% of the Herero and half the Nama died, many in the camps on Shark Island. It is the first genocide of the twentieth century — and, some historians argue, a rehearsal in method, vocabulary and personnel for what Europe would do to itself, a line of continuity that others dispute.

    1910–1920

    The Mexican Revolution

    Mesoamerica

    A decade of civil war ended the Díaz dictatorship at a cost of perhaps a million lives, but Emiliano Zapata's Tierra y Libertad and Pancho Villa's northern army forced the constitution of 1917, usually counted as the first constitution anywhere to write enforceable social rights into its text: land redistribution (Article 27) and an eight-hour day, minimum wage and right to strike (Article 123), two years ahead of Weimar and decades ahead of the industrial powers' own labour settlements.

    1911–1912

    The Chinese Revolution

    China

    An accidental bomb blast in Wuchang set off mutinies that swept the Qing away within months; the boy emperor Puyi abdicated in February 1912 and Sun Yat-sen's republic replaced 2,100 years of imperial rule — the largest single political discontinuity of the century, and the start of a struggle over what should replace it that ran all the way to 1949.

    1914–1918

    The First World War

    Global

    Industrial technology met nineteenth-century tactics: machine guns, artillery, and gas killed some 20 million, including soldiers from India, Africa, and the Dominions. It destroyed four empires, triggered the Russian Revolution, redrew the Middle East, and lit the fuse for the next war.

    1915–1916

    The Armenian genocide

    West Asia — Anatolia & the Syrian desert

    From the night of 23–24 April 1915, when hundreds of Armenian leaders were arrested in Constantinople, the Ottoman government deported the Armenians of Anatolia toward the Syrian desert; between 600,000 and 1.5 million died by massacre, starvation and exposure, many in the camps around Deir ez-Zor, as Assyrian and Greek Orthodox Christians were also killed. American and German diplomatic reports, survivors' testimony and the Ottoman courts-martial of 1919–20 document it; scholars treat it as genocide by consensus, and the Turkish state still denies it.

    1918–1920

    The influenza pandemic

    Global

    An influenza strain killed somewhere between 17 and 100 million people — the most-cited estimate is around 50 million — in little more than a year, on that estimate more than twice the dead of the First World War, with perhaps a third of the deaths in India — and it left almost no mark on monuments, curricula, or popular memory. It is the largest event in this chapter that most people have never been taught.

    1918

    The pandemic in the far North

    The Arctic — Alaska & Labrador

    In late 1918 the influenza emptied Inuit settlements: it killed 72 of Brevig Mission's roughly 80 people in five days, and 204 of the 263 at Okak in Labrador, abandoned the next year. Those dead also left the pandemic's best evidence. In 1997 Johan Hultin recovered lung tissue from a woman buried in the Brevig permafrost, and with US Army autopsy samples it let Jeffery Taubenberger's team finish sequencing the 1918 virus in 2005.

    1929–1930

    The Women's War

    West Africa — south-eastern Nigeria

    A census clerk's questions at Oloko in November 1929 convinced the women of the district that they were about to be taxed, and protest spread across some 6,000 square miles of south-eastern Nigeria. Igbo, Ibibio, Ogoni, Andoni, Efik and Ijaw women besieged the warrant chiefs by "sitting on a man" — song, dance and ridicule until he gave way — and troops shot about 55 of them dead. The 1930 Commission of Inquiry heard 485 witnesses; the warrant-chief system was dismantled and women sat on Native Courts for the first time.

    1930–1933

    The Kazakh famine

    Central Asia — Kazakhstan

    Stalin's collectivisation seized the steppe nomads' herds and forced them onto collective farms that could not feed them: between about 1.5 and 2.3 million people died — some 38–42% of all Kazakhs, the highest share of any people in the Soviet famines — and hundreds of thousands more fled to China, Mongolia and Afghanistan. The censuses carry it: 3.6 million Kazakhs in the republic in 1926, 2.2 million in 1937, a count so damning it was suppressed.

    1939–1945

    The Second World War & the atomic bomb

    Global

    The deadliest conflict in history — 70–85 million dead, including the industrialized genocide of six million Jews in the Holocaust — ending with the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki (1945): science's deepest insight into matter turned into a weapon that could end civilization.

    1943

    The Bengal famine

    South Asia

    Two to three million died in Bengal while rice was requisitioned for the war effort, fishing boats were confiscated to deny them to the Japanese, and relief shipping was refused in London. Amartya Sen's later study of it — the food existed; the entitlement to it did not — changed how economists everywhere understand famine.

    Why it happened

    Why coal, why Britain, and why it did not stay there

    Of all the transitions in this tour, this is the one with the least settled explanation. Something changed around 1800 that had never changed before: output per person, essentially flat for millennia, began to rise and did not stop. Why it began in a damp corner of north-west Europe rather than in the Yangzi delta, and why then rather than in 1500 or 2000, is still an open argument among people who have spent careers on it.

    The strongest version of the case for Britain is unromantic and largely geological. Coal lay near the surface and near navigable water, in a country where labour was expensive and energy was cheap — so a machine that burned coal to save wages paid for itself in Newcastle and almost nowhere else. Around that sat a patent system, a dense artisan culture that could actually build what natural philosophers described, a state that taxed heavily and borrowed cheaply, and a protected imperial market. By 1830 Lancashire was spinning cotton grown by enslaved people in the American South and selling the cloth into India, whose own weavers had dominated that trade a century earlier; India's share of world manufacturing output fell from roughly a quarter in 1750 to about 2% by 1900. Industrialisation and empire were never two stories.

    The counter-case is that in 1750 there was not yet much to explain. Comparisons of the Yangzi delta with England around that date find similar life expectancies, similarly efficient markets, similar consumption of sugar and cloth, and similar limits — both were running out of forest. What England had that Jiangnan did not was coal in the right place and the "ghost acreage" of the Americas: millions of hectares of land, worked by coerced labour, supplying calories and fibre that Europe would otherwise have had to grow at home. On that reading the divergence is late, contingent, and inseparable from slavery. Others put England's lead a century or two earlier and locate it in agricultural productivity, literacy, and institutions rather than in coal or colonies, and they dispute the wage series the coal argument rests on. The honest position is that each account explains part of it and none has closed the case — one of the live debates catalogued in How We Know.

    What it broke, though, is not in doubt. Every earlier society ran on the current solar income — grain for muscle, wood for heat, wind and falling water for the rest — and its population pressed against that ceiling until famine or disease pushed it back. Coal was a stock rather than a flow, and burning it lifted the ceiling faster than population could rise to meet it. Humanity took its entire existence to reach one billion people around 1804; the second billion arrived in 1927, and the third only thirty-three years after that. Behind the curve is the demographic transition: deaths fell first, births followed a generation or two later, and every population boom of the modern world lives in the gap between those two lines (see the population record).

    The mortality half of that transition was, at first, plumbing rather than medicine. Cholera came out of Bengal in 1817 and reached Britain by 1831; in 1854 John Snow mapped more than six hundred deaths onto a single Soho well and had the Broad Street pump handle removed — thirty years before Koch isolated the organism. Chadwick's sanitary report (1842), Bazalgette's 132 km of intercepting sewers after the Great Stink of 1858, filtered water, milk inspection, and smallpox vaccination raised life expectancy in England from about 40 years in 1850 to over 50 by 1911 — more than any drug would until antibiotics. The same steam network that carried sanitation carried infection: rinderpest killed some 90% of the cattle in eastern and southern Africa between 1888 and 1897, plague travelled the shipping lanes from 1894, and in 1918 influenza rode the troopships to every inhabited continent in four months.

    Why a handful of industrial states could conquer so much of the world so fast has a blunt answer and a subtler one. The blunt one is what the historian Daniel Headrick called the tools of empire: the East India Company's iron steamer Nemesis, drawing barely six feet of water, carried the Opium War into the Pearl River in 1841; preventive doses of quinine, in large-scale use from about 1850, let Europeans survive a West African interior that had killed them in droves; and breech-loaders, then the Maxim gun of 1884, made Omdurman possible. The subtler answer is that the gap was never absolute. A Zulu army destroyed a British column at Isandlwana in January 1879, and Menelik won at Adwa in 1896 with an army of perhaps 73,000 to more than 100,000 carrying modern rifles from France and Russia — and, earlier, from Italy itself: where numbers, terrain and the same weapons stood on the other side, the outcome changed. Disease, too, cut both ways. Quinine shielded newcomers in the tropics, while the newcomers' own infections — smallpox carried home to Rapa Nui, measles that killed more than 40,000 Fijians in 1875–76, influenza at Okak and Brevig Mission — emptied island and Arctic communities.

    The age's emancipations and its coercions ran on the same rails. Britain abolished slavery in 1833 by compensating the owners — £20 million, some 40% of annual government spending, a debt folded into government stock whose last tranche was redeemed only in 2015 — and the enslaved received nothing; the United States ended it in 1865 and Brazil in 1888. But the demand for cheap tropical labour did not end with it, and indenture took its place, carrying close to two million Indians and Chinese to Mauritius, Trinidad, Guyana, Natal, Fiji, and Peru. In the mills, women and children were not incidental but structural: children under thirteen were still legal factory labour in Britain until the Acts of 1833 and 1844 cut their hours, and women made up over half the cotton workforce at a fraction of men's wages. Pulling women's work out of the household and into the wage economy made their exclusion from the vote newly visible and newly arguable — Seneca Falls in 1848, New Zealand in 1893, South Australia in 1894, Finland in 1906, and the wave of enfranchisements that followed the First World War.

    Abolition in the Atlantic did not end unfreedom elsewhere, and in places the century deepened it. Russia freed more than 23 million serfs on private estates by decree in 1861 — more people than all the Atlantic emancipations together — but bound them to decades of redemption payments and to the village commune. Much of West Africa's new "legitimate" trade in palm oil was produced by enslaved labour, and the Sokoto Caliphate held at least a million enslaved people by 1900. Nor were the century's great migrations only European: Adam McKeown's global count finds the flows from India and south China into South-east Asia and around the Indian Ocean, and from Russia and north China into Siberia and Manchuria, broadly comparable in size and timing to the transatlantic one. The rice fields and rubber estates of South-east Asia and the Manchurian frontier were as much a part of the industrial world as the mills of Manchester.

    Gender was being rewritten in both directions at once. Olympe de Gouges answered the Rights of Man with a Declaration of the Rights of Woman in 1791 and went to the guillotine in 1793; Mary Wollstonecraft's Vindication of the Rights of Woman followed in 1792 — yet Napoleon's Civil Code of 1804 wrote married women's subordination to their husbands into law, and France did not abolish that "marital power" until 1938. The quieter revolution was in the household: French birth rates began to fall in the late eighteenth century, much earlier than in the rest of Europe — the earliest national example of the fertility half of the demographic transition. And women's politics was never only a European export. Nana Asma'u's women teachers in Sokoto from the 1830s, Yaa Asantewaa leading the Asante war of 1900 over the Golden Stool, and the women who brought down Nigeria's warrant chiefs in 1929 acted on their own terms and traditions, not as echoes of Seneca Falls.

    Coal did not free the harvest from the weather. Tambora's eruption in 1815 gave the northern hemisphere a year without a summer, and the El Niño droughts of 1876–79 and 1896–1902 were real failures of rain: a 2019 reconstruction of Indian soil moisture ties five of the six great Indian famines between 1873 and 1943 to drought, and only the last, Bengal in 1943, to none. Weather set the stage; markets and policy decided who starved. The new economy's own assault on living things was faster than any volcano. Railways, long-range rifles and the eastern tanneries' demand for hides took the American bison from tens of millions to the edge of extinction by the late 1880s, destroying the Plains nations' food along with it, and the passenger pigeon — once perhaps three to five billion strong — ended with a single bird, Martha, in the Cincinnati Zoo on 1 September 1914. Britain alone was digging 287 million tonnes of coal a year by 1913.

    And the exhaust. Air trapped in polar ice shows carbon dioxide sitting near 280 parts per million by the end of a Holocene that had opened nearer 265, lifting from the early nineteenth century and reaching about 311 ppm by 1950 — the first bend in a curve the next chapter inherits (the climate spine runs through the master timeline). What this age hands forward is not its empires, which were bankrupt by 1945, but their machinery — fossil energy at scale, the factory, the census, the cable — and their bill. Chapter IX opens in that same summer of 1945, at the Trinity flash three weeks before Hiroshima, with that machinery intact and the people who had been ruled by it intending to collect on every promise made to them.

    Competing explanations

    • Coal and colonies. England and the Yangzi delta were comparable in 1750; accessible coal plus the land and coerced labour of the Americas broke the tie. Influential and contested.
    • High wages, cheap energy. British wages were unusually high relative to the price of coal, so labour-saving machinery was profitable there before anywhere else. The underlying wage series is disputed.
    • Institutions. Secure property, enforceable contracts, patents, and post-1688 limits on the crown made long-horizon investment rational. Widely accepted as necessary, argued over as sufficient.
    • Useful knowledge. An "industrial Enlightenment" that put artisans and natural philosophers in the same room, and made improvement a publishable, cumulative activity.
    • An earlier, smaller divergence. National-accounts reconstructions put England's per-capita lead in the seventeenth century or before, which would make 1750 comparability overstated. Contested.
    • How much empire mattered. Plantation and colonial profits were real, but most British capital formation was domestic — the weight to give empire in the causal chain is the sharpest live disagreement in the field.
    • An industrious revolution. Jan de Vries argues that households in north-west Europe were already working longer and selling more of their labour before the machines arrived, to buy sugar, tea, cloth and crockery — so demand pulled the factory into being as much as coal pushed it. Built on probate inventories; how far it travels beyond England and the Low Countries is argued over.
    • Tools of empire, or terms of collaboration? Daniel Headrick's case that steamers, quinine and quick-firing guns made conquest cheap is widely accepted; critics add that local allies, rivalries and credit did as much of the work, and Adwa shows how fast the gap closed when Africans held the same rifles.
    • Famine: weather or policy? Amartya Sen's entitlement analysis and Mike Davis's Late Victorian Holocausts (2001) put the great famines on markets and imperial policy. Soil-moisture reconstructions confirm severe drought behind every great Indian famine from 1873 to 1899 but not in 1943, while Mark Tauger argues that a natural disaster in 1942 did cause a real shortage in Bengal. How the causes combine is contested.
    • From Windhoek to Auschwitz? Jürgen Zimmerer and others read the Herero and Nama genocide as a precursor of Nazi policy in method, vocabulary and personnel; Robert Gerwarth, Stephan Malinowski, Birthe Kundrus and others find those links thin and the differences decisive. Contested.
    • How many died in 1918? The familiar 50 million, possibly 100, comes from Johnson and Mueller (2002); Spreeuwenberg and colleagues (2018), modelling excess deaths from national mortality records and British India's statistics, put it nearer 17 million. The gap is one of method, and most of it turns on India, China and Africa, where registration was thinnest.

    Meanwhile, everywhere

    One industrial world, sixteen places

    Coal and steam started on a few coalfields, but no region was just a backdrop to them. Each of the sixteen below had its own states, wars, faiths and workers, and its own answer to the industrial empires.

    West Asia

    1839–1932

    The Ottoman state reformed against the clock: the Gülhane edict of 1839 opened the Tanzimat, a constitution and parliament followed in 1876, and the Young Turks restored them in 1908. In Iran a boycott broke a British tobacco monopoly in 1891–92, the first Majlis met in 1906, and oil was struck at Masjed Soleyman in 1908. The First World War brought the Armenian genocide, the Arab Revolt and British and French mandates; out of the wreck came the Republic of Turkey (1923) and the Saudi kingdom (1932).

    North Africa & the Nile

    1805–1931

    Muhammad Ali's Egypt conscripted its peasants and built state mills; the Suez Canal (1869) and its debts brought British occupation in 1882, and the revolution of 1919 won a qualified independence in 1922. France took Algiers in 1830, Tunisia in 1881 and most of Morocco in 1912. Italy invaded Libya in 1911, dropping the first bombs ever thrown from an aeroplane, and Omar al-Mukhtar fought on in Cyrenaica until he was hanged in 1931.

    West & Central Africa

    c. 1750–1929

    An age of African state-building as well as conquest: Asante ruled much of today's Ghana from Kumasi, Dahomey fielded a corps of women soldiers, the Sokoto Caliphate united the Hausa cities after 1804, and Samori Ture fought France from 1882 until his capture in 1898. Freetown (1792) and Liberia, independent in 1847, grew as homes for freed and returning Africans. After 1885 came conquest and the Congo's rubber terror, and new resistance, from Yaa Asantewaa's war of 1900 to the Women's War of 1929.

    East & Southern Africa

    c. 1816–1936

    Shaka built the Zulu kingdom in the 1810s and 1820s, amid upheavals (the mfecane) whose causes are still disputed, and a Zulu army destroyed a British column at Isandlwana in 1879. Diamonds (from 1867) and Witwatersrand gold (1886) brought migrant-labour compounds, the South African War of 1899–1902 and the Natives Land Act of 1913. In Madagascar the Merina kings, from Radama I (r. 1810–28), spread their rule over much of the island with British-trained troops until France conquered it in 1895–97. Zanzibar's clove plantations ran on enslaved labour long after an 1873 treaty closed its slave market — slavery itself was abolished only in 1897 — and Ethiopia built a state that won at Adwa in 1896, then faced Italy's poison gas in 1935–36.

    Europe & the Mediterranean

    1750–1945

    Coalfields from Lancashire to the Ruhr and the Donbas remade Europe's economy, and the French Revolution and Napoleon's wars remade its politics. Italy (1861) and Germany (1871) were unified from above, Russia freed its serfs in 1861, famine killed a million people in Ireland in 1845–52, and some 60 million Europeans emigrated between 1815 and 1932, most to the Americas. The age ended in two world wars, the Russian Revolution and the Holocaust, in which Nazi Germany and its collaborators murdered six million Jews, and Roma and disabled people besides.

    Central Asia & the Steppe

    1755–1933

    Qing armies destroyed the Zunghar Khanate in 1755–58, and war and smallpox killed, by most estimates, most of the Zunghar people. From the west, Russia absorbed the Kazakh hordes, took Tashkent (1865), Samarkand (1868) and Khiva (1873) and turned the oases over to cotton; a revolt against wartime labour conscription in 1916 was crushed, and tens of thousands died fleeing into China. Outer Mongolia broke from the Qing in 1911, and Soviet collectivisation brought the Kazakh famine of 1930–33.

    South Asia

    1757–1930

    The East India Company won Bengal at Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764); in the famine of 1769–70, Warren Hastings reckoned, a third of Bengal's people died. Tipu Sultan's Mysore (1799), the Marathas (1818) and the Sikh Empire (1849) fell in turn, and after 1857 the Crown ruled directly. Railways, the census and Lancashire cloth reshaped the economy, while the Indian National Congress (1885), the swadeshi boycotts of 1905, the Amritsar massacre (1919) and Gandhi's Salt March (1930) built a mass movement for independence.

    East Asia

    1793–1945

    Qing China was at its largest under the Qianlong emperor, who told Britain's envoy Macartney in 1793 that his empire had no need of British manufactures. Then came the Opium Wars, the Taiping war and defeat in 1895 by an industrialising Japan, which took Taiwan and, in 1910, Korea, where the Donghak peasant war of 1894 and the March First movement of 1919 were both put down by force. The Qing fell in 1912, and Japan's invasion of China in 1937 began eight years of war that killed millions.

    Southeast Asia

    1752–1942

    New dynasties shaped the mainland: the Konbaung in Burma (1752), the Chakri in Bangkok (1782) and the Nguyễn, who united Vietnam in 1802. Tambora killed tens of thousands on Sumbawa and Lombok in 1815. The Dutch imposed the Cultivation System on Java after the Java War of 1825–30, France took Saigon in 1859 and Britain Mandalay in 1885, and only Siam stayed independent. Filipinos proclaimed Asia's first constitutional republic in 1899, before an American war of conquest, and Japan overran the region in 1941–42.

    Australia

    1788–1938

    Smallpox reached the Sydney clans in 1789; in the south-east, disease, frontier killing and dispossession cut Aboriginal numbers by an estimated 80 per cent within two generations of contact. Resistance ran from Pemulwuy and Tasmania's Black War to the Gunditjmara's Eumeralla wars around the Budj Bim eel traps, and the seven men hanged for the Myall Creek massacre of 1838 were a rare conviction. Macassan trepang fleets sailed to Arnhem Land until 1906, and on 26 January 1938 William Cooper, Jack Patten and William Ferguson held the first Day of Mourning.

    Oceania & the Pacific

    1769–1945

    In 1769 Tupaia, a navigator from Raʻiātea, drew Cook a chart naming 74 islands, from Rotuma to Rapa Nui. Kamehameha I united Hawaiʻi (1795–1810), Tonga adopted a constitution in 1875, and Māori chiefs signed Te Tiriti o Waitangi in 1840, then fought the New Zealand Wars as settlers took their land. Measles killed more than 40,000 Fijians in 1875–76, some 60,000 Islanders were recruited or kidnapped for Queensland's cane fields, Hawaiʻi's queen was deposed in 1893, and the Pacific War swept from Guadalcanal to Kokoda.

    The Arctic

    1776–1930s

    Indigenous northerners met four expanding states. Greenland's trade was a Danish royal monopoly from 1776; Russian fur companies forced Unangan (Aleut) men to hunt sea otters until the United States bought Alaska in 1867 without consulting its peoples; Sámi at Kautokeino rose in 1852 and later faced Norwegianisation in school; and Soviet collectivisation reached Siberia's reindeer herders in the 1930s. Inughuit sledgers carried Peary and Henson north in 1909, and the 1918 influenza killed most of the people at Brevig Mission and Okak.

    North America

    c. 1750–1930s

    For a century after 1750 the Comanche held the southern Plains as a horse-and-trade power, until the United States pushed west: Tecumseh's confederacy broke in 1813, some 16,000 Cherokee were forced west in 1838–39, and the bison were all but gone by the late 1880s. The Civil War killed perhaps 750,000 and ended slavery; Chinese exclusion (1882), segregation laws and, from about 1916, the Great Migration north followed. Canada confederated in 1867, hanged the Métis leader Louis Riel in 1885 and sent Indigenous children to residential schools.

    Mesoamerica

    1810–1932

    Hidalgo's rising of 1810 began the war that made Mexico independent in 1821; Central America went its own way in 1823. Mexico lost half its territory to the United States in 1846–48 and shot the French-installed emperor Maximilian in 1867, and under Porfirio Díaz henequen and coffee estates swallowed village lands until the Revolution of 1910–20. Maya rebels ran their own state at Chan Santa Cruz until 1901, and in El Salvador in 1932 the army killed 10,000–40,000 people, most of them Indigenous peasants: La Matanza.

    The Caribbean

    1789–1937

    Saint-Domingue was the most profitable colony on earth in 1789; Haiti, free in 1804, was made in 1825 to pay France 150 million francs for its independence. Britain freed the enslaved in its colonies in 1834–38, then shipped in indentured Indians; at Morant Bay in 1865 troops killed 439 Jamaicans and some 354 more were executed. Cuba ended slavery only in 1886, US Marines occupied Haiti from 1915 to 1934, and in 1937 Trujillo's soldiers massacred thousands of Haitians along the Dominican border.

    South America

    1780–1930

    Túpac Amaru II's rising (1780–83) shook the Andes a generation before the wars that made nine new states by 1825. The Paraguayan War (1864–70) devastated Paraguay, and Peru's guano and the Atacama's nitrate fed Europe's fields and set off the War of the Pacific (1879–84), which left Bolivia landlocked. Chile and Argentina seized Mapuche and other Indigenous lands in the 1860s–80s, the Amazon rubber boom ran on debt bondage and terror, and Brazil ended slavery in 1888, last in the Americas, as millions of European migrants arrived.

    Ordinary lives

    Working lives, read from bones and memoirs

    Catoctin Furnace, Maryland · c. 1776–1850

    The ironworkers buried by the ore pit

    The furnace at the foot of Catoctin Mountain was in blast by 1776, casting pig iron, tools and munitions for the Revolutionary War. At least 271 enslaved people, and free African Americans in unknown numbers, mined its ore, burned its charcoal, worked its forge and drove its wagons. They buried their dead near an old ore pit, mothers beside their children. By about 1850 the furnace had switched to paid, mostly white labour, and the Black workers who built it were forgotten.

    How we know The graves were excavated in 1979–80 ahead of a highway. In 2023 a team led by Éadaoin Harney read the DNA of 27 of the dead, found five families among them, and traced 41,799 genetic relatives among consenting 23andMe customers, the closest of them still in Maryland. Their African ancestry points mainly to Senegambia and West-Central Africa; their European ancestry came mostly through fathers, which the authors read as evidence of the rape of enslaved women.

    Rupert's Valley, St Helena · 1840–1867

    The "liberated" who never went home

    From 1840 the Royal Navy brought the slave ships it captured in the South Atlantic to St Helena, and some 27,000 Africans were landed there by 1867 as "liberated Africans". Many were too weakened by the voyage to survive. They died in the island's receiving depots and were buried in unmarked graves in Rupert's Valley, perhaps 8,000 in all. With them lay the few things they had kept through capture: beads, buttons, a braided lock of hair.

    How we know Archaeologists recovered 325 people from 178 graves in 2007–08, ahead of the road to the island's first airport. A 2023 ancient-DNA study of 20 of them traced their origins to the region between northern Angola and Gabon; 17 of the 20 were male, matching the late slave trade's known preference for men. The dead were reburied in the valley in August 2022.

    Calcutta to the cane fields of Fiji · 1893–1914

    Totaram Sanadhya, girmitiya

    In 1893 a recruiter promised a teenager from a village in the Firozabad district, whose father's property had gone to moneylenders, an easy job with good pay. He was coached to lie to the magistrate who registered him and locked in a Calcutta depot when he changed his mind. He landed in Fiji on 28 May with some 500 others. On the plantation his weekly ration ran out in four days. After his five years of indenture he leased land, grew cane, became a Hindu priest, toured the estates to help the labourers still bound, and in 1910 wrote Fiji Indians' petition for representation and schooling. In 1914 he went home.

    How we know His Hindi memoir, My Twenty-One Years in the Fiji Islands (1914; English translation 1991), was banned in Fiji but widely read in India during the campaign that ended Indian indentured emigration in 1917. It is one man's account, written to persuade, so historians read it alongside the colonial emigration records.

    Tomioka, Gunma, Japan · 1873–1874

    Wada Ei at the reeling basins

    Japan's government opened its model silk mill at Tomioka in November 1872, with 300 reels under one brick roof, twice as many as the largest mills in France and Italy. Few families would send their daughters at first, because of a rumour that the French staff drank blood (it was red wine), so officials recruited samurai daughters from across Japan. Yokota Ei, later Wada Ei, came from Matsushiro in 1873 with fifteen other young women, trained for about a year and went home to teach reeling. The working day was eight hours then; after Mitsui bought the mill in 1893, summer shifts ran to twelve.

    How we know Her memoir, the Tomioka nikki, is the main inside account of the first mill women. She wrote it between about 1905 and 1913, more than thirty years after the events, and it was first published as a book in 1931, two years after her death, by the Shinano Education Association — so it is a memoir, not a diary kept at the time. The mill's buildings survive and are a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

    In their own words

    Voices from the other side of progress

    Seen whole

    Seven ways to hold two centuries

    How we know

    • Census and civil registration. Britain counted itself decennially from 1801 and registered every birth, marriage and death from 1837; India's first synchronous census came in 1881. For the first time a society's demography is a record rather than an estimate — which is why population figures in this chapter carry error bars measured in percent, and those in Chapter III in multiples.
    • Parish registers and family reconstitution. Before registration, historical demographers rebuild populations household by household from baptism, marriage and burial entries — the technique that gave us England's population back to 1541.
    • Reconstructed national accounts. Historical GDP is not measured but modelled, from wage and price series, tax and tithe records, and output data, then chained backwards — the Maddison Project's estimates are the standard source and are best read as orders of magnitude, not decimals.
    • Ice cores. Air bubbles in Antarctic ice at Law Dome record CO2 near 280 ppm before 1800 and about 311 ppm by 1950, overlapping the direct instrument record that begins in 1958.
    • Photography and film. From the 1839 daguerreotype through Roger Fenton in the Crimea and the Brady studio's Civil War plates to the newsreel, the era is the first that can be seen — and the first in which images were staged, censored and propagandised at scale.
    • Ships' logs, customs books and company ledgers. Bills of lading and colonial revenue records are what make the commodity flows above quantifiable at all.
    • Ice cores and tree rings. Tambora's sulphate layer in Greenland and Antarctic ice dates and sizes the 1815 eruption, and tree-ring widths and densities record the cold summers after it. The limit: converting a sulphate spike into degrees of cooling is modelled, and spikes differ from core to core, so eruption sizes are averaged across many.
    • Viral RNA from the permafrost. The 1918 virus was sequenced from lung tissue preserved in a Brevig Mission burial (recovered 1997) and in US Army autopsy blocks, the genome completed in 2005. It tells us what the virus was, not how many it killed — tolls come from modelling excess deaths, which is why they range so widely.
    • Censuses read against each other. The Paraguayan and Kazakh tolls come from subtracting one count from another — Paraguay's rediscovered 1870–71 census, the Soviet censuses of 1926 and 1937 — so they inherit every error in the baseline, and cannot by themselves separate the dead from those who fled.
    • The state's own inquiries. Much of what we know about the rebels comes from those who crushed them — the Spanish sentences on Túpac Amaru, Jamaica's 1832 trials, the Aba Commission's 485 witnesses in 1930. The state asked the questions, so historians read against the grain, and oral tradition — as with Inuit testimony on Franklin — supplies what the questions left out.
    • Manuscripts in Arabic and Ajami. The Sokoto Caliphate wrote its own history, in Arabic and in Hausa and Fulfulde set down in Arabic script, as in Nana Asma'u's poems. These are scholars' and rulers' texts; the enslaved people on whom the caliphate's farms depended barely speak in them.
    • Wide error bars on the dead. Second World War deaths are given as 70–85 million, the Taiping war as 20–30 million, and the 1918 influenza anywhere from 17 to 100 million depending on how Indian and African mortality is estimated. Treat every casualty figure on this page as a range that has been rounded — see How We Know and its register of live disputes.

    In the hand

    Objects that remade the world

    Watt's engine

    1769 · 1788

    A separate condenser (1769) and, from 1788, a spinning flyball governor turned coal's heat into steady, self-regulating rotary power — the tireless prime mover of the industrial age.

    A boll of raw cotton

    1793–1930

    The most traded commodity of the age — grown by enslaved people in Mississippi and by fellahin on Muhammad Ali's Nile, spun in Lancashire, and sold back as cloth to the Indian weavers it had ruined.

    The broken shackle

    1807–1888

    From Haiti's revolt to Brazil's last decree in 1888, link by link the Atlantic chain was struck off — the first worldwide victory for human rights.

    The Cherokee Phoenix

    1828

    Printed at New Echota from February 1828 in English and in the syllabary Sequoyah had completed in 1821 — the first newspaper published by Native Americans and the first in a Native American language, until money ran out in 1834 as removal closed in.

    Telegraph key

    1844

    A brass key tapping dots and dashes down a copper wire — "What hath God wrought" — one of the first machines to send a thought down a wire faster than a galloping horse — seven years after Cooke and Wheatstone patented theirs in Britain.

    A Marshallese stick chart

    described 1862

    Coconut-frond midribs lashed into a lattice, with shells for islands, map how ocean swells bend around atolls. Navigators memorised them before a voyage rather than carrying them; outsiders first described the system in 1862.

    Foyn's explosive harpoon

    1868

    A grenade-tipped harpoon fired from a steam catcher turned the fastest whales into cargo — and produced, from the Arctic to South Georgia, one of the great industrial-scale collapses of a wild population — alongside the bison and the passenger pigeon.

    Incandescent lamp

    1879

    A carbon filament glowing in a vacuum — Edison's lamp made the night optional and rebuilt the city around the electric grid.

    The Ammassalik wooden maps

    1885

    Kunit of Umivik carved East Greenland's coast in wood — headlands, fjords and islands felt by hand — and sold them to the Danish expedition of Gustav Holm in 1885. They are now in the Greenland National Museum in Nuuk.

    The Trinil skullcap

    1891

    Eugène Dubois dug a low, thick-browed calotte from a riverbank in Java and named it Pithecanthropus — the first human ancestor found by someone who went out deliberately looking for one.

    The Golden Stool

    c. 1700 · 1900

    The shrine of the Asante nation's soul, never sat upon. When Britain's governor demanded it in March 1900, Yaa Asantewaa led the war that followed; Asante was annexed, but the stool stayed hidden and is still in Asante keeping.

    The atom, split

    1945

    Physics' deepest look into matter, weaponised over Hiroshima and Nagasaki — a single device that placed the survival of civilization in human hands.
