    Chapter VII  ·  1450 – 1750 CE

    The Early
    Modern Age

    c. 1450 – 1750 CE · three centuries that fused the world

    Iberian ships stitched the hemispheres into one entangled world, as silver, print, and gunpowder built the first global economy atop the Columbian Exchange and the transatlantic slave trade.

    Scroll to begin — the year is 1450

    The chapter before us

    Between roughly 1450 and 1750, humanity's separate worlds were stitched — often violently — into a single entangled one. This is the hinge of the whole tour: the era when "world history" stops being a collection of regional stories and becomes one shared story.

    Chapter VI left the Old World half-knitted already: Mongol roads and monsoon sails had moved goods, ideas, and plague from Hangzhou to Cairo, Mali's gold had primed Mediterranean appetites for Africa, and Ming treasure fleets had shown what oceanic reach looked like — then been recalled. The Black Death's survivors inherited scarce labour, hungry treasuries, and Ottoman control of the eastern routes. This chapter is what happened when that recovering world acquired ships that could reach the two continents it had never counted.

    Print and gunpowder redistributed knowledge and power; Iberian ships joined hemispheres that had evolved apart for millennia, unleashing the Columbian Exchange, colonial conquest, and the transatlantic slave trade; and American silver threaded a first truly global economy running from Potosí through Manila to Ming and Qing China. It is a story with a moral core — the demographic catastrophe of the Americas and the forced migration of millions of Africans — that no triumphal telling can omit.

    And it is not a story with a single author. Land-based gunpowder empires — Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, and Qing — governed most of humankind and held most of its wealth; Songhai ruled the Sahel, while Benin, Kongo, and Asante, facing the Atlantic, shaped Africa's engagement with the sea; the Aztec Triple Alliance and the Inca Tawantinsuyu, the largest state the Americas had ever seen (and, at ~2 million km², among the largest on Earth around 1500), were sophisticated states, not blank land. Europe's Renaissance, Reformation, and Scientific Revolution belong in that crowded frame, one thread among many being pulled tight at once.

    01 · Who came first

    The ocean, already mastered

    Before any European ship crossed an ocean, Polynesian voyagers had settled the vast Pacific — from Hawaiʻi to Aotearoa to Rapa Nui — by reading stars, swells, and birds without instruments. Ming treasure fleets had reached East Africa by the 1420s. The sea was not empty of masters.

    02 · The breakout

    Three voyages, one net

    Columbus reached the Caribbean in 1492, da Gama rounded Africa to India in 1498, and the Magellan–Elcano expedition closed the loop around the globe in 1519–22. Latecomers to the ocean, Iberians nonetheless knotted every inhabited continent into a single web of sail.

    03 · The Exchange

    Two worlds trade lives

    Maize, potatoes, cassava, tomatoes, and cacao crossed east to feed and fatten Afro-Eurasia; wheat, sugarcane, horses, and cattle crossed west to remake the Americas. Diets, populations, and landscapes on every continent were rewritten by what the ships carried.

    04 · The catastrophe

    A hemisphere emptied

    The cargo also carried smallpox, measles, and influenza into populations with no immunity. An estimated 80–90% of Indigenous Americans died within a century — among history's greatest demographic collapses. It was disease, as much as steel, that toppled Tenochtitlan and Tawantinsuyu.

    05 · The Middle Passage

    The other crossing

    To replace the labour lost, Atlantic economies turned to enslaved Africans. About 12.5 million were forced onto ships and roughly 10.7 million survived the crossing to plantations and mines. The African diaspora — and the fortunes built on it — is inseparable from this era's rise.

    Broken spears lie in the roads; we have torn our hair in our grief. The houses are roofless now, and their walls are red with blood.

    — Nahuatl elegy for the fall of Tenochtitlan, from the Anales de Tlatelolco (dated 1528 by León-Portilla, the 1540s by Lockhart); Garibay–Kemp translation

    01 · The press

    Ink made cheap

    Gutenberg's press (c. 1450) made text replicable and cheap — roughly 200 million books in Europe by 1600. Movable metal type was older still: Korea's Jikji was printed in 1377, and Song China set ceramic type in the 1040s. Europe's small alphabet, cheap paper, and competing commercial printers made the technology explosive.

    02 · The Reformation

    A pamphlet detonates

    A print-amplified protest against indulgences shattered Latin Christendom into rival confessions and spread vernacular literacy through translated Bibles. A century of religious war ended at the Peace of Westphalia (1648) — long credited, with more myth than evidence, as the birth of the sovereign-state system.

    03 · New heavens

    The Earth demoted

    In 1543 Copernicus placed the Sun at the centre and Vesalius re-mapped the human body — both building on centuries of Islamic astronomy and medicine. To doubt the ancients on the cosmos was to doubt received authority everywhere.

    04 · New earth

    The apple and the Moon

    Galileo's telescope (1610) and Newton's Principia (1687) bound falling apples and orbiting moons under one law. The Royal Society took as its motto Nullius in verba — take nobody's word for it — and a new engine of knowledge began to turn.

    01 · Ayiti, 1492

    An island of chiefdoms

    On 12 October 1492 Columbus made landfall at Guanahaní — most likely San Salvador in the Bahamas, though the identification is still argued — in a Caribbean that was largely Taíno, from the Lucayan islands through Cuba, Xaymaca (Jamaica) and Borikén (Puerto Rico). Ayiti, the island Spain renamed Hispaniola, was divided among chiefdoms under their caciques: Marién, Maguá, Maguana, Xaraguá and Higüey. Villages grew cassava in mounded conucos, housed ancestral spirits in carved cemís, and gathered on stone-lined plazas, bateyes, for ceremony and the rubber-ball game; Caguana in Puerto Rico's highlands, laid out around 1270 CE, is among the largest that survive.

    02 · Collapse

    An island emptied in a generation

    Spain's first town in the Americas, La Isabela, rose on the north coast in 1494, and Santo Domingo on the south coast in the later 1490s. Under the encomienda, formalised in 1503, Taíno were forced into gold washing and field labour; that same year Governor Nicolás de Ovando massacred Xaraguá's assembled chiefs, and its ruler Anacaona was later hanged. The island's 1492 population is fiercely disputed — from tens of thousands at most (an upper bound inferred from ancient DNA in 2020, itself contested) to Las Casas's millions — but the collapse is not: Las Casas put the survivors near 60,000 by 1508, before smallpox arrived around 1518. From the Bahoruco mountains the cacique Enriquillo fought Spain from 1519 to a negotiated peace in 1533, while Las Casas, who gave up his own encomienda in 1514–15, spent half a century documenting the destruction.

    03 · Sugar

    The sugar frontier moves north

    Into the emptied islands came cane and captive Africans — Hispaniola was grinding sugar by the late 1510s — but the first great sugar economy rose in Brazil: by around 1600 the engenhos of Pernambuco and Bahia had made it the world's largest sugar producer. In the 1640s Barbados, English since 1627, turned from tobacco and cotton to cane with mills and methods learned from Dutch-held Pernambuco — the "sugar revolution" — and by 1684 it counted some 46,500 enslaved people to 19,600 white settlers. England seized Jamaica in 1655 and France secured western Hispaniola as Saint-Domingue in 1697; by the 1740s those two were the world's leading sugar producers, and by the 1780s Saint-Domingue alone grew about 40% of the sugar Europe consumed.

    04 · The African end

    Where the ships loaded

    Portugal built São Jorge da Mina at Elmina on the Gold Coast in 1482, at first to buy gold. Kongo's kings dealt with Lisbon as fellow Christian monarchs, while Luanda, founded in 1576, became Portugal's base for war on Ndongo — whose queen Njinga fought, allied with the Dutch and negotiated from her conquered realm of Matamba until her death in 1663. West Central Africa became the largest single source of captives, shipped above all to Brazil (Pernambuco drew most of its captives from Angola); Bahia bought heavily at Ouidah on the Bight of Benin, taken by Dahomey in 1727; Gold Coast captives went disproportionately to Barbados and Jamaica. From more than 36,000 voyages, the SlaveVoyages database estimates about 12.5 million people embarked between 1501 and 1866 and 10.7 million landed — more than 4.8 million of them in Brazil.

    05 · Resistance

    Freedom in the hills

    From the early 1600s, people who fled Pernambuco's cane fields built Palmares in the Serra da Barriga, a confederation of fortified towns of perhaps 10,000–20,000 people that beat back Dutch and Portuguese expeditions until its capital fell in 1694; its last leader, Zumbi, was killed on 20 November 1695, a date Brazil made a national holiday by a 2023 law. In Jamaica, Maroons descended from people who escaped at the English conquest of 1655, joined by generations of later runaways, fought Britain to a standstill: the Leeward Maroons of the Cockpit Country under Cudjoe (the Akan day-name Kojo), and the Windward Maroons of the Blue Mountains under Quao and Nanny. The treaties of 1739–40 recognised their towns and lands, Accompong among them, at the price of returning future runaways; Nanny, whose town was stormed in 1734, received her own land grant in 1740.

    Milestones

    Thirty-eight turning points

    by c. 1450

    The Haudenosaunee Great Law

    Eastern North America

    Five nations — Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca — were already federated under the Great Law of Peace, with clan mothers holding the power to seat and depose chiefs. Europeans arriving a century later met not wilderness but a working federal polity, whose beaver-pelt diplomacy in the Covenant Chain treated empires as negotiating equals.

    c. 1450

    Gutenberg's printing press

    Mainz, Rhineland

    Cheap, replicable text broke elite monopolies on knowledge — some 200 million books printed in Europe by 1600 — fuelling the Reformation, vernacular literacy, and the Scientific Revolution. Movable metal type was pioneered earlier still in Korea (the Jikji, 1377) and ceramic type in Song China (c. 1040).

    1453

    Fall of Constantinople

    Ottoman Empire

    Mehmed II's giant bombards breached walls that had stood a thousand years, ending Byzantium and announcing the age of gunpowder empires. Ottoman control of the eastern Mediterranean sharpened European incentives to seek ocean routes to Asia.

    c. 1450–1527

    The Italian Renaissance

    Florence · Rome · Venice

    Humanism, linear perspective, and figures like Leonardo, Michelangelo, and Raphael recentred art and inquiry on the observation of nature — a revival drawing on classical texts transmitted through Byzantine and Islamic scholarship and financed by Mediterranean banking.

    c. 1450 & 1721

    Greenland: the Norse vanish, the Inuit stay

    The Arctic · Kalaallit Nunaat

    The last written record of Norse Greenland is a wedding at Hvalsey church in 1408, and the youngest radiocarbon date yet reported from its settlements is around 1430 — by the later 1400s the colony was gone, while the Inuit, descendants of Thule hunters who had crossed from Canada a few centuries before, kept the coasts through the Little Ice Age. The Norwegian pastor Hans Egede landed in 1721 to find the lost Norse and convert them, found only Kalaallit, and founded a mission that moved to Godthåb (now Nuuk) in 1728 — the start of the Danish claim that still shapes Greenland. Twelve years on, smallpox, brought home on the 1733 supply ship by one of the mission's young Kalaallit converts, swept the coast around the colony — events known from Egede's own published record of the mission.

    c. 1464–1591

    Songhai & Timbuktu's golden age

    West Africa · Niger bend

    Under Sunni Ali and Askia Muhammad, Songhai became the largest empire in West African history, and Timbuktu's Sankore university and manuscript libraries made it a world centre of Islamic scholarship. Its fall to Moroccan musketeers at Tondibi (1591) is a stark emblem of the new gunpowder order.

    1491

    Kongo's Atlantic diplomacy

    West-Central Africa

    King Nzinga a Nkuwu accepted baptism as João I a year before Columbus sailed, and his successor Afonso I exchanged ambassadors with Lisbon and Rome for more than three decades. Afonso's surviving letters — including his 1526 protests against Portuguese slaving — are among the era's most important African-authored sources: an African state engaging the Atlantic world on its own terms, decades before any conquest.

    1492–1522

    The oceanic breakout

    Iberia · Atlantic · Indian Ocean

    Columbus's crossing (1492), da Gama's route to India (1498), and the first circumnavigation (1519–22) linked every inhabited continent by sea. Europeans were latecomers — Polynesians had settled the Pacific and Ming fleets had reached East Africa decades earlier — but this breakout redirected world trade toward the Atlantic.

    1492 onward

    The Columbian Exchange

    Americas ↔ Afro-Eurasia

    The greatest biological transfer in history: maize, potatoes, cassava, and cacao reshaped Old World diets while horses and livestock transformed the Americas. Old World diseases — smallpox above all — killed an estimated 80–90% of Indigenous Americans within a century, one of history's greatest demographic catastrophes.

    1501–1866

    The transatlantic slave trade

    Africa · Atlantic · Americas

    Roughly 12.5 million Africans were forced onto slave ships; about 10.7 million survived the Middle Passage to labour on plantations and in mines. The trade built Atlantic fortunes, devastated African societies, and created the African diaspora — inseparable from the rise of Atlantic capitalism. These are evidence-based estimates, not guesses: the SlaveVoyages database, built by David Eltis and colleagues from the manifests of more than 36,000 documented voyages, is one of history's great quantitative sources.

    1511 · 1621

    Melaka & the Banda massacre

    Southeast Asia

    Portugal stormed Melaka in 1511 and learned that Europeans could seize ports but not the trade — Malay, Gujarati, and Chinese merchants simply rerouted through Johor, Aceh, and Banten. The Dutch VOC tried totality instead: in 1621 Jan Pieterszoon Coen killed, enslaved, or deported nearly all of Banda's ~15,000 people to corner the world's nutmeg. The first multinational built its monopoly on demographic erasure.

    1511–1514

    Montesinos, the Laws of Burgos & the 1514 count

    The Caribbean · Hispaniola

    On 21 December 1511 the Dominican Antonio de Montesinos denounced Santo Domingo's colonists from the pulpit, telling them their treatment of the Taíno was sin and asking whether these were not human beings with souls. The Crown replied with the Laws of Burgos (27 December 1512), the first code written to govern Spaniards' conduct towards Indigenous people in the Americas — one that regulated forced labour rather than ending it. Two years later the repartimiento of 1514, the roll that parcelled the survivors out among colonists, listed just 26,334 Taíno, less than half the 60,000 Las Casas reported for 1508: the collapse measured in an official register before smallpox had even arrived.

    1517–1648

    Reformation & Counter-Reformation

    Wittenberg · Northern Europe

    A print-amplified protest against indulgences shattered Latin Christendom into rival confessions, spread vernacular literacy through translated Bibles, and provoked the Catholic Counter-Reformation. A century of religious war ended at Westphalia (1648), conventionally — if over-simply — cited as the origin of the modern state system.

    1519–1533

    Conquest of the Aztec & Inca

    Mesoamerica · the Andes

    Cortés took Tenochtitlan (1521) — a lake city larger than most in Europe — and Pizarro seized the Inca Tawantinsuyu after Cajamarca (1532), aided decisively by smallpox and Indigenous allies such as Tlaxcala. Sophisticated American states were folded into Spain's empire.

    1526–1707

    The Mughal Empire at its height

    South Asia

    Babur's field artillery won Panipat, founding a gunpowder empire that under Akbar practised religious pluralism and by 1700 produced roughly a quarter of world GDP — the workshop of the world for textiles. Its architectural summit, the Taj Mahal (1632–53), remains the era's most famous building.

    1529–1543

    Ethiopia's war with Adal

    The Horn of Africa

    Imam Ahmad ibn Ibrahim of Adal broke the Christian emperor's army at Shimbra Kure in 1529 and overran some three-quarters of Christian Ethiopia, helped, especially later in the war, by Ottoman guns and musketeers; in 1541 Portugal landed 400 musketeers under Cristóvão da Gama, Vasco's son, and in February 1543 the Imam was killed at Wayna Daga. Gunpowder reached the Horn on both sides at once, and the war became a front in the Ottoman–Portuguese contest for the Indian Ocean. We can read it from both camps — the Adal chronicle Futuh al-Habasha and the Portuguese narrative of Miguel de Castanhoso — and Fasilides' new capital at Gondar (c. 1636), whose castles still stand, marks the recovery.

    1543

    Copernicus, De revolutionibus

    Frombork, Poland

    By placing the Sun at the centre of the cosmos, Copernicus began the Scientific Revolution's demotion of Earth — and of received authority — as Vesalius's anatomy, published the same year, did for the human body. Both built on centuries of Islamic astronomy and medicine.

    1545–1571 →

    Potosí silver & the Manila galleons

    Andes · Pacific · Ming China

    Cerro Rico's silver — mined largely by coerced Indigenous mita labour and enslaved Africans — flowed east to Seville and west via the Acapulco–Manila galleons to China, where silver-based Ming taxation created insatiable demand. For the first time one commodity linked every continent in a single circuit.

    1545–1548

    Cocoliztli, the great pestilence

    Mesoamerica · New Spain

    A generation after smallpox, a haemorrhagic fever the Nahua called cocoliztli — 'pestilence' — swept New Spain; estimates of the dead run from 5 to 15 million, some 800,000 of them in the Valley of Mexico. In 2018 ancient DNA from the teeth of victims buried at Teposcolula-Yucundaa, Oaxaca, yielded Salmonella enterica Paratyphi C, an enteric fever — the first pathogen recovered for a conquest-era epidemic, though some specialists doubt it explains the bleeding the chroniclers describe. The Great Dying was many diseases, arriving in waves on people already worn down by war and forced labour.

    1571–1603

    Manila & the Sangley massacre

    Southeast Asia · Luzon

    Legazpi made Manila Spain's Asian capital in 1571, but the silver-for-silk exchange that kept it alive was run by Chinese merchants, mostly Hokkien — the Sangleys — who far outnumbered the Spanish residents: an estimated 10,000 to some 2,000 by 1586, and perhaps 25,000 Chinese by 1603. In October 1603, after a Chinese uprising, Spanish troops with Japanese residents and Tagalog auxiliaries killed an estimated 15,000–25,000 Chinese; trade soon resumed, because Manila could not survive without it. Further massacres followed in 1639 and 1662: the diaspora that made the galleon circuit work was also its recurring target.

    1577

    The Florentine Codex

    Mesoamerica · Tlatelolco

    For two decades the Franciscan Bernardino de Sahagún worked with Nahua elders, authors and artists — several trained at the Colegio de Santa Cruz in Tlatelolco — to record Mexica religion, medicine, natural history, and an Indigenous account of the conquest. Copied out in its final bilingual form by 1577 — twelve books in parallel columns of Nahuatl and Spanish, with nearly 2,500 painted images — it is widely regarded as the richest single source on Mexica life: Indigenous knowledge set down in alphabetic script largely by Indigenous hands. It reached Europe and the Medici library in Florence, hence its name; the Getty put a searchable digital edition online in 2023.

    1582–1639

    Russia crosses Siberia

    North Asia · the Arctic

    From Yermak's crossing of the Urals to Moskvitin's Cossacks standing on the Pacific shore (1639) took just 57 years — the outpost at Okhotsk followed in 1647, fortified in 1649 — an imperial expansion the size of Spain's American conquest, financed by yasak, a fur tribute exacted from Khanty, Evenki, Sakha, and dozens of other peoples. Running on sable instead of silver, it is the conquest Atlantic-centred world histories routinely omit.

    1592–1598

    The Imjin War

    East Asia · Korea

    Toyotomi Hideyoshi sent some 158,000 soldiers, labourers and porters into Joseon Korea as the road to conquering Ming China — one of the largest wars of its century anywhere. Admiral Yi Sun-sin's fleet cut Japanese supply lines (Hansan, 1592; Myeongnyang, 1597), Ming armies intervened at ruinous cost, and some 50,000–60,000 Koreans were carried off to Japan, among them potters credited with founding Japanese porcelain at Arita. Yi's war diary, the Nanjung Ilgi, is on UNESCO's Memory of the World register; the Mimizuka mound in Kyoto holds noses cut from Korean and Chinese dead.

    1593–1698

    Fort Jesus & the Swahili coast

    East Africa · Mombasa

    Portugal built Fort Jesus at Mombasa in 1593–96 to police the Swahili coast; a century later Oman's imam Saif bin Sultan besieged it from 1696 until it fell in 1698, after almost three years. The loss ended Portuguese power on the coast north of Mozambique — a return in 1728–29 lasted barely a year — and began the Omani ascendancy that would later make Zanzibar the seat of an Omani sultanate. The fort, designed by the Italian engineer Giovanni Battista Cairati, still stands, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2011: Europeans could take a port, but the western Indian Ocean stayed an African, Arab and Indian sea.

    1598–1641

    The Mapuche hold the Biobío

    South America · southern Chile

    At Curalaba on 23 December 1598 Mapuche forces killed Chile's governor, Martín García Óñez de Loyola, and in the years that followed destroyed seven Spanish towns south of the Biobío River. At the Parliament of Quillín in 1641 Spain recognised Mapuche autonomy beyond the river, in exchange for loyalty against foreign powers — a recognition rare for any Indigenous people in the Americas — and the frontier held until Chile's conquest of Araucanía in the 1880s. Spanish America was never conquered whole; the soldier-poet Alonso de Ercilla's epic La Araucana had already made the Mapuche war famous in Europe.

    1600–1639

    Tokugawa unification of Japan

    Japan

    After a century of civil war in which Japan became a leading gun producer, the Tokugawa shogunate imposed two and a half centuries of peace and strictly managed foreign trade through four gateways — Nagasaki for the Dutch and Chinese, Tsushima for Korea, Satsuma for Ryukyu, Matsumae for the Ainu. Edo grew into one of the largest cities on Earth.

    1602 · 1694

    The VOC & corporate capitalism

    Amsterdam · Dutch trading world

    The Dutch East India Company was the first joint-stock corporation with freely tradable shares, spawning the Amsterdam exchange and the Bank of Amsterdam (1609); the Bank of England (1694) institutionalised national debt. These tools financed both prosperity and violent colonial monopolies.

    c. 1605–1694

    Palmares, a fugitive state

    Pernambuco, Brazil

    In the hills behind Brazil's sugar coast, escaped Africans built a confederation of walled towns — perhaps 10,000–20,000 people under Ganga Zumba and then Zumbi — that repelled Dutch and Portuguese expeditions for decades, as maroon communities did across Jamaica and the Guianas. The enslaved did not only endure the plantation system; they built rival polities inside it.

    1606 & c. 1700

    Australia's two contacts

    Cape York · Arnhem Land

    Willem Janszoon's Duyfken made Europe's first recorded Australian landfall in 1606, and Tasman followed in 1642 — but sustained contact came from the north: Macassan fleets from Sulawesi moored in Arnhem Land each monsoon from around 1700 to cure trepang for Chinese kitchens. It was regular, negotiated trade, put on an archaeological footing by Campbell Macknight's excavations of Macassan hearths and pottery at Anuru Bay — and how early it began is still argued: a Bayesian model of 18 radiocarbon dates from Malara there gives an 80% probability of first use around 1637 (Wesley and colleagues, 2016), while document-based chronologies favour a start in the 1700s.

    1607–1678

    Jamestown, Plymouth & King Philip's War

    Eastern North America

    English footholds at Jamestown (1607) and Plymouth (1620) survived their first winters on Powhatan and Wampanoag corn and diplomacy — then tobacco and the fur trade made them expansive. By 1675 that expansion brought King Philip's War, proportionally among the deadliest conflicts in American history. The settler-colonial engine that dominates Chapter VIII starts here, small and dependent.

    1624–1663

    Njinga of Ndongo & Matamba

    West-Central Africa

    An Ambundu queen who fought, allied with, and out-negotiated Portugal for four decades — leading armies into her sixties and securing a 1656 treaty on her own terms. Njinga is the era's clearest proof that African resistance to the slave trade was statecraft, not only flight or revolt — and that women commanded states in this century, not just households.

    1642–1722

    Pacific worlds & Oceania

    Aotearoa · Tonga · Rapa Nui

    Tasman charted Tasmania, Aotearoa, Tonga, and Fiji (1642–43) and Roggeveen reached Rapa Nui (1722) — first contacts with societies whose ancestors had already achieved history's greatest feat of open-ocean navigation. Māori, Tongan, and Rapa Nui worlds were thriving and complex, not "discoveries" — and Rapa Nui was no society in ruins: fifteen ancient genomes (Moreno-Mayar and colleagues, 2024) show no population crash in the 1600s, and about 10% Native American ancestry from contact around 1250–1430.

    1644–1722

    The Qing conquest & Kangxi era

    East Asia

    The Manchu Qing replaced the Ming and, under the Kangxi Emperor, ruled — with Mughal India — one of the world's two most populous states and largest economies; over the next century the Qing more than doubled the Ming's territory, while exporting porcelain, silk, and tea. Jesuit astronomers at court embodied genuine two-way exchange between Europe and China.

    1652–1713

    The Cape: a supply post becomes a colony

    Southern Africa

    The Dutch East India Company planted a supply station at Table Bay under Jan van Riebeeck in 1652 to feed its Asia fleets; within a few years settlers were farming Khoikhoi grazing land, and two wars (1659–60 and 1673–77) pushed the Khoikhoi back. Settlers and Company imported enslaved people from Madagascar, Mozambique, and South and Southeast Asia, making the Cape an Indian Ocean slave society, and the smallpox epidemic of 1713 broke much of what remained of Khoikhoi society. Van Riebeeck's daily journal and the Company's own archives record it as it happened.

    1668–1699

    The Spanish–Chamorro wars

    Oceania · the Mariana Islands

    Guam was the Manila galleon's Pacific way-station, and in 1668 the Jesuit Diego Luis de San Vitores founded a mission there; by 1670 Chamorros were fighting it, and San Vitores was killed in 1672. Nearly three decades of revolt and reprisal ended only in 1698–99, when the last northern islanders were shipped to Guam; by then the Chamorros had been forcibly resettled into a handful of mission villages, and the latte-stone house platforms that had defined Marianas life for centuries fell out of use. Estimates put Guam's people at perhaps 35,000–50,000 in 1668 (the figure is contested); war, relocation, and galleon-borne epidemics cut their numbers to a few thousand within a few decades — the Columbian catastrophe replayed in the Pacific a century before Cook.

    1680 →

    The Pueblo Revolt & the horse frontier

    Rio Grande · the Great Plains

    Po'pay coordinated more than two dozen pueblos — runners carried knotted cords counting down the date — and drove Spain from New Mexico for twelve years: the most successful Indigenous uprising in North American history, and Spain returned only by conceding Pueblo religious practice. Horses dispersing from the abandoned ranches spread north, and within two generations Comanche and Lakota had built wholly new mounted societies.

    1687–1757

    The Qing–Zunghar wars

    Central Asia

    Kangxi's cannon broke Galdan's Zunghars at Jao Modo (1696), and by the 1750s Qianlong's armies had destroyed the last nomadic empire through campaign, famine, and massacre. Two and a half thousand years of steppe power driving Eurasian history — Scythians to Xiongnu to Chinggis — ended here, the steppe itself carved up between Qing and Romanov surveyors while the Uzbek khanates held the old Silk Road cities.

    1721

    Inoculation reaches London & Boston

    Africa · Istanbul · London · Boston

    Protecting people by deliberately giving them a mild case of smallpox was an Asian, African, and Ottoman practice long before Europe adopted it: it is documented in China by the 16th century, and the Kangxi Emperor had his children and troops inoculated. Lady Mary Wortley Montagu watched it done in Istanbul, had her son inoculated there in 1718 and her daughter in London in April 1721; that year in Boston, Cotton Mather — who had learned the method from Onesimus, an African man enslaved in his household — persuaded the physician Zabdiel Boylston to try it during an epidemic. Boylston's published counts showed about 2% of the inoculated dying against some 14% of natural cases: one of the first numerical tests of a medical treatment rested on knowledge carried from Africa and Asia.

    Why it happened

    Germs, weather, and unfree hands

    The fusing of the hemispheres was decided less by captains than by three impersonal engines: an immunity gap set 10,000 years earlier, a cooling climate, and labour taken by force on every continent.

    The disease asymmetry of 1492 was not chance. Smallpox, measles, and influenza are crowd diseases — zoonoses that could persist only in the dense, animal-adjacent populations of Afro-Eurasia: measles from cattle rinderpest (a split now dated to around the 6th century BCE), influenza from birds and pigs, and smallpox from no barnyard at all — genomics shows its closest relatives are a gerbil poxvirus and camelpox, and every known strain shares an ancestor only about 1,700 years old. The Americas' only large domesticates were the llama and alpaca, so the hand was dealt back in Chapter III, by which continents happened to hold herd animals worth taming. When the hemispheres met, immunity ran one way. Scholarly estimates put the Americas' population in 1492 anywhere from ~40 to ~110 million; by 1650 it may have been a tenth of that. The range is wide because the epidemics travelled faster than any record-keeper — but every serious estimate describes one of the greatest catastrophes in the human record.

    Nor was it one disease, or disease alone. Epidemics came in waves — smallpox in 1520, then the cocoliztli of 1545–48, for which ancient DNA has now offered a candidate pathogen — and each struck people already uprooted by war, flight, famine, and forced labour, whose untended fields could not feed the survivors. The catastrophe is even legible in the air. Antarctic ice records atmospheric CO₂ falling by 7–10 ppm between the late 1500s and the early 1600s. From 119 regional estimates, Alexander Koch, Mark Maslin and colleagues (2019) put the Americas at about 60 million people in 1492 (interquartile range 45–78 million), 90% of them gone within a century. Forest reclaiming some 56 million hectares of abandoned fields, they calculate, drew down about 3.5 ppm on its own; add the carbon-cycle feedbacks and land-use change elsewhere, and the land took up some 5 ppm, roughly half to two-thirds of that CO₂ dip — a human fingerprint on the global climate two centuries before coal, and the reason 1610 is one candidate start for the Anthropocene (Chapter IX).

    Then the weather turned. The Little Ice Age was already centuries old, and a run of great eruptions deepened it: Huaynaputina in Peru exploded in February 1600, the largest eruption in South America's recorded history, and the Russian famine of 1601–03, which killed perhaps two million people, has been linked to its volcanic winter; Mount Parker in the Philippines followed in 1641. Between 1645 and 1715 sunspots all but vanished — the Maunder Minimum — though climate models now give that dimmer sun only a supporting role, with volcanoes doing more of the work. Tree rings record some of the coldest summers of the millennium (the climate spine tracks it). The mid-century "General Crisis" followed: drought and famine helped bring down the Ming in 1644, the Thirty Years' War killed perhaps a third of the German lands' people, and the 1640s alone saw revolts or civil wars in England, France, Spain, Naples, Ukraine, and the Ottoman palace. Geoffrey Parker's argument — that a single climate shock stressed harvest-dependent states everywhere at once — is among the strongest cases ever made for climate as a driver of history.

    Beneath both ran demography and coercion. World population grew from roughly half a billion in 1500 to some 700–800 million by 1750 (the population curve) — growth fed by American crops: maize and sweet potato in China, cassava in West Africa, the potato in northern Europe. Much of the era's wealth, though, was produced by people given no choice: 12.5 million enslaved Africans; the Potosí mita conscripting some 13,000 Andeans a year; encomienda tribute; Russian serfdom, fixed to the land by the 1649 law code; and indentured servants across the Atlantic. Slave ships carried roughly two African men for every woman, skewing societies on both shores — while Europe's witch trials, peaking 1560–1630, executed perhaps 40,000–60,000 people, three-quarters or more of them women. Njinga's four decades of war and treaty, and the Haudenosaunee clan mothers seating chiefs, are the reminder that women also exercised this era's power, not only suffered its persecutions.

    Unfreedom was also being written into law, on several continents within a century and a half. Spain's Laws of Burgos (1512) and New Laws (1542) regulated Indigenous forced labour without ending it — and the New Laws provoked a settler revolt in Peru. Barbados's slave code of 1661 became the template for Jamaica's and Carolina's; France codified plantation slavery in the Code Noir of 1685. Captive markets ran in other seas too: Cape Town's slaves came from Madagascar, Mozambique, and South and Southeast Asia, and in the Mediterranean the corsairs of Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli sold European captives — a million or more between the 1500s and the mid-1700s on Robert Davis's contested estimate — while Muslim captives rowed the galleys of Malta and Tuscany. Coercion was the era's common technology, not a European peculiarity; what was new was its Atlantic scale and its racial logic.

    Gender set the pace of growth as well as the terms of power. West of a line from Trieste to St Petersburg, women typically married in their mid-twenties and a substantial minority never married at all — the "European marriage pattern" John Hajnal described in 1965. Late marriage acted as a brake: marriages, and so births, rose and fell with wages and harvests, and it sent young women into years of wage-earning service before they wed; whether it also helped that corner of the world pull ahead economically is one of the live arguments below.

    By 1750 the world was joined but not yet transformed: energy still came from muscle, wood, wind, and falling water, and no economy had escaped the harvest — though since 1712 a Newcomen steam engine had been pumping water from a coal mine at Tipton in England's Black Country. Chapter VIII is what happened when one corner of this silver-linked world learned to burn its way out.

    Competing explanations

    • Guns, germs, or allies? Tenochtitlan fell to smallpox and to tens of thousands of Tlaxcalan soldiers; Cajamarca exploited an Inca civil war. Historians argue over the weights, not the ingredients — and stress that colonial violence and forced labour compounded the epidemics rather than merely following them.
    • Was the General Crisis really climatic? Tokugawa Japan and Mughal India weathered the same cold without collapse — granaries and institutions decided who broke, so the Little Ice Age was a stress test, not a verdict. See what remains contested.
    • Why an Iberian breakout and not a Ming one? Zheng He's fleets dwarfed anything in Lisbon; the difference was incentive, not capability — in fragmented, competitive Europe a rejected Columbus could shop for a rival patron, while a unified court could simply rule the ocean not worth the cost.
    • How many people lived in the Americas in 1492? Serious estimates span ~40 to ~110 million because the epidemics outran every census; the width of that range is itself a measure of how fast the catastrophe moved.
    • Did guns make states, or states make guns? Michael Roberts (1955) and Geoffrey Parker (1988) argued that a "military revolution" — massed firearms, drilled infantry, the angled-bastion fortress — forced rulers to build the tax-hungry states that later conquered much of the world. Critics answer that the change was slower and patchier, and that the Ottomans, Mughals, and Qing mastered the same weapons without Europe's outcome.
    • Did the slave trades underdevelop Africa? Walter Rodney (1972) argued that they drained the continent; John Thornton (1992) stressed that African rulers and merchants set the terms in the early centuries. Nathan Nunn (2008) found that the countries from which the most people were taken are among the poorest today — a robust correlation whose mechanisms are still argued.
    • Did late marriage make Europe rich? Tine de Moor and Jan Luiten van Zanden's "girl power" thesis links the European marriage pattern to north-western Europe's early growth; Tracy Dennison and Sheilagh Ogilvie (2014), testing it across 39 countries, found its most extreme forms went with economic stagnation instead.
    • The era argued with itself. At Valladolid in 1550–51, Bartolomé de las Casas and Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda debated before a royal panel whether Spain's wars on Indigenous Americans could be just. The panel never issued a collective verdict, and the conquests went on.

    Meanwhile, everywhere

    Sixteen regions, one tightening web

    The ships made the connections; they did not make most of the history. In every region the forces that mattered most were still its own states, faiths, harvests and wars — even as silver, sugar, guns and disease reached a little further inland with every decade.

    West Asia

    1453–1736

    Two gunpowder empires split the region along a new religious frontier. Ismail I made Twelver Shiʿism the faith of Safavid Iran from 1501; Ottoman cannon and janissary muskets beat his cavalry at Chaldiran in 1514, and in 1516–17 Selim I took Mamluk Syria, Egypt and the Hijaz. Under Süleyman (r. 1520–66) the architect Sinan raised the Süleymaniye, Istanbul's first coffeehouses opened around 1555, and Shah Abbas I made Isfahan his capital in 1598, settling deported Armenian silk merchants at New Julfa. An Afghan army took Isfahan in 1722, and Safavid rule ended in 1736.

    North Africa & the Nile

    1504–1727

    Ottoman armies made Egypt a province in 1517, ending the Mamluk sultanate under the eyes of the Cairo chronicler Ibn Iyas, and Algiers, Tunis and Tripoli became Ottoman regencies whose corsairs raided and ransomed across the Mediterranean. Morocco stayed independent: at Wadi al-Makhazin in 1578 the Saʿdians destroyed a Portuguese army and killed King Sebastian, and months later Ahmad al-Mansur began the El Badi palace in Marrakesh, paying for its Italian marble with its weight in sugar. Up the Nile the Funj sultanate of Sennar, founded in 1504, succeeded the medieval Christian kingdoms of Nubia; Moulay Ismaʿil (r. 1672–1727) built his capital at Meknes with an army of enslaved Black soldiers, the ʿAbid al-Bukhari.

    West & Central Africa

    c. 1464–1728

    Songhai ruled the Niger bend from Gao and Timbuktu until Moroccan musketeers broke it at Tondibi in 1591; to the east, Idris Alooma of Kanem–Bornu (r. c. 1570–1603) hired Turkish instructors to train his gunmen. Oyo's cavalry made it the largest Yoruba state, strong enough to invade Dahomey in 1728, and Benin's guild brasscasters melted Rhineland manillas into the palace plaques. On the Atlantic side, Kongo's Christian kings wrote to Lisbon and Rome while Portugal's Luanda made war on Ndongo; by the early 1700s Asante, bound together by the Golden Stool, and Dahomey, master of Ouidah from 1727, were the rising powers.

    East & Southern Africa

    1498–1713

    Portugal sacked Kilwa and Mombasa in 1505 and policed the Swahili coast from Fort Jesus (1593) until Oman took it in 1698. Christian Ethiopia survived the Adal war of 1529–43 only with Portuguese musketeers, then met the Oromo migrations that the monk Bahrey chronicled in 1593, and rebuilt its court at Gondar from about 1636. On the Zimbabwe plateau Mutapa became a Portuguese vassal in 1629, until Changamire Dombo's Rozvi drove the Portuguese off the plateau in the 1690s; at the Cape, the VOC's 1652 supply post grew into a slave colony on Khoikhoi land.

    Europe & the Mediterranean

    1453–1721

    Castile took Granada and expelled Spain's Jews in 1492, and expelled the Moriscos in 1609–14; the Ottomans won at Mohács in 1526 and besieged Vienna in 1529 and again in 1683. Print carried the Reformation and a century of religious war — the Thirty Years' War (1618–48) killed perhaps a third of the German lands' people — while Amsterdam's exchange, bank and trading companies made the Dutch Republic the hub of European commerce. In the east, Muscovy tied its peasants to the land in 1649, and Peter I proclaimed a Russian Empire in 1721.

    Central Asia & the Steppe

    c. 1465–1757

    The Kazakh Khanate took shape around 1465, and Muhammad Shaybani's Uzbeks took Samarkand in 1500 and Bukhara in 1501, pushing Babur south towards India. Muscovy annexed Kazan (1552) and Astrakhan (1556), the Kalmyks rode west to the lower Volga in 1630, and the Zunghars built the last great steppe empire: their 1723 invasion devastated the Kazakhs, whose Junior Jüz khan swore allegiance to Russia in 1731, before Qing armies destroyed the Zunghars in the later 1750s. Captives were steppe currency too — in 1722 Bukhara held more than 5,000 Russian prisoners.

    South Asia

    1498–1739

    The Mughals were one power among several. Portugal seized Goa in 1510; Vijayanagara's great capital at Hampi was sacked after the battle of Talikota in 1565; in Punjab, Guru Nanak (1469–1539) founded the Sikh tradition, and Guru Gobind Singh created the Khalsa in 1699. Shivaji was crowned Maratha king in 1674 and Aurangzeb's quarter-century of Deccan wars (1681–1707) exhausted the empire, while the English East India Company's posts at Surat, Madras and Calcutta (1690) were still minor players when Nader Shah sacked Delhi in 1739 and carried off the Peacock Throne.

    East Asia

    1450–1722

    Ming China ran on silver: the Single Whip reforms of the 1570s–80s put taxes in silver and drew in the metal of Potosí and Japan. Ming armies helped save Joseon Korea from Hideyoshi's invasions (1592–98), but drought, rebellion and the Manchus brought the dynasty down in 1644, after Manchu armies had twice invaded Korea (1627, 1636–37). Japan passed from a century of civil war to the Tokugawa peace (1603), crushed the largely Christian Shimabara rising (1637–38) and ran foreign trade through four gateways; Li Shizhen's pharmacopoeia (completed 1578) and Song Yingxing's Tiangong kaiwu (1637) catalogued Chinese medicine and technology.

    Southeast Asia

    1511–1740

    After Portugal took Melaka in 1511, the rerouted trade built up Aceh — strongest under Iskandar Muda (1607–36) — Johor and Banten, and Islam spread through the archipelago's port courts. On the mainland, Toungoo Burma took Ayutthaya in 1569; it regained its independence in 1584 and became a great entrepôt, while Vietnam split between the Trịnh and Nguyễn lords, at war from 1627 to 1672. The Dutch founded Batavia in 1619 and survived Sultan Agung of Mataram's sieges of 1628 and 1629; in 1740 Company troops and their allies massacred at least 10,000 of Batavia's Chinese residents.

    Australia

    c. 1450–1750

    Hundreds of Aboriginal nations, speaking some 250 languages, managed the whole continent as Country: fire-stick burning kept grasslands open for game, the Gunditjmara still worked their eel channels at Budj Bim, and songlines carried knowledge of routes, water and law across great distances. Torres Strait Islanders traded by canoe with New Guinea. Europeans touched only the edges — Janszoon's Duyfken and Torres in 1606, Tasman in 1642, Dampier on the north-west coast in 1688 — while Macassan trepangers from Sulawesi began their seasonal visits to Arnhem Land.

    Oceania & the Pacific

    1450–1722

    The Pacific was still being shaped by its own peoples: in Aotearoa, iwi guarded kūmara stores and gardens with terraced pā hillforts — more than 5,000 sites are known — and on Rapa Nui a population that ancient genomes show had not crashed kept farming its stone-mulched gardens. European ships came rarely and violently: Mendaña's 1595 expedition killed an estimated 200 Marquesans in two weeks, Quirós claimed Vanuatu's Espíritu Santo for Spain in 1606 and sailed away within six weeks, and Māori canoes drove Tasman from Golden Bay in 1642, killing four of his crew. Only the Marianas, on the Manila galleon route, were conquered (1668–99).

    The Arctic

    c. 1450–1728

    Inuit held Greenland's coasts after the Norse colony died out in the 1400s; on Baffin Island in 1577 Martin Frobisher seized three Inuit — Kalicho, Arnaq and her baby Nutaaq — and all three died soon after reaching England. Sámi herders and fishers could owe tax to Norway, Sweden and Russia at once, and Lutheran missions burned their drums. Across Siberia the fur-tribute state reached the Lena (Yakutsk, 1632) and the lands of the Chukchi, and in 1728 Vitus Bering sailed the strait that Semyon Dezhnev had passed in 1648. In Greenland the missionary Hans Egede landed near today's Nuuk in 1721, and in 1728 his mission moved to the new colony of Godthåb, the start of the Danish claim.

    North America

    c. 1450–1729

    The great Mississippian towns were past their peak, but paramount chiefdoms such as Coosa — perhaps 50,000 people across several allied polities — still flourished when Hernando de Soto's army marched through in 1540; within decades disease had emptied its core. The Haudenosaunee fought the Beaver Wars and made the Great Peace of Montreal of 1701, signed by New France and 39 Indigenous nations including their own; Spain founded St Augustine in 1565 and lost New Mexico to the Pueblo Revolt of 1680; horses remade the Plains. As English colonies spread along the coast, the Yamasee War (1715–17) and the Natchez revolt (1729) showed the South's nations fighting back.

    Mesoamerica

    1486–1697

    Under Ahuitzotl (1486–1502) the Triple Alliance more than doubled its dominion, south along the Pacific coast into western Guatemala; Tenochtitlan was among the largest cities on Earth when Cortés and his Indigenous allies took it in 1521. New Spain grew rich on Zacatecas silver (from 1546) and cochineal red, its second-most-valuable export; Nahua towns kept annals, wills and land records in Nahuatl written in Latin letters, and Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz wrote in Mexico City. The Itza Maya capital of Nojpetén held out until 1697 — 176 years after Tenochtitlan.

    The Caribbean

    1492–1740

    The Taíno world of the Greater Antilles collapsed within a generation under the encomienda and epidemics, and Spain's islands became cattle ranches and harbours — Havana gathered the treasure fleets. The Kalinago held the Lesser Antilles longer: in 1660 England and France recognised Dominica and St Vincent as theirs, though the English soon broke the treaty. From the 1640s sugar turned Barbados, then Jamaica (English from 1655) and Saint-Domingue (French from 1697), into majority-African slave societies; Port Royal, Jamaica's buccaneer capital, slid into the sea in the 1692 earthquake, and the Maroons won treaties in 1739–40.

    South America

    c. 1450–1750

    The Inca state spread from Cusco until some 40,000 km of roads bound nearly 2 million km² of the Andes; after Cajamarca (1532) a Neo-Inca state held out at Vilcabamba until 1572, when Viceroy Toledo executed Túpac Amaru and organised the Potosí mita. Brazil grew on sugar and captive labour — first Tupi-speaking peoples, then Africans — while Jesuit missions gathered Guaraní communities from 1609, São Paulo's bandeirantes raided them for slaves, and in 1695 a bandeirante struck the gold of Minas Gerais. South of the Biobío, the Mapuche kept their independence.

    Ordinary lives

    Four people the record kept

    Volcán Llullaillaco, Salta, Argentina · c. 1500

    A girl given to the mountain

    Around 1500, three children were entombed in a shrine near the 6,739 m summit of Llullaillaco as a capacocha, the Inca state's rite of child offering. The eldest, a girl of about 13, was probably an aclla, one of the "chosen women" taken from their families into the state's service. Her tightly braided hair kept a year-long record: her coca use rose sharply about twelve months before her death and peaked about six months before it, her alcohol intake peaked in her final weeks, and her diet shifted towards maize. She died with a coca quid still between her teeth. Whether she consented, complied or was sedated is the question the evidence forces — and whether she should be displayed in Salta's museum is argued by Indigenous leaders today.

    How we know Excavation in 1999 (Johan Reinhard and María Constanza Ceruti); CT scans; segment-by-segment analysis of scalp hair for coca and alcohol metabolites and for carbon and nitrogen isotopes (Wilson and colleagues, PNAS 2007 and 2013).

    Mexico City, New Spain · 16th century

    Three Africans in a hospital grave

    In a mass burial at the Royal Hospital of San José de los Naturales — founded in the early 1530s to treat the city's Indigenous people — archaeologists found three people whose bodies carried a longer journey. Their skeletons, their genomes and the strontium in their tooth enamel all point to birth in sub-Saharan Africa: they were first-generation Africans in New Spain, almost certainly brought in bondage, as most Africans in the early colony were. Their bones even kept their infections — complete genomes of yaws and hepatitis B closest to strains circulating in West Africa today, evidence that the slave trade carried pathogens across the Atlantic along with people. Archives mostly record such arrivals in sparse administrative detail; these three are individuals.

    How we know Ancient genomes, osteology, strontium isotopes from tooth enamel, carbon and nitrogen isotopes from dentine, and recovered pathogen genomes (Barquera and colleagues, Current Biology 2020).

    Andong, Joseon Korea · 1586

    Wŏn's mother writes to her husband

    When Yi Ŭngt'ae, of a local gentry family, died at 31 in 1586 — perhaps in an epidemic — his pregnant wife wrote him a letter in vernacular Korean, in the hangul script, and laid it on his chest in the coffin. Her name is not in the family records; she is remembered through her child's, as Wŏn's mother. She also buried a pair of sandals woven from hemp and her own hair, wrapped in a note saying she had made them from her hair and that he had died before he could wear them. The grave clothes put him at about 180 cm and her at about 160; the letter addresses him as an equal, and the couple probably lived with her family — a relative equality between spouses that Korean elite custom was abandoning by the 1600s.

    How we know Rescue excavation of the tomb by Andong National University in April 1998, before relocation; the letter itself, handwriting-matched notes, and some forty to fifty garments preserved in the grave.

    Jaunpur & Agra, Mughal India · 1586–1643

    Banarasidas, a merchant who wrote himself down

    Born around 1586 into a Shrimal Jain jeweller's family in Jaunpur, Banarasidas learned letters and numbers from local Brahmin teachers, moved to Agra to trade in 1610–11, and suffered repeated business losses. In 1641, aged 55, he set down his life in Braj Bhasha verse as the Ardhakathānak, the "half story" — half of the 110 years Jain tradition allots a full life — the earliest known autobiography in an Indian language. It records what court chronicles leave out: fear among the wealthy when Akbar died in 1605, flight from the plague that struck Agra under Jahangir, and grief — he married three times and saw all nine of his children die.

    How we know His own text, completed at Agra in 1641 and translated into English by Mukund Lath (Half a Tale, 1981) and Rohini Chowdhury (2009): a single, self-interested witness, but one with no court to flatter.

    How we know

    • Parish registers and family reconstitution — baptisms, marriages, and burials let historical demographers (Wrigley and Schofield's Cambridge Group) rebuild European populations parish by parish; Qing household registers and Spanish tribute counts do parallel work.
    • Tree rings, frost rings, and sunspot records — dendrochronology dates the Little Ice Age's cold summers year by year, and archived telescope observations let John A. Eddy identify the Maunder Minimum (1645–1715) in 1976.
    • Pollution as archive — smelting at Potosí left lead and antimony spikes in Andean ice and lake sediments, while Ming tax codes and Chinese price series record the silver arriving: the circuit is legible at both ends.
    • Ship manifests as statistics — the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database (SlaveVoyages) aggregates over 36,000 documented voyages, which is why 12.5 million embarked and 10.7 million landed are evidence-based estimates, not rhetoric.
    • Ancient pathogen DNA — teeth from the Teposcolula-Yucundaa cemetery in Oaxaca yielded Salmonella enterica Paratyphi C genomes (Vågene and colleagues, 2018), the first pathogen recovered for a conquest-era epidemic. The limits: screening finds only what survives in teeth and what the method looks for, and some specialists doubt this bacterium matches the symptoms chroniclers described for cocoliztli.
    • Ice cores and carbon budgets — the CO₂ dip of the late 1500s and early 1600s is measured in Antarctic ice; how much of it the regrowing American forests caused is a model (Koch and colleagues, 2019), and every step of it, from 1492 population to hectares abandoned, carries a wide interquartile range.
    • Bayesian radiocarbon — at Malara (Anuru Bay) in Arnhem Land, 18 radiocarbon dates modelled together give an 80% probability that Indonesian trepangers first used the site around 1637 (Wesley and colleagues, 2016). But "old" wood and the flat, wiggly calibration curve of the last few centuries blur such dates, which is why the long and short chronologies of Macassan contact are both still defended.
    • Ancient genomes and isotopes — fifteen Rapanui genomes (2024) show no population crash in the 1600s, and lead isotopes and trace elements in Benin's brass plaques match manillas made in the Rhineland (2023): chemistry now tests stories that once rested on travellers' impressions.
    • Archival series, read for what they omit — the 1514 Hispaniola repartimiento lists the Taíno allotted to colonists, so it undercounts anyone beyond Spanish reach; Boylston's 1726 tables comparing inoculated and naturally infected Bostonians are an early numerical test of a treatment, but nobody was randomised. Indigenous-authored sources — the Florentine Codex, Afonso of Kongo's letters, Guaman Poma's chronicle — are voices from inside the conquest, written under missionaries or for Spanish readers, and translation shifts them: the "broken spears" of this page's own pull quote may be a misreading of omitl, "bones".
    • Honest uncertainty — estimates of the Americas' population in 1492 run from ~40 to ~110 million; the epidemics outran every record-keeper, and the range itself is the finding. See what remains contested and how historians know anything at all.

    In the hand

    Objects that circled the globe

    Gutenberg Bible page

    c. 1455

    A leaf of the 42-line Bible, the first great book from the press — a technology that would print some 200 million volumes in Europe within 150 years.

    Piece of eight

    Potosí mint, from the 1570s

    The Spanish silver dollar, struck from Potosí ore — the closest thing the early-modern world had to a global currency, spent from Seville to Beijing.

    Ming blue-and-white

    16th–17th c.

    Export porcelain fired at Jingdezhen for buyers from Manila to Amsterdam — the return current of silver, and a status object on three continents.

    Guaman Poma's Nueva corónica

    c. 1615

    An Andean noble's 1,189-page chronicle addressed to Philip III, with 398 full-page drawings of Inca life and of abuses by colonial priests and officials. It lay unnoticed in Copenhagen's Royal Library until 1908.

    Mariner's compass

    16th c.

    Compass and astrolabe turned open ocean into navigable space, letting Iberian and later Dutch ships hold a course between hemispheres.

    Timbuktu manuscript

    15th–16th c.

    One of the hundreds of thousands of Timbuktu manuscripts on law, astronomy, and medicine — proof that West Africa was a centre of the written word, not its periphery.

    Benin brass plaque

    16th–17th c.

    Cast by Edo guild brasscasters to cover the pillars of the oba's palace, some showing Portuguese soldiers and traders. Lead isotopes (2023) trace much of the brass to Rhineland-made manillas — the metal bracelets Europeans paid on the West African coast.

    Wampum belt

    17th c.

    Quahog-shell beads woven into diplomatic record — belts of the Covenant Chain bound Haudenosaunee and European polities to remembered terms. Writing, in another medium.

    Siberian sable pelt

    17th c.

    The yasak tribute unit that pulled Russia across the whole width of Siberia — Urals to Pacific, five time zones and some 4,600 km — in six decades — "soft gold" paid by Khanty, Evenki, and Sakha hunters, worth more per kilo than silver.

    Sámi drum

    seized 1691

    Taken from the noaidi Anders Poulsen at Vadsø during the Lutheran campaigns that seized or burned Sámi drums; only 70–80 historical drums survive. It spent nearly three centuries in Copenhagen, went on loan to the Sámi museum in Karasjok in 1979, and was formally handed back to the Sámi in 2022.

    Macassan prau

    c. 1700–1907

    The Sulawesi fleet's trading ship, mooring in Arnhem Land each monsoon to cure trepang for Chinese kitchens — Yolŋu languages still carry Makassar loanwords from the trade.

    Latte stone

    c. 900–1700

    A stone pillar (haligi) crowned with a hemispherical cap (tasa), set in paired rows to raise Chamorro houses across the Marianas — the tallest, at the House of Taga on Tinian, stand about 5 m. Building stopped under Spanish resettlement.
