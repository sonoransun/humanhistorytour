    Chapter V  ·  1200 BCE – 500 CE

    Classical Antiquity
    & the Iron Age

    c. 1200 BCE – 500 CE · about 1,700 years

    Cheap iron, a learnable alphabet, and coined money rebuilt the world, and from the Aegean to the Ganges to the Yellow River sages independently asked what a good life and a just society are.

    Scroll to begin, out of the Bronze Age ruins

    The chapter before us

    Out of the wreckage of the Bronze Age collapse, humanity rebuilt on cheaper metal, simpler writing, and bigger ideas: iron tools anyone could afford, an alphabet anyone could learn, and coined money anyone could spend. Three cheap technologies reset the balance of power across a hemisphere.

    Chapter IV left a world of palace monopolies — bronze for the few, writing for trained scribes, trade run from the citadel. The collapse of c. 1200 BCE burned the palaces but not the toolkit: cities, states, law, and literacy all survived the fall, waiting to be rebuilt on cheaper foundations. This chapter is what got built.

    In a few astonishing centuries — the Axial Age — thinkers from the Aegean to the Ganges to the Yellow River independently asked what a good life and a just society are, founding traditions (Greek philosophy, Buddhism, Confucianism, Zoroastrianism, Judaism, and later Christianity) that still shape billions of lives. Empires scaled up to match: Achaemenid Persia, Maurya and Gupta India, Qin and Han China, the Greek world, and Rome stitched continents together with roads, laws, and bureaucracies.

    This was a planet-wide age of experiment. While Rome paved the Mediterranean, Kush and Aksum minted their own power on the Nile and the Red Sea, Nok smiths worked iron in West Africa, the Olmec and Maya raised pyramids in Mesoamerica, Teotihuacan grew into one of the largest cities on Earth, and Lapita navigators reached islands no human had ever seen. When the Western Roman Empire finally unraveled in 476 CE, the classical world's scripts, faiths, and infrastructures had already become permanent human inheritance.

    01 · The old order

    The Bronze Age world

    For centuries palace kingdoms — Hittite Hattusa, Mycenae, Ugarit — traded tin and copper across a connected Mediterranean, their scribes writing in cuneiform and Linear B — syllabic scripts of scores to hundreds of signs that only a trained elite could read. Ugarit alone had already tried a thirty-sign cuneiform alphabet, a hint of what was coming.

    02 · Collapse

    The lights go out

    Within a generation, around 1200–1150 BCE, drought, migration, and the raiding "Sea Peoples" toppled that world. Cities burned, trade routes snapped, and writing itself receded across the Aegean.

    03 · The forge answers

    A cheaper metal

    From the ruins rose iron. Its ore lay almost everywhere and — unlike bronze — needed no scarce imported tin, so a village smith could arm a farmer or a soldier. Hard metal was democratized.

    04 · Iron goes global

    The grey wash spreads

    Iron moved across Anatolia, the Levant, and Greece — and along its own long-debated pathways into Africa, where the smiths of Nok in West Africa and Meroë on the Nile built traditions of their own.

    05 · Sparks into letters

    An alphabet for merchants

    The same traders who moved iron carried a second tool: a 22-letter alphabet simple enough for a shopkeeper. Adapted into Greek, Aramaic, and Latin, it became the script of most of humankind.

    Do not impose on others what you yourself do not desire. — Confucius, Analects 15.24, c. 5th century BCE

    01 · Greece

    The Greek awakening

    From Thales' reputed eclipse prediction (585 BCE — Herodotus' story, which many historians of science doubt) to Aristotle's Lyceum, Ionian and Athenian thinkers sought natural causes for the cosmos and reasoned argument for the just city — inventing philosophy and the seeds of the sciences.

    02 · India

    Liberation on the Ganges

    Around the fifth century BCE the Buddha taught a middle way out of suffering while Mahavira preached radical nonviolence, and the Upanishads reimagined the self and the absolute — traditions that would travel across all of Asia.

    03 · China

    The good society

    Confucius (551–479 BCE) grounded order in ritual, family, and personal virtue, while the Daodejing — traditionally credited to Laozi, whose historicity is uncertain, and taking shape by c. 300 BCE — counseled harmony with nature's flow: the twin poles of Chinese thought, argued out amid the chaos of the Warring States.

    04 · Israel & Iran

    One moral cosmos

    In Iran, Zoroaster framed existence as a choice between truth and the lie; in Israel, the Hebrew prophets forged an ethical monotheism. Five distant lands, one turning — the moral vocabulary most of humanity still speaks.

    01 · The land road

    Silk by relay

    No one travelled the whole road. Silk left Han Chang'an (Luoyang after 25 CE), passed the frontier garrison at Dunhuang and the oases that skirt the Taklamakan desert, Kashgar among them, and changed hands again and again — at Merv, at the Parthian capital Ctesiphon, at the caravan city of Palmyra and at Antioch — before a last leg by ship to Rome. Han silk has been found in Palmyra's tower tombs, yet when the Han envoy Gan Ying was sent west in 97 CE he turned back at a western sea, perhaps the Persian Gulf, after Parthian sailors warned that the voyage could take two years; Parthia, the Han annals say, blocked direct contact to keep the silk trade for itself.

    02 · The monsoon road

    Pepper by the shipload

    From Alexandria, cargoes went up the Nile and across the desert to the Red Sea ports of Myos Hormos and Berenike; under Augustus, Strabo was told, about 120 ships a year sailed from Myos Hormos for India. Sailing down the Red Sea past Aksum's port of Adulis, they rode the monsoon winds across the Arabian Sea to Muziris in Kerala (probably today's Pattanam), and on round the cape to Arikamedu on the Tamil coast, where Italian tableware and wine jars have been excavated, while Berenike has yielded 7.5 kg of Indian peppercorns and, in 2022, a marble Buddha. The Muziris papyrus, a Greek loan contract of the mid-2nd century CE, follows one returning cargo on the ship Hermapollon — Gangetic nard and ivory among it — liable to a 25 per cent import duty. Beyond India, goods were often portaged across the narrow neck of the Malay peninsula to Funan's port of Óc Eo, where a gold medallion of Antoninus Pius (152 CE) has been found; in 166 CE the Han annals record men claiming to be envoys of Daqin — Rome — landing at Rinan, in today's central Vietnam.

    03 · Africa's forges

    Smelters on the Niger and the Nile

    Africa was not the end of anyone else's road; it ran networks of its own. Nok smiths in the hills north-east of the Niger–Benue confluence were smelting iron by c. 500 BCE, and made the celebrated terracotta sculptures by which the culture is known. At Meroë on the Nile, capital of Kush, mounds of iron slag still ring the royal city; Kush sent ivory, ebony and enslaved people down the river to Egypt, and raided it too — a bronze head of Augustus carried off c. 25 BCE lay buried beneath a Meroë temple stair until it was excavated in 1910.

    04 · Caravans and coin

    Garama and Aksum

    In the Fezzan, the Garamantes watered desert fields through underground foggara channels, ruled from Garama, and traded north to Roman Leptis Magna on the coast: Roman amphorae and glass reached their tombs, and enslaved captives from the south, it is widely thought, went the other way. On the Red Sea, Aksum shipped ivory through its port of Adulis and from c. 270 CE struck its own gold, silver and bronze coins, mostly lettered in Greek, the language of Red Sea commerce; Aksumite coins have turned up in southern Arabia and India. King Ezana's issues of c. 340 CE were among the first coins anywhere to bear the cross, and a Manichaean text attributed to Mani (3rd century) ranked Aksum with Rome and Persia — and a fourth realm usually read as China — as the four great kingdoms of the world.

    05 · The other hemisphere

    Networks the ring never touched

    Teotihuacan — perhaps 100,000 people or more at its height around 450 CE — housed a neighbourhood of Zapotec families from the Valley of Oaxaca, home of Monte Albán, and its green obsidian from the Pachuca mines travelled across Mesoamerica. In January 378 CE, Maya inscriptions record, the Teotihuacan-linked warlord Siyaj K'ak' arrived at Tikal, some 1,000 km away, on the day its king died, and a new dynasty followed — conquest or alliance is still debated. On Peru's desert coast, the Moche (c. 100–800 CE) built the adobe Huaca de la Luna and prized Spondylus shell brought south from the warm waters off Ecuador, while Cahuachi, the Nazca ceremonial centre above some of the Nazca lines, seems to have filled with pilgrims at festival times. None of these networks had any contact that we know of with the Afro-Eurasian ring.

    06 · Exchange without empire

    Hopewell: a continent in one mound

    In the Ohio valley, Hopewell communities (c. 100 BCE – 500 CE) raised geometric earthworks around Chillicothe and Newark and buried in them materials from across a continent: obsidian from Obsidian Cliff in Yellowstone, some 2,300 km to the west, native copper from Lake Superior, marine shell from the Gulf coast (Crystal River in Florida shared in the exchange), and sheets of mica from the southern Appalachians. There were no cities, kings or draft animals; the goods moved through gift, trade and perhaps long pilgrimages, and chemical fingerprinting of the obsidian is what ties it to its Yellowstone source. The Hopewell Ceremonial Earthworks became a World Heritage Site in 2023.

    Milestones

    Forty-two turning points

    c. 1200 BCE

    Bronze Age collapse & the Iron Age

    E. Mediterranean & Anatolia

    Within a generation the Hittite Empire, Mycenaean palaces, and Ugarit fell amid drought, migration, and systems failure. Iron needed no scarce imported tin, so hard tools and weapons became cheap and common — democratizing farming and war.

    c. 1050 BCE

    The Phoenician alphabet

    Levant — Tyre, Byblos, Sidon

    A 22-letter consonantal script simple enough for merchants, not just scribes. Carried across the Mediterranean, it became Greek (with vowels added), Aramaic, Hebrew, Latin, and Arabic — ancestor of most scripts written today.

    c. 1200–400 BCE

    Olmec civilization

    Gulf Coast of Mexico

    Mesoamerica's first great culture carved multi-ton basalt heads and set a template later peoples inherited: pyramid-and-plaza centers, the ritual ballgame, calendrics, and perhaps the region's earliest writing.

    c. 1000–850 BCE

    Lapita voyagers reach Remote Oceania

    Western Pacific

    Austronesian-descended navigators crossed hundreds of kilometres of open ocean by stars, swells, and birds to reach Vanuatu, Fiji, Tonga, and Samoa — the finest deep-water seafaring on Earth, and the founders of Polynesia.

    c. 1000 BCE – 150 CE

    Jiigurru: Australia's oldest pottery

    Australia — Jiigurru (Lizard Island), Great Barrier Reef

    Sherds excavated on Jiigurru, an island group off far-north Queensland, come from layers radiocarbon-dated to c. 1000–600 BCE and c. 20 BCE – 135 CE — the oldest securely dated pottery in Australia. Made on the island from local granite-derived sand and clay, they overturn the old claim that Aboriginal Australians never made pots, and they place the reef's canoe voyagers in a wider Coral Sea world that included New Guinea's potters. The 2024 study (Ulm, McNiven and colleagues, working with Dingaal and Ngurrumungu Traditional Owners) rests on stratified sherds, radiocarbon and petrography.

    c. 1000 BCE – 350 CE

    Iron Age Africa: Nok, Kush & the Bantu

    West Africa, Nubia & beyond

    Nok smiths worked iron by c. 500 BCE; Kush ruled Egypt as its 25th Dynasty, then flourished at Meroë with its own script and pyramids; Bantu-speaking farmer-smiths carried iron and crops across half a continent.

    c. 1200 BCE – 500 CE

    Budj Bim's aquaculture, still running

    SE Australia — Gunditjmara Country

    The eel-farming complex of weirs, channels, and holding ponds first engineered c. 4,600 BCE stayed in continuous operation through this entire chapter — older than the pyramids, still working when Rome fell. Late Holocene Australia intensified on its own terms: finer tool kits, seed-grinding economies, and denser settlement without grain farming or states.

    c. 1200–500 BCE

    Chavín de Huántar & the first Andean horizon

    Peruvian Andes

    A highland temple of stone galleries, water channels, and psychoactive ritual whose art style spread from coast to cordillera — the first pan-Andean "horizon," binding ecologies together by pilgrimage rather than conquest. New radiocarbon series put most building before c. 750 BCE and the temple's ceremonial life over by c. 500–400 BCE, earlier than the older 900–200 BCE chronology. After it, on the south coast, Paracas weavers made textiles of a fineness modern looms struggle to match.

    911–609 BCE

    The Neo-Assyrian Empire

    Mesopotamia & the Near East

    The first iron-armed world empire ruled from the Tigris to Egypt with siege engineering, relay couriers, and mass deportation as deliberate state policy. Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh — some 30,000 cuneiform tablets, Gilgamesh among them — became, in ruin, antiquity's best-preserved archive.

    c. 900 BCE – 300 CE

    Yayoi Japan & Korea's first states

    Korean peninsula & Japanese archipelago

    Wet-rice farming crossed from the Korean peninsula into northern Kyushu c. 900–800 BCE (a 2003 AMS redating moved it some five centuries earlier than once thought); bronze and iron followed c. 400–300 BCE, and by then the archipelago's economy had been reset. Gojoseon fell to Han armies in 108 BCE, and in the commanderies' shadow rose Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla — the state system of East Asia's next thousand years.

    c. 850 BCE – 1025 CE

    The long pause: Ancestral Polynesia takes shape

    Oceania & the Pacific — Tonga & Samoa

    After Lapita voyagers reached Tonga and Samoa, the eastward push stopped. High-precision radiocarbon dating puts the first settlement of the Society Islands only at c. 1025–1120 CE (Wilmshurst et al. 2011), so for the whole of this chapter the Polynesian frontier held. Meanwhile decorated Lapita pottery gave way to plain wares and eventually to none at all, and the language, crops and social forms later carried to Hawaiʻi, Rapa Nui and Aotearoa — what archaeologists call Ancestral Polynesian society — took shape in the Tonga–Samoa region.

    c. 814–30 BCE

    Carthage & Alexandria: Africa's Mediterranean

    North African coast

    The Mediterranean's two richest non-Roman cities stood on Africa's shore: Carthage, Rome's naval equal, whose general Hannibal annihilated eight legions at Cannae (216 BCE) before the city was razed in 146 BCE; and Ptolemaic Alexandria, whose Library hosted Eratosthenes measuring Earth's circumference to within a few percent c. 240 BCE. The era's geared Antikythera mechanism (c. 100 BCE; decoded by Derek de Solla Price and Tony Freeth) computed eclipses — and nothing of comparable complexity is known again until the astronomical clocks of the fourteenth century, though a far simpler Byzantine geared sundial-calendar of c. 400–600 CE shows the tradition never quite died.

    c. 800–600 BCE

    Arzhan: the first Scythian kings

    Central Asia & the Steppe — Tuva, southern Siberia

    In the Tuva basin the royal mound Arzhan 1 (radiocarbon-dated to c. 800 BCE) and the unlooted grave of Arzhan 2 (c. 650–600 BCE; excavated 2000–04 by a German–Russian team) hold the earliest known "Scythian triad" of horse harness, weapons and animal-style art. Arzhan 2's royal couple lay among some 9,300 objects, about 5,700 of them gold. The mounted-archer aristocracy took shape here in the east, long before Herodotus described Scythians on the Black Sea and six centuries before the Xiongnu.

    c. 630–600 BCE

    The invention of coinage

    Lydia, with parallels in China & India

    Lydian kings stamped electrum with guarantees of weight and purity; Greek cities followed within decades. China cast bronze spade and knife money and India punch-marked silver — the impersonal economy made portable.

    c. 800–200 BCE

    The Axial Age

    Greece, Israel, Iran, India, China

    In a near-simultaneity across lands with little direct contact (though Ionia, Judah, and Gandhara all sat inside the same Persian empire by the late sixth century BCE), the Hebrew prophets, the Buddha and Mahavira, Confucius, the authors of the Daodejing, and the Greek natural philosophers — with Zoroaster, whose date is contested, perhaps centuries earlier — reframed the good life — still the ethical vocabulary of most of humanity.

    c. 750 BCE – 575 CE

    Marib and the incense kingdoms of Arabia

    West Asia — Saba & Himyar, Yemen

    By about the eighth century BCE Sabaean engineers had dammed the Wadi Dhana at Marib; rebuilt again and again, the dam came to water some 100 km² of desert fields and underwrote the frankincense and myrrh that caravans carried north to the Mediterranean. In the late fourth century CE Himyar's kings turned to Judaism — Sabaic inscriptions switch to one god, Rahmanan — and South Arabia stayed a Jewish-ruled kingdom until Aksum invaded in the 520s. The dam's final breach, c. 570–575 CE, is remembered in the Quran.

    c. 600 BCE – 550 CE

    Dong Son drums & Funan's ports

    Southeast Asia

    Casters in the Red River valley made bronze drums traded from Yunnan to Java; by c. 100 CE the Mekong kingdom of Funan ran the entrepôt of Óc Eo, where a Roman medallion of Antoninus Pius lies in the same layers as Indian beads and Han mirrors — Southeast Asia as a hinge of world trade, not a periphery.

    550–330 BCE

    The Achaemenid Persian Empire

    Iran & the Near East

    Cyrus and Darius built the largest empire yet seen, ruling dozens of peoples through satrapies and a pragmatic accommodation of local cults — the Cyrus Cylinder, often over-read as a charter of tolerance, is a conventional Babylonian foundation text that records Cyrus restoring Babylon's gods and shrines. The Royal Road ran some 2,700 km from Sardis to Susa with relay couriers.

    c. 500 BCE – 500 CE

    Adena mounds & the Hopewell exchange

    Eastern North America

    Adena communities raised the first great burial mounds on the Ohio; their Hopewell successors moved Yellowstone obsidian, Appalachian mica, and Lake Superior copper across half a continent — a ceremonial exchange sphere contemporary with Rome, built without kings, cities, or draft animals.

    c. 500 BCE – 1300 CE

    The Dorset (Tuniit) & the ivory Arctic

    North American Arctic & Bering Strait

    Dorset people engineered life on sea ice — soapstone lamps, snow knives, triangular harpoon endblades — while Old Bering Sea and Ipiutak carvers on the strait produced some of antiquity's finest ivory art. High technology for Earth's hardest biome, contemporary with the Parthenon and Han Chang'an.

    c. 500 BCE – 300 CE

    Urewe iron and the Bantu road south

    East & Southern Africa — Great Lakes to KwaZulu-Natal

    Around Lake Victoria, from Tanzania's Kagera region to western Kenya, Urewe-ware villages with clay shaft furnaces were smelting iron by about the fifth century BCE. Their Kwale-branch successors carried iron, pottery and farming down the eastern coast, and pioneers reached KwaZulu-Natal between c. 200 and 300 CE. Linked to the eastern Bantu languages, this slow drift of farmer-smiths is why related languages are spoken across Africa's east and south today; the evidence is pottery, furnaces, radiocarbon and historical linguistics.

    c. 500–300 BCE

    The Saladoid crossing: the Caribbean's Ceramic Age

    The Caribbean — Puerto Rico & the Lesser Antilles

    Pottery-making farmers from the lower Orinoco (named for Saladero in Venezuela) reached Puerto Rico and the Lesser Antilles between c. 500 and 300 BCE. Their red wares painted in white, and flat clay griddles of the kind used to bake cassava bread, open the islands' Ceramic Age; they are ancestors of the Taíno whom Columbus met. Ancient genomes (Nägele et al. 2020, 93 islanders; Fernandes et al. 2021, 174 individuals) show the newcomers barely mixed with the Archaic foragers already there and in time almost wholly replaced them.

    c. 500 BCE – 600 CE

    Garden cities of the Upano valley

    South America — Amazonian Ecuador

    Airborne lidar over some 300 km² of the Upano valley, in Ecuador's Amazonian foothills, revealed about 6,000 earthen platforms in clustered settlements, joined by straight roads running as far as 25 km and surrounded by drained fields and terraces for maize, manioc and sweet potato (Rostain et al. 2024). First settled c. 500 BCE and abandoned sometime between c. 300 and 600 CE, this was garden urbanism as old as the Roman Republic, in a forest long imagined as empty of cities. Who governed it, and how, is still unknown.

    508–30 BCE

    Classical Greece & the Hellenistic world

    Aegean, then Egypt to the Indus

    Cleisthenes' reforms made Athens the famous experiment in citizen self-rule; Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle followed. Alexander then scattered Greek cities and learning from Egypt to Central Asia, fusing traditions in Alexandria.

    321–185 BCE

    The Maurya Empire & Ashoka's edicts

    South Asia

    Chandragupta unified most of the subcontinent; his grandson Ashoka, sickened by the Kalinga war, renounced conquest and inscribed edicts of nonviolence and welfare on rocks and pillars — and sent the missions that made Buddhism a world religion.

    c. 250 BCE

    Jenne-jeno: a city without a citadel

    West & Central Africa — Inland Niger Delta, Mali

    Radiocarbon dates put the first permanent settlement at Jenne-jeno, on the Niger's inland delta, at c. 250 BCE, and iron was worked from its first levels. By c. 800 CE the town and its satellite villages may have housed 10,000–27,000 people — yet the excavators, Susan and Roderick McIntosh, found no palace, citadel or wealthy ruling class: power seems to have been shared among clustered corporate groups. They argue that West African towns grew on local trade in food, iron and crafts, before the trans-Saharan camel caravans rather than because of them.

    247 BCE – 651 CE

    Parthian & Sasanian Persia

    Iran & Mesopotamia

    Rome's peer, not its footnote, for seven centuries: Parthian horse-archers destroyed Crassus at Carrhae in 53 BCE, and the Sasanian Shapur I captured the emperor Valerian alive in 260 CE. Persian dynasties kept the Silk Road's tolls and made the classical world bipolar, not Roman.

    221 BCE – 220 CE

    Qin unification & the Han dynasty

    China

    Qin Shi Huang unified the warring states, standardizing script, currency, and weights (and guarding his tomb with the Terracotta Army). The Han built a Confucian civil-service state, opened the Silk Road, and invented papermaking.

    209–126 BCE

    The Xiongnu & the opening of the Silk Roads

    Central Asian steppe

    Modu Chanyu forged the first steppe empire in 209 BCE — a nomad confederation that extracted treaty payments from Han China for sixty years. Zhang Qian's mission west (138–126 BCE), sent to outflank it, brought back the intelligence that opened the Silk Roads; and the earlier frozen Pazyryk kurgans of the Altai (5th–3rd century BCE) preserve the steppe world's carpets, tattoos, and horses.

    c. 100 BCE – 800 CE

    Nazca, Moche & the engineered desert

    Pacific coast of South America

    Nazca people etched kilometre-scale geoglyphs and, probably from c. 450–650 CE, dug spiral-welled puquios — underground aqueducts that still water fields today (a minority argue for a colonial date) — while the Moche state perfected gold and copper metallurgy and buried the Lord of Sipán in the richest unlooted tomb yet found in the Americas (excavated by Walter Alva, 1987).

    c. 25–21 BCE

    The Kandake fights Rome

    North Africa & the Nile — Kush (Meroë)

    Soon after Rome annexed Egypt, Kushite forces led by a Kandake (queen) — generally identified as Amanirenas, and by Strabo's account blind in one eye — took Syene, Elephantine and Philae and pulled down statues of Augustus. The prefect Petronius struck back as far as Napata, but after some three years of war Kushite envoys met Augustus on Samos and came away with peace and no tribute to pay. One looted bronze head of the emperor was buried beneath a temple stair at Meroë, where worshippers trod on it; it was found in 1910 and is now in the British Museum.

    509 BCE – 117 CE

    Rome: Republic to Empire

    Mediterranean world

    From the Republic and the Twelve Tables, Rome grew through citizen armies and civil war into Augustus' empire. At its peak it governed 60–70 million people, bound by some 80,000 km of paved roads, aqueducts, concrete, and a lasting law.

    c. 30–380 CE

    Early Christianity & rabbinic Judaism

    Roman Levant & the Mediterranean

    From Jesus and Paul's road-borne mission, a persecuted sect spread through the empire's cities — legalized in 313, made official in 380. After the Temple's destruction in 70 CE, Judaism was refounded around rabbis, synagogue, and text.

    c. 30–375 CE

    The Kushan Empire & the Buddha's image

    Central & South Asia

    Astride the Silk Roads, Kanishka's empire minted coins for Greek, Iranian, Indian, and Buddhist gods alike; sculptors at Gandhara and Mathura carved the first human images of the Buddha (which came first is still argued), and Kushan-sponsored monks carried Buddhism over the passes into Han China — antiquity's most consequential cultural transmission — while the physicians Charaka and Sushruta codified Indian medicine and surgery.

    40–43 CE

    The Trưng sisters rise against the Han

    Southeast Asia — Red River delta (Han Jiaozhi)

    In 40 CE Trưng Trắc and her sister Trưng Nhị raised the Red River delta against the Han governor Su Ding, and the Chinese sources say some sixty-five towns went over to them. The general Ma Yuan's army crushed the rising by 43 CE, and Han rule tightened. The main account, the Hou Hanshu, was compiled on the conquerors' side in the fifth century and Vietnamese chronicles later still, so motives and even the sisters' deaths are told differently — but the rising itself is well attested, and Vietnam still honours them.

    98 CE

    Tacitus' Fenni: the far north enters the record

    The Arctic — northern Fennoscandia & the Baltic

    Tacitus' Germania (98 CE) ends at the edge of the known world with the Fenni: hunters, women as well as men, with no horses, no houses and no iron, tipping their arrows with bone. Whether he meant ancestors of the Sámi, of the Finns, or simply everyone beyond the Baltic is debated. Historical linguists place Proto-Sámi some 2,000–2,500 years ago in southern Finland or Karelia, spreading north across Fennoscandia in the centuries that followed — the root of the Sámi world. The evidence is a Roman text built on hearsay, read against language history.

    165–284 CE

    The plagues & the third-century crisis

    Rome, Han China & the routes between

    The Antonine Plague (165–180 CE; traditionally identified as smallpox, an identification ancient-DNA studies now question) and the Plague of Cyprian (249–262) travelled the same ships and roads as pepper and silk, hollowing tax rolls and legions; Rome's near-collapse and the Han fall of 220 CE bracket the lesson that connected worlds share diseases as surely as goods. At the Han court, Zhang Heng's bronze seismoscope (132 CE) was already registering distant earthquakes.

    c. 100–550 CE

    Teotihuacan & the Classic Maya

    Mesoamerica

    Teotihuacan grew to perhaps 100,000–150,000 people, with the Pyramid of the Sun and multiethnic compounds, its reach touching Maya capitals. The Maya perfected the Americas' most complete — and only fully deciphered — writing system, the Long Count calendar, and precise astronomy.

    c. 100–700 CE

    Aksum: coins, stelae & Ezana's conversion

    Horn of Africa

    From its port Adulis, Aksum shipped ivory and gold into the Roman–Indian trade, struck Africa's only home-grown coinage south of Egypt (from c. 270 CE), wrote in its own Ge'ez script, and raised 30-metre monolithic stelae. King Ezana's conversion c. 340 CE made it one of the first Christian states on Earth.

    c. 320–550 CE

    The Gupta golden age

    South Asia

    Decimal place-value numerals — ancestors of the digits the world now uses — Aryabhata's astronomy describing a rotating Earth, Kalidasa's drama, and Nalanda's university: Sanskrit science and letters radiated across Asia.

    376–476 CE

    Fall of the Western Roman Empire

    Western Europe

    Gothic crossings of the Danube, Alaric's sack of Rome (410), and fiscal-military erosion ended with Odoacer deposing Romulus Augustulus in 476. The East endured as Byzantium — 'fall' or 'transformation'? — but western unity was gone.

    c. 450 CE

    The Lady of Cao: a woman at the top of Moche society

    South America — El Brujo, north coast of Peru

    In 2006 Régulo Franco's team opened an intact tomb at Huaca Cao Viejo: a woman in her mid-twenties, heavily tattooed, wrapped in many layers of cloth with gold and silver jewellery and weapons — war clubs and spear-throwers among them — and accompanied by a second young woman, probably sacrificed. Until then the Moche elite was assumed to be male. Whether she ruled, served as a priestess or both is read from the grave, not recorded; she may have died of complications of pregnancy or childbirth.

    Why it happened

    Cheap metal, kind weather, unfree hands

    The classical flowering was not powered by genius alone. It ran on a democratized metal, some six unusually warm centuries, a world population that roughly doubled or tripled — and, everywhere you look closely, on the labour of people who were not free.

    Start with the metallurgy, because it changed the politics of war. Bronze needs tin, and tin came from a handful of distant sources — so bronze implied a trade network, and a trade network implied a palace to run it. Iron ore sits in almost every hillside. Once smiths mastered it, any chiefdom could arm a thousand farmers, and the palace monopoly on force was finished. Armies grew from Bronze Age thousands to the hundreds of thousands Rome and Han China could field, and states had to learn to count, tax, and conscript whole populations to keep up — the writing–money–state spine tightening into bureaucracy. China pushed the logic furthest. Its earliest known cast iron dates to about the eighth century BCE, and by the fifth century BCE casting was widespread — liquid metal poured into moulds for ploughshares and hoes by the thousand, a technique Europe would not use at scale until its blast furnaces of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries CE. Under Emperor Wu (from c. 117 BCE) the Han state made salt and iron a state monopoly to pay for its wars on the Xiongnu, and the policy was contested openly enough that in 81 BCE the court staged a debate on it, preserved as the Discourses on Salt and Iron — one of antiquity's fullest arguments over state and market.

    Climate framed both ends of the chapter. Juniper rings from Gordion in central Anatolia record an unbroken, severe drought around 1198–1196 BCE (±3 years), just as the Hittite capital was abandoned: not a slow drying but three failed years in a row, the kind of shock that overwhelms granaries built to ride out one bad harvest (Manning et al. 2023). Then came the good centuries. The Roman Climatic Optimum (c. 250 BCE – 400 CE) — warm, wet, and unusually stable — fattened harvests from Britain to the Yellow River, and it is no accident that antiquity's biggest empires all peak inside it (see the climate spine). Its end is now being pulled earlier: the first high-resolution record from Roman Italy, Gulf of Taranto sediments read at roughly three-year steps, shows instability from c. 100 CE and cooling after c. 130 CE (Zonneveld, Harper et al. 2024), and Kyle Harper ends the optimum about 150 CE. Volcanoes punctuated it. Alaska's Okmok blew early in 43 BCE, one of the largest eruptions of the past 2,500 years, and 43 and 42 BCE were among the coldest Northern Hemisphere years of recent millennia — cold, wet seasons that probably meant failed harvests while Caesar's heirs fought over the Republic (McConnell et al. 2020). In Ptolemaic Egypt, eruption years that weakened the Nile flood have been tied to revolts against the crown (Manning et al. 2017).

    Demography and disease did quiet work underneath. World population roughly doubled or tripled in the thousand years to 1 CE — from 50–110 million in 1000 BCE to 170–300 million, depending on whose reconstruction you trust (McEvedy & Jones sit at the low end, HYDE 3.3 at 110 and 232 million): the Han census of 2 CE counted 57.7 million people, and Rome governed 60–70 million more — for the first time, two states each held somewhere between a fifth and two-fifths of humanity (world population data). Crowds, roads and ships made those populations a feast for pathogens. Thucydides watched a plague kill Pericles and a great many of the Athenians packed inside the walls in 430–426 BCE; its pathogen is still contested. Malaria was the quieter, endemic killer: the DNA of Plasmodium falciparum has been recovered from the teeth of people buried in imperial-era southern Italy (Marciniak et al. 2016). Then the weather loosened and pandemics arrived: the Antonine Plague (165–180 CE, traditionally called smallpox though its pathogen is unproven, perhaps 5–10 million dead) and the Plague of Cyprian (249–262 CE) travelled the same ships and roads as pepper and silk, hollowing out tax rolls and legions — and both fall inside cold phases of the Italian record, c. 160–180 and 245–275 CE. In China the annals record repeated epidemics in the 170s and 180s CE, and the Yellow Turban rising of 184, led by a faith-healer, helped break the Han. Rome's third-century crisis and the Han collapse of 220 CE follow the pandemics; they do not precede them.

    And under it all, coercion. Perhaps a third of Classical Athens was enslaved — the silver that paid for its fleet came out of Laurion's shafts, worked by slave gangs; Sparta's helots outnumbered its citizens and were terrorized by annual ritual; Roman Italy held perhaps 1–3 million enslaved people (the market on Delos boasted of moving 10,000 in a day, and Spartacus' rising of 73–71 BCE took eight legions to crush); Qin and Han China built walls and tombs with convict and corvée labour; the Arthashastra — traditionally Mauryan, though its surviving text was compiled c. 50–300 CE — regulates debt bondage in India. Captive-taking was coercion's other face: when Kush raided Roman Egypt c. 25 BCE its army carried townspeople off as slaves, and at the Moche Huaca de la Luna the remains of dozens of young men — captives taken in combat, whether staged ritual or real war is debated — lie in a plaza beside the temple platform. Gender was a parallel structure, and it varied: Athenian citizen women lived secluded, could not own land, and by law could make no contract worth more than a medimnos — about a bushel and a half — of barley, while Spartan women held perhaps two-fifths of Sparta's land and Egyptian women signed contracts, sued, and divorced under their own names. The exceptions who reached the record earned it twice over — Ban Zhao, who completed the Han dynasty's official history c. 100 CE; the Vestals, whose persons were sacrosanct in Rome; Hypatia, who led Alexandria's mathematicians until a mob murdered her in 415 CE. Beyond the Mediterranean the record of women's power is richer than the textbooks: a Kandake of Kush fought Rome to a treaty, the Trưng sisters raised the Red River delta against the Han, a tattooed Moche woman was buried with weapons at El Brujo, and at two cemeteries on the Xiongnu empire's western frontier the richest graves belonged to women (Lee et al. 2023).

    Nor did scale need an emperor. Some of the era's most durable works were built by societies with no palace at all: Budj Bim's eel channels, the Hopewell earthworks, the platform towns of the Upano valley in Amazonian Ecuador, and Jenne-jeno on the Niger, which grew into a town with no citadel and no sign of a ruling class. Water control was the common thread, whoever held power — Saba's dam at Marib, the Garamantes' foggara channels, the Nazca puquios and the Upano's drained fields all demanded maintenance, generation after generation, by people who had to agree on how to share the water. When that failed, as at Marib in the sixth century CE, the landscape failed with it.

    Competing explanations

    • Why an Axial Age at all? Iron-age prosperity creating a class with leisure to criticize; small rival states competing for talent; or a coincidence stitched into a pattern by hindsight — all three are argued seriously.
    • What brought down the Bronze Age palaces? A year-dated drought now sits beside the "Sea Peoples," earthquakes, and brittle palace economies that depended on long-distance tin. Most specialists now describe a cascade in which several of these hit at once; which fell first, and whether drought was cause or merely the last straw, is still argued.
    • Did iron reach sub-Saharan Africa from outside, or was it invented there? Some early furnace dates from Niger, Nigeria and the Great Lakes would make African smelting independent of the Mediterranean; critics question what exactly the dated samples date. The forge scene's arcs show one reading, not a verdict.
    • How much did climate drive events? Ice cores, tree rings and lake sediments now line cold years up with Rome's civil wars, Egypt's revolts and the pandemics. Critics (e.g. Strunz & Braeckel 2020) warn that alignment is not mechanism and ask for the causal chain — harvest, price, army, tax — in each case.
    • Why did the Roman West fall? Barbarian migration, fiscal-military erosion, climate downturn plus pandemic, or "transformation, not fall" — the debate is two centuries old and still open.
    • How deadly were the plagues? Estimates for the Antonine Plague run from about 2% to 25% of the empire's population; 5–10 million dead is a mid-range guess, not a measurement. See what remains contested.
    • Why did Polynesian voyaging stop for so long? After Tonga and Samoa were reached, the better part of two thousand years passed before anyone sailed on to East Polynesia. Shifts in the winds, the time needed to develop larger canoes, and the sheer distance to the next islands have all been proposed; none is proven.

    When the western tax spine finally snapped in 476 CE, the classical toolkit — alphabet, coin, codified law, world religion, paved road — did not go down with it. Byzantium, Sasanian Persia, Aksum, Gupta India, and the Han successor states carried it forward, and within two centuries a new faith out of Arabia would re-string the whole network. That relay is the next chapter.

    Meanwhile, everywhere

    Sixteen regions, seventeen centuries

    Rome and Han China fill most histories of these centuries, but every inhabited region had its own story. Much of it is known only from archaeology, so some cards rest on sherds, furnaces and radiocarbon rather than on kings.

    West Asia

    c. 1200 BCE – 500 CE

    Assyria rebuilt first, ruling from the Tigris to Egypt at its height (671–c. 655 BCE), before Babylonians and Medes sacked Nineveh in 612 BCE. Cyrus took Babylon in 539 BCE, and for a thousand years Iranian dynasties — Achaemenid, Parthian (from 247 BCE) and Sasanian (from 224 CE) — set the region's rhythm. In Arabia, Saba's dam at Marib watered an incense economy, Nabataean Petra grew rich on caravans until Rome annexed it in 106 CE, and Himyar's kings turned to Judaism in the late fourth century.

    North Africa & the Nile

    c. 1200 BCE – 500 CE

    Egypt's New Kingdom ended c. 1070 BCE and the Nile passed from hand to hand: Kushite pharaohs from Napata (744–656 BCE), Persians, Alexander's Ptolemies, then Rome from 30 BCE, whose capital ate Egyptian grain. Upriver, Kush ruled from Meroë, smelted iron and wrote its own Meroitic script, whose letters can be read but whose language is still poorly understood. On the coast Carthage rivalled Rome until 146 BCE; inland, Masinissa (r. 202–148 BCE) built the kingdom of Numidia, and the Garamantes farmed the Fezzan.

    West & Central Africa

    c. 1000 BCE – 500 CE

    Nok farmers in central Nigeria, known for their terracotta heads, were smelting iron by c. 500 BCE. On the Niger's inland delta, Jenne-jeno was settled c. 250 BCE with iron and domesticated African rice in its first levels, and grew into a town without a citadel. Bantu-speaking farmers spread through the Congo rainforest along savanna corridors (Grollemund et al. 2015); then, c. 400–600 CE, radiocarbon dates across the basin collapse — a population crash, possibly from a long epidemic, followed by resettlement only centuries later (Seidensticker et al. 2021).

    East & Southern Africa

    c. 700 BCE – 500 CE

    In the Tigray highlands the Great Temple of Yeha (c. 700 BCE) was built in the Sabaean style of South Arabia; from c. 100 CE Aksum grew into a Red Sea power, and its king Ezana turned Christian c. 340. Around Lake Victoria, Urewe smiths were smelting iron by the fifth century BCE, and their farming successors reached KwaZulu-Natal c. 200–300 CE. The Periplus (mid-first century CE) names Rhapta, somewhere on the Tanzanian coast, as the last market on the African shore, exporting ivory and tortoiseshell and named for its sewn boats.

    Europe & the Mediterranean

    c. 1200 BCE – 476 CE

    North of the Alps, the Hallstatt chief buried at Hochdorf c. 530 BCE lay on a bronze couch beside a cauldron that had held some 400 litres of mead, and La Tène Celts later built walled towns (oppida) such as Manching. Celts sacked Rome c. 390 BCE; Caesar conquered Gaul in 58–50 BCE, but three legions died in the Teutoburg Forest in 9 CE and the Rhine stayed a frontier. Ink tablets from Vindolanda (c. 100 CE), near the line Hadrian's Wall would take from 122 CE, keep soldiers' and families' letters; the Huns' arrival in the 370s drove the Gothic crossing of the Danube in 376.

    Central Asia & the Steppe

    c. 900 BCE – 500 CE

    Mounted archers made the steppe a power in its own right. The Arzhan mounds of Tuva (from c. 800 BCE) show the Scythian world forming in the east, and frozen Pazyryk tombs in the Altai (5th–3rd century BCE) kept tattooed bodies, felt and the oldest known knotted-pile carpet. Greek settlers built Ai-Khanoum on the Oxus (c. 300–145 BCE); the Xiongnu (from 209 BCE) drew tribute from Han China; Sogdian merchants and Kushan kings ran the middle of the Silk Roads. By the 370s the Huns were moving west, and by the late fifth century the Hephthalites held Bactria.

    South Asia

    c. 1200 BCE – 500 CE

    Iron and rice turned the Ganges plain into a land of rival kingdoms, the sixteen mahajanapadas, where the Buddha and Mahavira taught and Magadha won out; the Mauryas (321–185 BCE) then ruled most of the subcontinent. In the Tamil south, the Chera, Chola and Pandya kings of the Sangam poems grew rich on pepper sold to Roman ships, and in Sri Lanka Anuradhapura, a capital from the fourth century BCE, was fed by great irrigation reservoirs. Under Satavahana rule Sanchi's stupa gained its carved gateways (from c. 50 BCE), one relief a gift of the ivory-carvers of Vidisha; from c. 320 CE the Guptas presided over the age of Aryabhata and Kalidasa.

    East Asia

    c. 1200 BCE – 500 CE

    The Zhou kings lost their western capital in 771 BCE, and five centuries of rivalry among their successor states produced Confucius, cast iron and mass infantry armies before Qin unified China in 221 BCE. The Han counted 57.7 million subjects in 2 CE; after 220 came centuries of division, in which Buddhism took root — the Northern Wei began the Yungang caves in 460. In Korea, Goguryeo, Baekje and Silla took shape; in Japan, Yayoi rice villages gave way from the third century CE to great keyhole-shaped tombs, and Chinese records tell of a queen, Himiko, who sent envoys with cloth and enslaved people to the Wei court in 238 (or 239).

    Southeast Asia

    c. 1000 BCE – 500 CE

    Dong Son casters in the Red River valley made great bronze drums, and Sa Huỳnh communities on the central Vietnamese coast (c. 1000 BCE – 200 CE) buried their dead in jars. At Khao Sam Kaeo on the Thai peninsula (c. 400–100 BCE), foreign craftsmen settled and metalwork mixed South Asian, Vietnamese and Han techniques; the Pyu built walled brick cities such as Beikthano in Myanmar; and Funan's Óc Eo became a port of the Indian Ocean trade. Han armies took the Red River delta in 111 BCE, and in 192 CE Ou Lian broke from Han rule to found Linyi, forerunner of Champa.

    Australia

    c. 1200 BCE – 500 CE

    No grain, no states — and no standing still. At Budj Bim the Gunditjmara kept their stone eel channels working, as they had for thousands of years; on Jiigurru (Lizard Island), reef voyagers were firing pottery by c. 1000–600 BCE, the oldest yet found in Australia. Radiocarbon-based models suggest that El Niño droughts until about 2,000 years ago fragmented populations and emptied marginal country, and that wetter conditions afterwards brought growth across the whole continent (Williams et al. 2015).

    Oceania & the Pacific

    c. 1000 BCE – 500 CE

    Lapita voyagers reached Fiji, Tonga and Samoa by c. 1000–850 BCE, and there the eastward push stopped for the better part of two thousand years while Ancestral Polynesian society took shape. To the north, people were living on the Marshall Islands' atolls by about the first century CE (earth ovens at Laura, on Majuro). Hawaiʻi, Aotearoa and Rapa Nui were still unpeopled, and stayed so until centuries after this chapter ends.

    The Arctic

    c. 800 BCE – 500 CE

    Dorset people — the Tuniit of Inuit tradition — lived on the sea ice of Arctic Canada and Labrador with soapstone seal-oil lamps and miniature ivory carvings; Greenland itself seems to have stood empty from about 1 CE, when its Early Dorset people vanish, until Late Dorset people crossed Nares Strait c. 700 CE. On the Bering Strait, Old Bering Sea and Ipiutak hunters carved some of antiquity's finest ivory; Ipiutak at Point Hope has nearly 500 house pits, though only 20–30 houses were lived in at any one time. In Fennoscandia Proto-Sámi speech was beginning to spread north, and in 98 CE Tacitus described the Fenni, hunters beyond the Baltic.

    North America

    c. 1200 BCE – 500 CE

    The great earthen ridges of Poverty Point in Louisiana fell quiet c. 1100 BCE; later, Adena and then Hopewell communities raised mounds and geometric earthworks in the Ohio valley, fed partly by native seed crops such as goosefoot, sunflower and marsh elder. In the Southwest, Basketmaker II farmers grew maize on the Colorado Plateau, and along the Santa Cruz River near Tucson canal irrigation, begun in the second millennium BCE, grew larger over the centuries. On the Northwest Coast, the Marpole culture of the Fraser River delta (c. 450 BCE – 350 CE) shows the ranked, salmon-rich village life of later centuries taking shape.

    Mesoamerica

    c. 1200 BCE – 500 CE

    The Olmec of San Lorenzo and La Venta set Mesoamerica's first template, and lidar found Aguada Fénix in Tabasco (c. 1000–800 BCE), a platform 1.4 km long with more volume than Egypt's Great Pyramid — the oldest and largest Maya monument known. Preclassic Maya kings raised El Mirador's giant pyramids and painted San Bartolo's murals (c. 100 BCE), and Zapotecs founded Monte Albán c. 500 BCE. The eruption of Xitle (sometime c. 245–315 CE) buried what remained of rival Cuicuilco, and Teotihuacan, swollen with migrants and refugees, came to dominate the Basin of Mexico and to reach Tikal by 378 CE.

    The Caribbean

    c. 500 BCE – 500 CE

    Archaic foragers and fishers had lived on the islands for thousands of years when pottery-making farmers from the lower Orinoco — the Saladoid — crossed to Puerto Rico and the Lesser Antilles c. 500–300 BCE. They brought manioc, flat clay griddles, settled villages and pendants of carnelian and other imported stone, some carved as birds of prey; ancient DNA shows they barely mixed with the Archaic islanders. Cuba remained the country of Archaic peoples for centuries more.

    South America

    c. 1200 BCE – 500 CE

    Chavín de Huántar drew pilgrims from coast and highlands until c. 500 BCE; after it came Paracas weavers, the Nazca lines and the Moche lords of the north coast, among them the Lady of Cao (c. 450 CE). On the Titicaca altiplano, Tiwanaku was founded c. 110 CE and grew for centuries. In Amazonia, the Upano valley's platform towns flourished c. 500 BCE – 300/600 CE, and on Marajó island at the Amazon's mouth the Marajoara phase, known for its painted burial urns and earth mounds, began c. 400 CE on the earliest dating (others place its flowering after c. 800).

    Ordinary lives

    Four lives the chronicles left out

    Empires are what the textbooks remember. These four people were caught by chance: a town dump, a clerk's grave, the chemistry of teeth, a block of ice.

    Oxyrhynchus, Middle Egypt · 36–52 CE

    Saraeus, a wet-nurse, and Tryphon, a weaver

    Tryphon son of Dionysius wove cloth in a Nile-valley town under Roman rule; in 36 CE he married Saraeus, daughter of Apion, in an agreement mostly about her dowry. She took in work as a wet-nurse. A man named Pesouris had picked up a baby boy, Heraclas, from a rubbish heap, where unwanted infants were left, to raise as his slave, and paid Saraeus 8 staters to nurse him. The foundling died, and Pesouris tried to take her own baby son instead. In 49 CE the district governor, judging by the child's features, let her keep him if she paid the wages back. Three years later Tryphon was excused from service for "cataract and shortness of sight."

    How we know Papyri from the town's rubbish mounds: the court record P.Oxy. I 37 (49 CE), Tryphon's petition P.Oxy. I 38, his release P.Oxy. I 39 (52 CE) and the marriage agreement P.Oxy. II 267 (36 CE), published by Grenfell and Hunt.

    Yunmeng, Hubei, China · 262–217 BCE

    Xi, a clerk of Qin

    Xi was born in 262 BCE and spent his life as a small working part of the Qin state: a scribe at 18, county clerk at Anlu and then at Yan, a judicial clerk from 235 BCE, and with the army on campaign in 234 and 232. He died in 217 BCE, four years after Qin unified China, and was buried with 1,155 bamboo slips: the laws he applied and a year-by-year chronicle that includes his own career. The statutes he copied set grain rations for convict labourers and bondservants by sex and age, and ordered conscripts whose city wall fell down within a year to rebuild it without the work counting toward their labour duty.

    How we know Tomb 11 at Shuihudi, excavated in December 1975. His career comes from the chronicle (Biannian ji) among the slips; the laws are translated in A. F. P. Hulsewé, Remnants of Ch'in Law (1985).

    Teotihuacan, central Mexico · c. 200–550 CE

    A Zapotec family in the great city

    In the neighbourhood now called Tlailotlacan lived families from the Valley of Oaxaca. They built Teotihuacan-style apartment compounds but buried their dead in Zapotec-style tombs, one with an antechamber, and kept Zapotec pottery in use. Oxygen isotopes in their bones and teeth show a first wave of migrants and then, generations later, people who still spent their childhoods in the homeland before coming back. The city depended on newcomers: in the Tlajinga district about 45% of the people sampled were migrants, and so many newborns were buried at Teopancazco that its excavator, Linda Manzanilla, argues the city grew by migration more than by births.

    How we know Excavated tombs and pottery in the enclave; oxygen isotopes in bone and tooth enamel (White et al. 1998, 2004); strontium and oxygen in Tlajinga (Buckley et al. 2021); burials at Teopancazco (Manzanilla 2017). Isotopes say where a person drank water as a child, not what they called themselves.

    Ukok Plateau, Altai, southern Siberia · 5th century BCE

    A tattooed woman of the high steppe

    Under a kurgan on the Ukok Plateau, in a coffin cut from a single larch trunk, lay a woman of the Pazyryk culture who died aged about 20–30, with six harnessed horses beside her. Ice that formed in the chamber kept her deer tattoos and her clothes: a shirt of wild tussah silk that her excavator thought came from India or south of China, a red woollen skirt, felt boots, a tall headdress. At her waist was a bag holding a fragment of a Chinese bronze mirror set in a carved wooden frame, and a few Indian glass beads. Medical imaging in 2014 suggested breast cancer and injuries from a fall.

    How we know Excavation of the frozen chamber by Natalia Polosmak's team in 1993; tree-ring study of the chamber logs; later medical imaging. Many Altaians regard her as an ancestor, and whether she should have been disturbed is still argued.

    Seen whole

    Seven ways to hold seventeen centuries

    How we know

    • Decipherment. Cuneiform was cracked from the trilingual Behistun cliff inscription (Henry Rawlinson and colleagues, 1830s–50s), unlocking Ashurbanipal's 30,000-tablet library; Michael Ventris deciphered Linear B as Greek in 1952, turning the Bronze Age palaces of scene one into readable archives.
    • Numismatics. Coins are self-dating government documents: Roman denarius hoards buried in Tamil Nadu are hard evidence for the monsoon trade the Periplus describes.
    • Papyrology. The rubbish mounds of Oxyrhynchus in Egypt preserved some 500,000 papyrus fragments — tax receipts, shopping lists, lost plays — everyday antiquity, not just the classics.
    • Shipwreck archaeology. The Antikythera wreck (c. 60 BCE) carried the geared mechanism; every excavated cargo is a freeze-frame of a single voyage's trade.
    • Ice cores. Greenland ice records lead pollution from Roman silver smelting — an economic index that peaks under Augustus and collapses during the Antonine Plague and the third-century crisis. Volcanic fallout is read the same way: well-dated fallout layers in six Arctic cores put a huge eruption in early 43 BCE, and the chemistry of its glassy ash pins it on Alaska's Okmok. Tying that cold to Rome's politics, though, is inference, not measurement.
    • Tree rings and isotopes. Ring widths and stable isotopes in junipers from Gordion date the Hittite-era drought to 1198–1196 BCE, give or take three years. That is a regional record from one set of trees, and matching a drought's date to a collapse does not prove the drought caused it.
    • Radiocarbon, and its blind spot. AMS dating of tiny samples moved Yayoi rice five centuries earlier in 2003; screening 1,434 East Polynesian dates for reliable short-lived material cut that region's settlement date by centuries (Wilmshurst et al. 2011). But the calibration curve is almost flat from about 800 to 400 BCE (the "Hallstatt plateau"), so radiocarbon dates in the first half of this chapter often cannot be narrowed below two or three centuries — which is why ranges like Jiigurru's run so wide.
    • Ancient DNA. Genomes show Saladoid newcomers almost wholly replacing the Caribbean's Archaic islanders (Nägele et al. 2020; Fernandes et al. 2021), net migration from the Near East into imperial Rome (127 genomes; Antonio et al. 2019), and women in the richest graves on the Xiongnu frontier (Lee et al. 2023). Pathogen DNA has confirmed falciparum malaria in Roman Italy, while no ancient DNA yet identifies the Antonine Plague. Samples are small (the Xiongnu study has 18 people), DNA rarely survives in hot, wet ground, and genes are not the same as language or identity. See reading bodies and molecules.
    • Lidar. Airborne laser scanning found the Upano valley's 6,000 platforms and roads under the forest canopy. It shows form, not date or who governed: only excavation and radiocarbon turn a lidar map into a history (seeing from above).
    • Languages and outsiders' texts. The Bantu, Proto-Sámi and Proto-Polynesian stories lean on historical linguistics, whose dates are estimates with wide error. Much of what we know of Kush, the Trưng sisters and the Fenni was written by outsiders or by the conquering side — Strabo, the Hou Hanshu, Tacitus — often centuries later or at second hand.
    • Survivorship. We read the "classics" largely because medieval monasteries chose to copy them; most ancient literature is lost. See How We Know — and what remains contested.

    In the hand

    Objects from the classical world

    Lydian electrum stater

    c. 600 BCE

    A lump of gold-silver alloy stamped with a lion and a royal guarantee of weight — the first true coinage, and the seed of the money economy.

    Olmec colossal head

    c. 900 BCE

    A multi-ton basalt portrait in a close-fitting helmet, hauled tens of kilometres — the monumental signature of the Americas' first great civilization.

    Nok terracotta head

    c. 500 BCE

    A fired-clay portrait with almond eyes from Nigeria's Nok culture — companion to some of Africa's earliest iron smelting, south of the Sahara.

    Ashoka's lion capital

    c. 250 BCE

    A polished sandstone column crowned with lions and a wheel of law — Ashoka carved dhamma across India and helped send Buddhism out to the world.

    Terracotta Army soldier

    c. 210 BCE

    One of thousands of individually modelled clay warriors guarding Qin Shi Huang's tomb — the standardized might of newly unified China, made eternal.

    Dong Son bronze drum

    c. 400 BCE

    A waisted bronze drum with a sunburst tympanum, cast in the Red River valley and traded from Yunnan to Java — the sound of Southeast Asia's own bronze age.

    Hopewell mica hand

    c. 100 CE

    A human hand cut from a single sheet of shimmering Appalachian mica, buried in an Ohio mound — grave art from a trade network that spanned half of North America.

    Moche stirrup-spout vessel

    c. 300 CE

    A ceramic portrait of an individual face, topped by the Andes' signature looping spout — Moche potters left a gallery of real people where no writing survives.

    Jiigurru potsherd

    c. 1000–600 BCE

    A plain fragment of pot made on Jiigurru (Lizard Island) from the island's own granite-derived sand — among the oldest securely dated pottery in Australia, left by Aboriginal voyagers on the Great Barrier Reef.

    Saladoid white-on-red bowl

    c. 500 BCE – 500 CE

    Red-slipped pottery painted in white, carried by the potter-farmers who crossed from the Orinoco to Puerto Rico and the Lesser Antilles — the signature of the Caribbean's Ceramic Age.

    Head of Augustus from Meroë

    made c. 27–25 BCE

    A bronze head, with glass and calcite eyes, taken from a statue in Roman Egypt by Kushite raiders and buried beneath a temple stair at Meroë so that worshippers walked over the emperor. Found in 1910; now in the British Museum.

    Ipiutak ivory burial mask

    c. 100 BCE – 800 CE

    A composite mask of carved ivory pieces from the Ipiutak cemetery at Point Hope, Alaska, excavated 1939–41 — openwork art from a coastal site of nearly 500 house pits, though only 20–30 houses were lived in at any one time.
