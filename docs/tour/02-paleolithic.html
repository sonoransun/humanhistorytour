    Chapter II  ·  300 kya – 12,000 BCE

    Paleolithic & the
    Cognitive Revolution

    c. 300,000 years ago – 12,000 BCE

    For nine-tenths of our history being human meant sharing the planet with other humans — and the oldest painted animal we have found is a wild pig on a Sulawesi cave wall, not a European bison.

    Scroll to begin, 300,000 years ago

    The chapter before us

    Chapter I ended with a genus, not a species: Homo erectus walking out of Africa, the Acheulean handaxe holding one design for a million years, fire domesticated, and brains growing faster than any primate's had before. What carried forward into this chapter is the whole inheritance — an upright body built for walking, hands that knap stone, a hearth, a childhood long enough to teach, and cousins already scattered from Iberia to Java. What is new is the thing none of that predicts: a mind that makes marks which stand for something else.

    The Paleolithic is the longest chapter in the human story — roughly nineteen of every twenty years our species has existed — and the one in which we become recognisably ourselves. Between the first Homo sapiens in Africa and the eve of farming, people acquired symbolic language, art, music, ritual burial, and a restless curiosity that carried them onto nearly every habitable continent.

    It is also the last era in which we were not alone. Modern humans met, learned from, and interbred with Neanderthals and Denisovans, whose DNA still lives in us — every person outside Africa carries a sliver of a vanished cousin. To frame this deep past correctly is to see "civilisation" as a thin, recent veneer over a far older, richer, and thoroughly global human experience.

    And it was global from the start. The creative mind switched on across the whole map at once — from a red ochre workshop at Blombos to a painted pig in Sulawesi to the lion-headed ivory of the Swabian Jura — while a single great dispersal filled Australia by sea and, last of all, reached the Americas at the tip of Patagonia.

    Kinds of human alive at once

    ≥5

    At least five for most of the chapter: Homo sapiens, Neanderthals, Denisovans, Homo floresiensis and Homo luzonensis. Homo naledi (~335–236 kya) and Java's last Homo erectus (~117–108 kya) overlap its early part. By ~40–30 kya, one. Dirks et al. 2017 · Rizal et al. 2020 · Sutikna et al. 2016 · Détroit et al. 2019

    Sea level at the glacial maximum

    −120 m

    Ice sheets stood at their limits ~26,500–19,000 years ago. About 120 m is the classic figure, from Barbados corals; a 2014 global reconstruction puts the fall at up to ~134 m around 21,000 years ago. Fairbanks 1989 · Clark et al. 2009 · Lambeck et al. 2014

    People alive, c. 10,000 BCE

    4.5M

    The nearest date to this chapter's close with published estimates: HYDE 3.3, the series every population counter on this tour follows, gives ~4.5 million; McEvedy & Jones 4 million. No census exists; both extrapolate from how thinly recent foragers live, and informed guesses run from about 1 to 10 million. HYDE 3.3 · McEvedy & Jones 1978

    Neanderthal DNA in living non-Africans

    ~2%

    1.5–2.1% on the first high-quality genome (Altai, 2014), revised to 1.8–2.6% with a second (Vindija, 2017); East Asians carry a little more than Europeans. Papuans and Aboriginal Australians also carry ~3–5% Denisovan DNA. Prüfer et al. 2014 · Prüfer et al. 2017

    01 · Origin

    One birthplace, still

    Everyone alive descends from African Homo sapiens. Earlier probes reached the Levant — Misliya ~180 kya, Skhul and Qafzeh ~120–90 kya — but left no lasting line. The story here still begins on one continent.

    02 · Out of Africa

    The pulse that stuck

    Around 70–60,000 years ago a wave crossed the Horn and the Levant and did not turn back. This single dispersal founded every non-African population on Earth — the trunk from which all later branches grow.

    03 · Australia

    Across open water

    Reaching Sahul — Australia joined to New Guinea — meant deliberate open-sea crossings through the islands of Wallacea. By ~65,000 years ago — or nearer 50,000 on most genetic estimates for Sahul as a whole — people were at Madjedbebe: among the earliest clear evidence of deliberate open-sea voyaging by our species, and the start of the world's longest continuous culture.

    04 · Eurasia

    Two arms across a continent

    By ~45,000 years ago sapiens had swept west into Ice-Age Europe and east across Central Asia to China — meeting Neanderthals and Denisovans, and carrying a little of each away in their blood.

    05 · The Americas

    The last continent

    When sea levels fell, the Beringian bridge opened. Footprints at White Sands (~23 kya) and a camp at Monte Verde in Chile (~14.5 kya, though a 2026 re-analysis disputes it) show people reaching the last uninhabited landmass — the global spread complete.

    Long before the first field or the first city, someone ground red ochre, cut a careful diamond of lines into stone, and meant something by it.

    01 · Company

    The last era in which we were not alone

    Sapiens in Africa. Neanderthals from Wales to the Altai. Denisovans from Siberia to Harbin in the north-east, the Tibetan Plateau, the Penghu Channel off Taiwan and Laos — and, on genetic evidence, probably far into island Southeast Asia. Homo floresiensis on Flores and Homo luzonensis on Luzon, both small-bodied islanders. Five kinds of human at minimum, and for almost the whole history of our genus that plurality was simply the normal state of the world.

    02 · Neanderthals

    Reconsidered, and then reconsidered again

    Three hundred and thirty-six metres inside Bruniquel Cave in the Aveyron, someone snapped some four hundred stalagmite sections into even lengths, laid them in two rings, and lit fires on them. Uranium-series on the regrown calcite puts it at ~176,000 years ago — some 130 millennia before sapiens are securely attested in Europe, in total darkness, which means planned lighting and division of labour. At Shanidar they returned to one rock to lay out their dead across generations. Their birch-tar glue requires heating bark in a low-oxygen burn — a process you cannot see while doing it. Their brains averaged larger than ours.

    03 · Denisovans

    A people named from their DNA

    In 2010 a fingertip bone from Denisova Cave yielded a genome belonging to no human anyone had ever described — a whole population named before it had a face. Since then: a jawbone from Xiahe on the Tibetan Plateau identified by its collagen rather than its DNA, a molar from Laos, a jaw dredged from the Penghu Channel off Taiwan, Denisovan DNA lifted straight out of cave sediment with no fossil at all — and, in 2025, a face: the near-complete Harbin cranium from northeast China, assigned by its proteins and by mitochondrial DNA scraped from its dental calculus. And Denisova 11 — "Denny" — a girl whose mother was Neanderthal and whose father was Denisovan. A first-generation hybrid, from a scrap of bone about two and a half centimetres long.

    04 · The ice

    What a hundred and twenty metres buys

    At the Last Glacial Maximum, ~26,500–19,000 years ago, the ice sheets held enough water to drop the global sea about 120 metres. Britain joined the continent across the drowned plain we call Doggerland; the Sunda shelf welded Southeast Asia into one landmass; Beringia opened as a cold grassland a thousand kilometres wide. Roads appeared and closed on the same tide — and the coasts people actually walked are now, all of them, under water.

    05 · Alone

    And then there was one

    Neanderthals vanish from the record by ~40,000 years ago. The Denisovan trail fades soon after — a rib from the Tibetan Plateau may be as young as ~32,000 years, and Papuan genomes hint at mixing as late as ~30,000; Flores and Luzon fall silent by ~50,000. Competition, absorption, disease, climate, or plain arithmetic in populations that never numbered more than tens of thousands — the cause is genuinely unsettled. The result is the strangest fact about the present: of every way there has been of being human, exactly one is left, carrying fragments of the others in its blood.

    01 · Symbol

    The first meaning

    At Blombos, ochre is ground in a shell "paint pot", a block is engraved with a deliberate cross-hatch, and snail shells are pierced for a necklace. Abstract thought and personal ornament — tens of millennia before any European cave.

    02 · Image

    The oldest painted animal

    A red warty pig with three human-like figures around it at Leang Karampuang is the earliest known figurative painting — and the earliest known narrative scene — at least 51,200 years old by laser-ablation uranium-series (2024). A life-size pig at nearby Leang Tedongnge (≥45,500) and an animal at Lubang Jeriji Saléh in Borneo (≥40,000) follow. The Europe-first story of art is simply wrong.

    03 · Myth & music

    Lion-men and flutes

    The Aurignacian brings the ivory Löwenmensch — a lion-headed human, the oldest known myth in the round — the Venus of Hohle Fels, and flutes cut from bird bone and mammoth ivory: the first music we can hold.

    04 · Gallery

    The Ice-Age cathedral

    More than twenty millennia later, Magdalenian painters fill the halls of Lascaux with aurochs, horses and deer. A summit of Ice-Age art — but a late one, standing on some 60,000 years of mark-making that began in Africa and Asia.

    01 · Sunda & Sahul

    Two ice-age continents, and the water that never closed

    At the glacial low the Sunda shelf joined Sumatra, Java and Borneo to mainland Asia. Niah Cave, 15 km from the Sarawak coast today, already stood well inland of a much lower sea when people began living there ~46,000 years ago. To the south-east, Australia, New Guinea and Tasmania were one continent, Sahul, until the returning sea flooded Torres Strait and Bass Strait. The two never joined: the deep channels of Wallacea stayed open water at every low stand, so the first Australians came over the sea, by boat or raft. Sulawesi, midway, and Muna, an island just off its south-east coast, hold the oldest dated rock art yet found: a hand stencil on Muna with a minimum age of 67,800 years (reported in 2026, not yet widely tested), and at Leang Karampuang on Sulawesi human-like figures around a pig painted at least 51,200 years ago, the oldest known scene that tells a story. That early presence is often read as favouring a northern route, by Sulawesi and the Moluccas to New Guinea, over a southern one by Timor. Beyond the water: Madjedbebe by ~65,000 years ago (contested; ~50,000 is the cautious reading) and burials at Lake Mungo, far inland, ~40,000 years ago.

    02 · Beringia

    A grassland where the strait is now

    Where the Bering Strait runs today, the fallen sea exposed a plain up to 1,000 km wide from Siberia to Alaska — cold, dry steppe-tundra, not ice. People were already at the Yana Rhinoceros Horn Site, 71° N in Arctic Siberia, by ~32,000 years ago, and populations closely related to them contributed part of the ancestry of every Native American. Genomes point to a "Beringian standstill": a founding population that developed apart from its Asian relatives for several thousand years around the glacial maximum — whether on the land bridge itself or in north-east Asia is still argued. An infant buried at Upward Sun River in Alaska ~11,500 years ago belonged to a lineage, the Ancient Beringians, that branched from other Native Americans ~22,000–18,000 years ago; the bridge itself went under about 11,000 years ago.

    03 · The Americas

    South, while the ice still stood

    The oldest well-dated evidence in the Americas falls inside the glacial maximum itself: footprints, mostly of teenagers and children, at White Sands in New Mexico, dated ~23,000–21,000 years ago from seeds, then independently from pollen and luminescence (2023) and lake mud (2025) — though some researchers remain sceptical. At Paisley Caves in Oregon, coprolites carrying human DNA date to ~14,300 years ago, though some still doubt they are human. Monte Verde in southern Chile — a camp with seaweed carried from a coast then tens of kilometres away — has anchored "pre-Clovis" at ~14,500 years ago since the 1990s; a 2026 re-analysis arguing it is far younger is itself disputed. The inland ice-free corridor probably did not open until ~14,000 years ago, so many researchers favour a route down the Pacific coast — the line on this map is that hypothesis, not a finding, traced along a shore that is now under the sea.

    04 · Europe & the Gulf

    Refuges in the south, dry land under today's seas

    In Europe the ice pushed people south. Britain was probably empty through the coldest millennia, and Doggerland — the plain under today's southern North Sea — lay as cold tundra at the edge of the ice; its richest human record comes later, from Mesolithic foragers who lived there until the sea took it, roughly 10,000–7,000 years ago. The Franco-Cantabrian refuge of south-west France and northern Spain held on, and its caves carry the longest run of Ice-Age painting: Chauvet ~37,000–33,500 years ago, Altamira across some twenty millennia, from a red sign at least 35,600 years old to its Magdalenian bison, Lascaux ~17,000. Far to the east the Persian Gulf, a shallow sea at most ~90–100 m deep, was nearly all dry land crossed by the Tigris and Euphrates; the sea began flooding back in some 14,000–12,000 years ago and reached roughly today's shore by ~6,000 — so whether the basin sheltered people, as Jeffrey Rose's "Gulf Oasis" hypothesis proposes, cannot be tested on land.

    05 · What the sea took

    A hundred and twenty metres of missing coast

    From about 19,000 years ago the ice sheets melted and the sea rose roughly 120 metres, most of it by about 7,000 years ago — around a metre a century on average, and far faster in pulses: near 14,500 years ago it climbed more than a dozen metres within a few centuries. The coasts people walked for the whole of this chapter — shores that fed them fish and shellfish, and the likeliest roads into Sahul and the Americas — now lie on the drowned shelves and surface only by accident. Near Marseille, Cosquer Cave, painted ~27,000 and ~19,000 years ago when the shore lay kilometres to the south, is now entered through a passage whose mouth is 37 m under the sea, and four-fifths of the cave has flooded, taking whatever art was there. A jaw dredged up by fishermen from the Penghu Channel off Taiwan — the floor of a strait that was dry land at glacial lows — was identified from its proteins in 2025 as a male Denisovan, of uncertain age (either 10,000–70,000 or 130,000–190,000 years old). The scarcity of early coastal sites is a gap in the record, not proof that no one lived by the sea.

    Milestones

    Thirty-eight turning points

    ~300 kya

    Homo sapiens — Jebel Irhoud

    Jebel Irhoud, Morocco

    The oldest fossils of our own species push the origin of anatomically modern humans back to ~300,000 years and support a pan-African emergence — sapiens arising across an interconnected continent, not in a single cradle.

    ~176 kya

    The rings of Bruniquel Cave

    Aveyron, France

    Neanderthals broke some four hundred stalagmite sections into even lengths, laid them in two rings 336 m underground, and burned fires on them. Uranium-series dating by Sophie Verheyden and Jacques Jaubert put it at ~176,000 years — deliberate construction in total darkness, some 130 millennia before sapiens are securely attested in Europe.

    ~150 kya

    Bété I — people in the rainforest

    Southern Côte d'Ivoire, West Africa

    Stone tools at Bété I, dated by paired luminescence and electron-spin-resonance methods to around 150,000 years ago, lie in sediments whose plant waxes, isotopes, phytoliths and pollen all record wet tropical forest. Published in 2025, it is the oldest clear association yet known between people and rainforest: the forest was no barrier to early Homo sapiens, and West Africa belongs in the story of our origins.

    ~100–50 kya

    Behavioural modernity

    Africa, with global expression

    The "Cognitive Revolution": symbolic language, abstraction, planning, and cumulative culture. Scholars still debate a sudden leap against a slow African accretion — the revolution that may not have been a revolution at all.

    ~100–70 kya

    Symbolism at Blombos Cave

    Blombos, South Africa

    Cross-hatched engraved ochre, an ochre-processing "paint workshop", and pierced Nassarius shell beads — among the earliest secure evidence of abstract thought and personal ornament, long before European cave art.

    ~74 kya

    Toba, and the tools at Jwalapuram

    Andhra Pradesh, India

    The Toba supereruption buried South Asia in ash, and at Jwalapuram stone tools lie directly below and above that layer. Somebody was there before and after: the "Toba bottleneck" as a near-extinction of humanity is now the weakest reading of the evidence, and South Asia enters the story as a place people lived, not merely passed through.

    ~70–60 kya

    The great Out-of-Africa dispersal

    NE Africa → the Levant

    The migration pulse that founded every non-African population. Earlier excursions (Misliya ~180 kya, Skhul–Qafzeh ~120–90 kya) show repeated tries; this one succeeded and seeded Eurasia, Oceania, and the Americas.

    ~70–50 kya

    Shanidar Cave

    Zagros Mountains, Iraqi Kurdistan

    Ralph Solecki excavated ten Neanderthals here in the 1950s, one of whom lived for years with a withered arm and a blind eye — meaning others fed him. Emma Pomeroy's team recovered another, Shanidar Z, against the same rock in 2018–20: a single place returned to for the dead over generations, whatever one concludes about the disputed "flower burial" pollen.

    ~65 kya (contested)

    Peopling of Sahul

    Madjedbebe, N. Australia

    Reaching the Australia–New Guinea landmass demanded deliberate open-water crossings through Wallacea — among the earliest clear evidence of seafaring by our species, and the start of the world's longest continuous cultural tradition. The 65,000-year luminescence date is disputed; most genetic estimates put the settlement of Sahul nearer 50,000 years ago.

    ~50–43 kya

    Interbreeding with Neanderthals

    Southwest Asia

    As sapiens moved north they met and had children with Neanderthals; every non-African carries ~1.5–2% Neanderthal DNA, shaping immunity, skin, and hair. Genomics dates the main gene-flow pulse to about 47,000 years ago.

    ~200–30 kya

    Denisovans & their legacy

    Denisova Cave, Siberia

    A distinct archaic lineage known mostly from DNA and a few bones. Living Papuans and Aboriginal Australians carry ~3–5% Denisovan ancestry, and a Denisovan EPAS1 variant gives Tibetans their high-altitude adaptation.

    ~90 kya

    Denisova 11 — "Denny"

    Altai Mountains, Siberia

    A bone splinter about two and a half centimetres long, sequenced by Viviane Slon in Svante Pääbo's laboratory, turned out to be a girl with a Neanderthal mother and a Denisovan father — a first-generation hybrid, found almost on the first try. That it was found at all implies such children were ordinary, not freakish.

    ~59–51 kya

    Chagyrskaya — a Neanderthal family

    Altai Mountains, Siberia

    Genomes from eleven Neanderthals in Chagyrskaya Cave and two in nearby Okladnikov (2022) include a father and his daughter. Long runs of homozygous DNA, up to a third of some genomes, mark a small community, and Y-chromosome diversity an order of magnitude below mitochondrial diversity is best explained by women moving between communities — the first genetic glimpse of how a Neanderthal community was organised.

    ~51 kya

    Oldest figurative art — Sulawesi

    Leang Karampuang, Indonesia

    A red-ochre warty pig and three human-like figures on a Sulawesi cave wall — the oldest known figurative painting and the oldest narrative scene, dated to at least 51,200 years in 2024 — decisively overturn the Europe-first story of art; the nearby Leang Tedongnge pig (≥45.5 kya) and Borneo's Lubang Jeriji Saléh animal (≥40 kya) follow.

    ~49–44 kya

    Ivane Valley — the New Guinea highlands

    Papua New Guinea, ~2,000 m

    Within a few millennia of reaching Sahul, people were living 2,000 metres up in cold montane forest, eating pandanus nuts and yams. Their waisted stone tools suggest they cleared forest patches to encourage useful plants — managing a landscape tens of millennia before New Guinea became one of the world's independent homes of farming.

    ~49 kya

    Warratyi — the arid interior

    Flinders Ranges, South Australia

    A rock shelter in the dry interior was occupied by about 49,000 years ago, some ten millennia earlier than previously known for Australia's arid zone. Its layers hold ochre, early bone and hafted tools, and bones of the giant marsupial Diprotodon beside artefacts — by its excavators' account the only reliably dated, stratified case of people and extinct megafauna together.

    ~48 kya

    Fa-Hien Lena — bows in the rainforest

    Sri Lanka

    Bone points bearing the fractures of projectile impact make this rainforest cave among the earliest evidence of bow-and-arrow hunting outside Africa (only Grotte Mandrin in France, ~54,000 years ago, has an older claim, itself debated), found with a complex symbolic kit. It is also one of our species' oldest rainforest homes beyond Africa, and its excavators argue the bow was taken up here for small, quick animals of the canopy rather than big game on open plains.

    ~46 kya onward

    Niah Cave and the Hoabinhian

    Borneo & mainland Southeast Asia

    Occupation layers at Niah show people living in rainforest by ~46,000 years ago, and the "Deep Skull" itself (~37,000 years) is among the oldest well-dated modern-human remains in island Southeast Asia. It was a far harder living than open grassland, requiring detoxified plants and small-game trapping. The later Hoabinhian toolkit ran from Vietnam to Sumatra for millennia: Southeast Asia was a homeland, not a corridor.

    ~45 kya

    Sopochnaya Karga — the Arctic, earlier than thought

    Yenisei Gulf, Arctic Siberia, 72° N

    A frozen mammoth carcass bears wounds from weapons, inflicted before and after death: people were hunting at 72° north about 45,000 years ago, some thirteen millennia before Yana. A hunted wolf of similar age from a distant site suggests they had already spread widely across northern Siberia.

    ~45–40 kya

    The Aurignacian "creative explosion"

    Swabian Jura, Germany

    Sapiens' arrival in Europe brings the ivory Löwenmensch (Lion-man), the Venus of Hohle Fels, and bird-bone and ivory flutes — the earliest musical instruments and the first mythic imagination carved in the round.

    ~40 kya

    Lake Mungo burials

    Willandra Lakes, Australia

    Mungo Lady is among the world's earliest known cremations and Mungo Man an ochre-sprinkled burial — profound evidence of mortuary ritual. Ancestral remains of deep significance to Aboriginal communities.

    ~37–33.5 kya

    Chauvet Cave

    Ardèche, France

    Charcoal lions, rhinos, and horses drawn with shading, perspective, and the rock's own contours — proof that fully modern artistic technique existed at the very start of the European Upper Palaeolithic, not only its end.

    ~33 kya onward

    Eggshell beads — a southern African network

    Highland Lesotho, southern Africa

    Strontium isotopes in ostrich-eggshell beads from Lesotho's highland shelters show some were made from shell laid in other regions, several hundred kilometres away at the least, from ~33,000 years ago onward. Among San foragers such gift exchange binds partners who can call on each other in hard times; the ice-age network looks like the same insurance, built in an unstable climate.

    ~32 kya

    Yana Rhinoceros Horn Site

    Arctic Siberia, 71° N

    Hunters camped five hundred kilometres inside the Arctic Circle 32,000 years ago, leaving eyed needles, mammoth-ivory beads and a diadem. Tailored clothing — not fire alone — is what made the far north habitable, and the "Ancient North Siberians" of Yana sit close to the root of the ancestry that would later cross into the Americas.

    ~32 kya

    Kilu Cave — out to the Solomons

    Buka Island, northern Solomons

    Reaching Buka meant crossing at least 60 km of open sea from Nissan, beyond the edge of Sahul, and people were there ~32,000 years ago — the oldest known occupation in the Solomon Islands. Starch grains and needle-like crystals on the cave's stone tools come from taro (Colocasia and Alocasia): the root crops later farmed across the Pacific were already being eaten.

    ~26.5 kya (contested)

    Chiquihuite Cave

    Zacatecas, Mexico

    Nearly two thousand limestone flakes from a cave 2,700 m up were dated to ~26,500 years, which would put people in Mesoamerica during the height of the ice. Sceptics read the flakes as geology, not tools; either way this is the sharpest live test of how early the Americas were reached.

    ~26.5–19 kya

    Last Glacial Maximum

    Global

    Peak ice sheets and the lowest sea levels of the era exposed land bridges (Beringia) and shelves (Sunda, Sahul), reshaping coasts and routes. Populations retreated to refugia; the cold framed the timing of the Americas' peopling.

    ~23–13 kya

    Peopling of the Americas

    Beringia to Patagonia

    Human footprints at White Sands (~23–21 kya) and the camp at Monte Verde, Chile (~14,500 years) place arrival well before the old "Clovis-first" model — humans reach the last uninhabited continent, completing the global spread. A 2026 geological re-analysis arguing that Monte Verde's camp is far younger is itself disputed by the site's excavators and others.

    ~20–16 kya

    The first pottery — Xianrendong and Jōmon

    Jiangxi, China; the Japanese archipelago

    Cooking pots appear at Xianrendong ~20,000 years ago and across Jōmon Japan by ~16,000 — made by mobile foragers, ten millennia before any farmer. Pottery vessels were invented to boil fish, shellfish and nuts, which inverts the textbook order in which pottery follows agriculture.

    ~17 kya

    Lascaux Cave

    Dordogne, France

    The Magdalenian "Sistine Chapel of prehistory" — halls of aurochs, horses, and deer with enigmatic signs. A summit of Ice-Age art, balanced here against the older non-European work that came before it.

    ~16.6–15.3 kya

    Cooper's Ferry — before the corridor opened

    Lower Salmon River, Idaho, USA

    Radiocarbon dating places the first occupation between 16,560 and 15,280 calibrated years ago, with stemmed points unlike Clovis and closer to north-east Asian traditions. That is before the inland ice-free corridor opened (≤14,800 years ago), so the site strongly supports a first entry down the Pacific coast.

    ~15–13.7 kya

    Taforalt — wild nuts and rotten teeth

    Grotte des Pigeons, north-east Morocco

    In the earliest known cemetery in the Maghreb, foragers who harvested acorns and pine nuts had caries in 51% of adult teeth — a rate like that of modern sugar-eating populations, thousands of years before any farming. Genomes from the cave (2018) put about a third of their ancestry with sub-Saharan Africans and the rest closest to the Natufians of the Levant.

    ~50–11 kya

    Late Pleistocene extinctions

    Global (esp. Australia, Americas)

    Mammoths, giant ground sloths, sabre-tooth cats, and giant marsupials vanished as humans spread and climate lurched — a still-debated mix of hunting and warming, and an early lesson in humanity reshaping ecosystems.

    ~15,000+ ya

    Domestication of the dog

    Eurasia

    The grey wolf became the first domesticated animal — a partnership for hunting, warmth, and company, confirmed by burials like Bonn-Oberkassel (~14,200 years). A prelude to the domestications that open the Neolithic.

    ~14,500–11,500 ya

    The Natufians

    Levant

    Foragers who built stone-founded houses, harvested wild cereals with sickles, buried their dead beneath the floor — one with a hand resting on a puppy — and stopped moving. Sedentism arrives before farming, not after it; at Shubayqa in Jordan a flatbread was baked from wild grain ~14,400 years ago.

    ≥13.4 kya

    Jebel Sahaba — violence on the Nile

    Near Wadi Halfa, northern Sudan

    Sixty-one people were buried here, and a 2021 re-examination found more than a hundred previously unrecorded wounds, mostly from projectiles, some with stone points still embedded; a quarter of the injured had both healed and fresh trauma. Not one battle but repeated raids, probably driven by the environmental upheaval at the end of the ice age, at this chapter's edge — one of the earliest sites of interpersonal violence known anywhere.

    ~13–12 kya

    Naia — the girl in Hoyo Negro

    Quintana Roo, Yucatán, Mexico

    A girl of 15 to 17 probably died by falling into a pit in a then-dry cave, among the bones of extinct megafauna; the rising sea later flooded it, and divers found her near-complete skeleton in 2007. Her teeth show heavy decay and gum disease from a carbohydrate-rich diet. A 2014 claim that her mitochondrial DNA links the first Americans to living Native Americans was questioned in 2015.

    ~12,900–11,700 ya

    The Younger Dryas

    Northern Hemisphere

    A twelve-century snap back to near-glacial cold, most plausibly triggered by glacial meltwater flooding the North Atlantic and stalling its circulation. Wild cereal stands thinned and Natufian villages contracted — and when the warmth returned, people who had spent generations harvesting grain began planting it.

    Why it happened

    Ice, arithmetic, and a species that was never crowded

    Told as a story about ideas, the Paleolithic is a run of firsts: the first bead, the first painted animal, the first flute. Told as a story about causes, it is mostly about two things — the orbital wobble that decides where water sits, and the fact that there were almost no people.

    The Earth's orbit stretches and its axis tilts and wanders on cycles of roughly 100,000, 41,000 and 23,000 years. For the last million years the 100,000-year beat has dominated, giving a world that spends about ninety millennia cold and about ten warm, over and over. Our whole species evolved inside that metronome. Its loudest stroke falls in this chapter: at the Last Glacial Maximum, ~26,500 to 19,000 years ago, ice sheets held enough water to lower the sea about 120 metres. Southeast Asia fused into the Sunda shelf; Doggerland carried people dry-shod from Yorkshire to Denmark; Beringia opened as a grassland a thousand kilometres wide. Every route in this chapter was scheduled by climate — see the climate spine for the full sequence — and the chapter ends on the Younger Dryas, twelve centuries of cold that hit the Levant just as its foragers had settled down.

    Climate set the roads; what let people use them was range. The species that left Africa was already a generalist: at Bété I in Côte d'Ivoire people were living in wet tropical forest around 150,000 years ago. By the middle of this chapter Homo sapiens had taken on nearly every kind of country the planet offered — rainforest at Fa-Hien Lena in Sri Lanka and Niah in Borneo (~48,000–46,000 years ago), the Ivane Valley 2,000 metres up in New Guinea (~49,000–44,000), Fincha Habera at 3,469 metres in Ethiopia's Bale Mountains, where foragers relied on an endemic giant rodent (~47,000–31,000), the arid interior of Australia at Warratyi (~49,000), and a mammoth hunt at 72° north in Arctic Siberia (~45,000). No body is adapted to all of those at once. What travelled was a portable kit — fire, sewn clothing, projectiles, boats, and knowledge held across a network of people — which is why the spread outran any change in our biology.

    Then the arithmetic. At the start of this chapter perhaps a few hundred thousand Homo sapiens were alive, all of them in Africa; at the glacial maximum the global figure is usually put between half a million and two million; by 10,000 BCE, around four million, with honest estimates running from one to ten. That is a growth rate near 0.001% a year — the entire species adding a dozen or so people a year, on average, for three hundred millennia. Small numbers have consequences. Innovation depends on how many minds are connected: the Still Bay and Howiesons Poort industries of southern Africa appear, flourish for a few thousand years, and then simply stop, which is what happens when a technique outlives the last person who knew it. Culture in this era does not only accumulate; it flickers. The population curve that makes everything later possible has not begun to bend.

    Small numbers also decide who disappears. The oldest modern-human genomes from Europe, from Ranis in Germany and Zlatý kůň in Czechia ~45,000 years ago, belong to one small, isolated population linked by distant family ties; it is the deepest known split from the out-of-Africa lineage, and no later population has been shown to descend from it. The Neanderthals of Chagyrskaya in the Altai lived in a community so small that up to a third of some of their genomes sits in long runs of identical inherited DNA. Groups this size can vanish without being defeated by anyone — a hard winter, a failed hunt, too few partners — which is why plain arithmetic is one of the strongest candidates in the argument over the Neanderthal ending. The Altai may have been an extreme, though: a 2026 study of 27 late Neanderthals from Belgium and France found none of that close-kin mating and no build-up of harmful mutations over time — those groups were larger or better connected, and slow genetic decay does not explain their end. The same Chagyrskaya genomes give the first social fact we have for a Neanderthal community: it was the women who moved between groups.

    Thin populations also explain what is absent: epidemic disease. Measles needs a pool of roughly a quarter to half a million people in regular contact to persist at all, so it cannot have existed. Paleolithic sickness was endemic and personal — intestinal parasites, wound infection, the injuries legible on the Shanidar skeletons — plus whatever came from butchery. What pathogens do instead in this chapter is keep records: Helicobacter pylori in the human stomach and the herpes viruses split into regional lineages that mirror the dispersal out of Africa almost exactly, so a stomach bacterium can be read as a migration map. Crowd disease as a maker of history has to wait for crowds, and crowds are the next chapter's business.

    Where sickness does show in bone, it tends to follow diet rather than contact. At Taforalt in Morocco, ~15,000–13,700 years ago, foragers eating acorns and pine nuts had caries in half their adult teeth, and Naia, the girl found in the flooded cave of Hoyo Negro in Yucatán, had heavy decay and gum disease too. The encounter with other humans was also an immune exchange: variants inherited from Neanderthals still tune the innate immune response of living people, and a stretch of chromosome 3 inherited from them was identified in 2020 as the major genetic risk factor for severe COVID-19.

    There were no states, and nothing in this chapter's evidence points to slavery or any other coerced labour; the institutions the record does show are networks. From ~33,000 years ago, ostrich-eggshell beads in the Lesotho highlands were made from shell brought from regions several hundred kilometres away — gift exchange of the kind San foragers still use to call on partners when their own country fails. Violence existed too, and at least once it tracked stress: at Jebel Sahaba on the Nile, at least 13,400 years ago, sixty-one people were buried, many with wounds from repeated raids, re-read in 2021 as a response to the environmental upheaval at the end of the ice age. And some of the Earth was still empty. The Caribbean islands, home to ground sloths and a giant owl, would see no people until about 6000–5000 BCE on Trinidad, then still joined or nearly joined to South America, and about 4000 BCE on the islands beyond; the remote Pacific beyond the Solomons would see none until Lapita voyagers some three thousand years ago.

    And the labour. "Man the hunter" is a framing from a 1966 conference, not from the evidence. At Wilamaya Patjxa in the Peruvian Andes a young woman was buried ~9,000 years ago — just past this chapter's edge — with a full big-game toolkit, and Randall Haas's review of contemporaneous American burials found something between a third and a half of identified big-game hunters were female. Meanwhile the food that actually kept people alive largely does not fossilise: at Ohalo II beside the Sea of Galilee, waterlogging preserved 140 plant species and a grinding stone bearing wild-cereal starch from ~23,000 years ago. Cordage, netting and basketry — the three-ply fibre twisted at Abri du Maras ~50,000 years ago — did more calorific work than any spear. Nor was this a society of equals: at Sungir, around 34,000 years ago, two children were buried with roughly 10,000 mammoth-ivory beads, each of which took about an hour to make. Thousands of hours of labour spent on children too young to have earned any of it is the earliest glimpse of status that is inherited rather than won. By the end of this chapter the Natufians have houses, sickles and buried dogs, and the cold snap of the Younger Dryas is about to make planting look like a good idea — which is where the Neolithic begins.

    Competing explanations

    • Was there a Cognitive Revolution at all? A rapid neurological or genetic switch around 50,000 years ago, versus a slow African accretion visible in ochre, beads and bone points from ~300,000 years. The African record keeps pushing the traits earlier, which favours accretion.
    • Why the megafauna died. Overkill by human hunters, or the climate whiplash at the end of the Pleistocene. Losses track human arrival closely in Australia and the Americas, far less so in Africa, where the animals had co-evolved with hominins for millions of years — the honest answer is both, weighted differently on each continent.
    • How the Americas were reached. The interior ice-free corridor probably opened too late (~14,000 years ago) to explain the earliest sites, which favours a Pacific coastal route — but that coastline is now under 120 metres of water, so the decisive evidence may be unrecoverable. The anchors keep moving, too: White Sands has gained independent dates, while a 2026 re-analysis argues that Monte Verde's camp is no older than ~8,200 years — a claim fiercely contested.
    • Why the Neanderthals ended. Competition, absorption into a much larger incoming population, climate volatility in their refugia, or plain demographic bad luck in groups that never numbered more than a few tens of thousands. No single explanation currently wins.
    • Did Toba nearly finish us? The supereruption of ~74,000 years ago was long read as a near-extinction bottleneck; the tools above and below the ash at Jwalapuram, and revised genetic models, now make a milder disruption the better bet.
    • How old is Sahul? Luminescence dates put people at Madjedbebe ~65,000 years ago; critics argue artefacts sank through the sandy deposit, and most genetic estimates for Sahul's founders fall nearer 50,000. Independent dates from a second site of that age would settle it.
    • Did Neanderthals paint? Red marks in three Spanish caves — La Pasiega, Maltravieso and Ardales — were dated in 2018 by uranium-thorium to at least 64,800 years, before sapiens is securely known in Europe. Critics argue that thin crusts can give ages that are too old, or that some marks are natural, and a 2018 critique of the same data prefers ~47,000. If the older dates hold, symbol-making was not ours alone.
    • One cradle, or a braided stem? Genomic modelling published in 2023 fits a "weakly structured stem": two or more African populations exchanging genes for hundreds of thousands of years, which explains signals once read as mixing with an unknown archaic African "ghost".     Meanwhile, everywhere

    Three hundred millennia, sixteen regions

    For most of this chapter the human world was Africa, with cousins scattered across Eurasia; by its end people lived on every continent except Antarctica. Here is what each region's evidence shows — and, where nobody lived yet, what did.

    West Asia

    ~180–14 kya

    The first sapiens known outside Africa turn up here — a jaw at Misliya on Mount Carmel (~180 kya), burials at Skhul and Qafzeh (~120–90 kya) — in forays that left no lasting line. Between 121,000 and 112,000 years ago, in a green phase of Arabia, people, probably sapiens, left footprints beside elephant tracks at the Alathar lake in the Nefud. Neanderthals held the Zagros and the Levant, returning to Shanidar for their dead. At Ohalo II on the Sea of Galilee (~23 kya) foragers slept on grass bedding and ground wild cereals, and by ~14,500 years ago the Natufians were building stone-founded houses.

    North Africa & the Nile

    ~300–13 kya

    The oldest known fossils of our species come from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco (~300 kya), and shell beads from Bizmoune Cave are at least ~142,000 years old. When wet phases turned the Sahara to grassland and lakes, people crossed it; at Nazlet Khater in Upper Egypt they were mining chert ~33,000 years ago. On the Nile, repeated raids at Jebel Sahaba (≥13.4 kya) left wounds on many of the sixty-one dead, and at Taforalt (~15–13.7 kya) foragers living on acorns and pine nuts had caries in half their teeth.

    West & Central Africa

    ~150–13 kya

    At Bété I in Côte d'Ivoire people were living in wet tropical forest ~150,000 years ago — the oldest known rainforest home of our species. On the Semliki river in DR Congo, people at Katanda carved barbed bone harpoons ~90,000 years ago, and the notched Ishango bone comes from the same waters (perhaps ~20,000 years ago; its date is uncertain). Middle Stone Age toolmaking lasted in Senegal until ~11,000 years ago, the latest yet documented in Africa, and a skull from Iho Eleru (long published as Iwo Eleru) in Nigeria (~13 kya) keeps archaic-looking features — signs of old, long-separate populations like those geneticists read in West African genomes.

    East & Southern Africa

    ~335–14 kya

    At the chapter's start our species shared the region: sapiens were at Omo Kibish in Ethiopia at least ~233,000 years ago, while small-brained Homo naledi lived in South Africa's Rising Star cave system ~335,000–236,000 years ago. In Kenya a child, Mtoto, was buried at Panga ya Saidi ~78,000 years ago; in the south people engraved ochre and pierced beads at Blombos, and painted animals on stone slabs in Namibia's Apollo 11 Cave ~30,000 years ago. Foragers lived at 3,469 m in Ethiopia's Bale Mountains and mined ochre at Lion Cavern in Eswatini by ~43,000 years ago. Madagascar had no people yet.

    Europe & the Mediterranean

    ~176–14 kya

    Neanderthals held Europe for most of the chapter: they built the rings deep in Bruniquel Cave ~176,000 years ago, and at Le Rozel in Normandy ~80,000 years ago a group made up mostly of children and adolescents left 257 footprints in the dunes. Sapiens arrived by ~47,000–45,000 years ago (Bacho Kiro, Ranis), and Neanderthals are gone from the record by ~40,000. At Dolní Věstonice in Moravia, Gravettian people fired clay figurines 31,000–27,000 years ago, the oldest ceramics known. The ice pushed people into southern refuges, where Lascaux was painted ~17,000 years ago; on the cold plains of Ukraine, people at Mezhyrich built houses of mammoth bone ~18,000 years ago.

    Central Asia & the Steppe

    ~90–24 kya

    The Altai is where kinds of human met. Denisovans and Neanderthals both used Denisova Cave, where "Denny", a girl with a Neanderthal mother and a Denisovan father, lived ~90,000 years ago; a small Neanderthal community at Chagyrskaya (~59,000–51,000 years ago) included a father and his daughter. Sapiens came in by ~45,000 years ago: a man at Ust'-Ishim on the Irtysh left one of the oldest modern-human genomes, and blade-making industries appear in the Altai and in northern Mongolia (46,000–45,000). At Mal'ta near Lake Baikal a boy buried ~24,000 years ago belonged to a population that later supplied part of the ancestry of Native Americans.

    South Asia

    ~96–14 kya

    Middle Palaeolithic toolmakers, of a kind no fossil yet names, came and went with the monsoon: at Katoati, on the Thar Desert's north-eastern edge, they camped in wet phases between ~96,000 and 60,000 years ago. At Jwalapuram, and at Dhaba in the Son Valley, tools lie below and above the ash of the Toba eruption ~74,000 years ago, so people lived through it; microliths appear at Dhaba ~48,000 years ago. In Sri Lanka's rainforest, hunters at Fa-Hien Lena shot bone-tipped arrows ~48,000 years ago, and those at Batadomba-lena hunted monkeys in the canopy.

    East Asia

    ~160–16 kya

    Denisovans were here first: a jaw from Xiahe on the Tibetan Plateau is at least ~160,000 years old, and the Harbin skull, at least ~146,000, was shown to be Denisovan in 2025. Modern humans had arrived by ~45,000 years ago, when hunters at Shiyu in Shanxi took horses with hafted points and carried obsidian from sources 800–1,000 km away; a man at Tianyuan near Beijing (~40 kya) left one of the region's oldest genomes. People crossed the Kuroshio current to the Ryukyu islands ~35,000–30,000 years ago, and foragers made the world's first pottery at Xianrendong (~20 kya) and in Jōmon Japan (~16 kya).

    Southeast Asia

    ~164–42 kya

    The most crowded corner of the human family. Homo erectus survived at Ngandong on Java until ~117,000–108,000 years ago; small-bodied Homo floresiensis on Flores and Homo luzonensis on Luzon are known until about 50,000 years ago; a Denisovan molar from Tam Ngu Hao 2 in Laos is 164,000–131,000 years old. Sapiens were at Tam Pà Ling in Laos by 86,000–68,000 years ago, painted a pig and three human-like figures at Leang Karampuang on Sulawesi at least 51,200 years ago, and by ~42,000 years ago were catching tuna and other open-sea fish off Timor, at Jerimalai.

    Australia

    ~65 kya (contested) – 14 kya

    People reached Sahul by ~65,000 years ago on Madjedbebe's contested dates, or nearer 50,000 on most genetic estimates, and spread across it: into the arid interior at Warratyi by ~49,000 years ago, and to the Willandra Lakes, where Mungo Lady was cremated and Mungo Man laid in ochre ~40,000 years ago. With Bass Strait dry land, people walked into Tasmania at least ~35,000 years ago; at the glacial maximum, wallaby hunters at Kutikina Cave on the Franklin River were the southernmost people on Earth. In the Kimberley a kangaroo was painted 17,500–17,100 years ago, the oldest Australian rock painting yet directly dated.

    Oceania & the Pacific

    ~49–32 kya · Near Oceania only

    Only Near Oceania — New Guinea, the Bismarck Archipelago and the Solomons — had people. By 49,000–44,000 years ago they were living 2,000 m up in New Guinea's Ivane Valley, eating pandanus nuts and yams, with stone tools that probably cleared forest patches for useful plants. Some 32,000 years ago they had crossed at least 60 km of open sea to Buka in the Solomons, and were eating taro there. Remote Oceania — Vanuatu, Fiji, Polynesia and most of Micronesia — stayed empty until voyagers reached the Marianas, c. 1500 BCE on the excavators' dating, and Lapita crews passed the Solomons c. 1100–1000 BCE.

    The Arctic

    ~45–20 kya · the Siberian side

    People reached the Arctic far earlier than was once thought: a mammoth carcass at Sopochnaya Karga, 72° N on the Yenisei Gulf, bears wounds from hunters' weapons ~45,000 years ago, and people camped at Yana, 71° N, ~32,000 years ago with eyed needles and ivory ornaments. At the glacial maximum Beringia joined Siberia to Alaska as a cold, dry grassland up to 1,000 km wide. The North American Arctic, Greenland and Fennoscandia — later home to the Dorset, the Thule Inuit and the Sámi — lay under or beside the ice sheets, with no one living there yet.

    North America

    ~23–14.5 kya

    Laurentide and Cordilleran ice covered nearly all of Canada. South of it, human footprints at White Sands in New Mexico date to ~23,000–21,000 years ago — doubted by some, but re-dated from seeds, pollen, luminescence and lake mud. Stemmed points at Cooper's Ferry in Idaho date to ~16,600–15,300 years ago, before the inland corridor opened, and at Page-Ladson in Florida people butchered a mastodon ~14,500 years ago. Clovis, long taken for the first Americans, begins only ~13,000 years ago, after this chapter ends.

    Mesoamerica

    ~26.5 kya (contested) – 12 kya

    The evidence here is thin and argued over. Chiquihuite Cave in Zacatecas, 2,740 m up, yielded some 1,900 stone pieces in layers dated ~26,500 years old; critics read them as rock broken by nature, and there are no hearths and no human DNA. The securest evidence is a person, just past this chapter's edge: Naia, a girl of 15 to 17 who fell into the then-dry pit of Hoyo Negro in Yucatán 13,000–12,000 years ago, just after this chapter ends, among the bones of extinct animals. The same now-flooded caves hold ochre mines worked from ~12,000 years ago.

    The Caribbean

    No people yet

    At the glacial low the Bahama banks stood above the sea as broad limestone islands and Cuba was larger, but open water still divided the islands from the mainland. They belonged to animals: ground sloths such as Cuba's Megalocnus, the size of a black bear; a nearly flightless owl more than a metre tall; and monkeys on Jamaica, Cuba and Hispaniola. People reach Trinidad, then still joined or nearly joined to South America, about 8,000–7,000 years ago, and the islands beyond only about 6,000 years ago — and Megalocnus survived long enough to meet them.

    South America

    ~15–14 kya (older claims contested)

    Monte Verde in southern Chile — a creek-side camp where seaweed was carried in from a distant coast — has anchored the continent's settlement at ~14,500 years ago since the 1990s; a 2026 re-analysis arguing it is far younger is itself disputed. Foragers visited Huaca Prieta on Peru's desert coast from ~15,000 years ago, taking food from shore, estuary and mountains with simple pebble tools and no fishhooks. Older claims, such as plates of giant ground-sloth bone worked into ornaments at Santa Elina in central Brazil around the glacial maximum, remain contested.

    Ordinary lives

    Four lives read from a grave, a pyre, a hut floor and a flooded cave

    No one in this chapter left a name, a letter or a face. These four are known from what was done with their bodies and their homes. Nothing in the evidence shows anyone owning another person's labour; that arrives with surplus, chapters later.

    Panga ya Saidi, coastal Kenya · ~78,000 years ago

    Mtoto, a child of about three

    A child of two and a half to three was laid in a pit dug for the purpose, curled with the knees drawn up, and covered with earth. The position of the head suggests it rested on something that has since rotted away — perhaps a pillow, its excavators suggest. The team named the child Mtoto, Swahili for "child". The child was Homo sapiens, though its teeth keep a few older features, and this is the oldest known burial in Africa: someone took trouble over a small death.

    How we know Excavation and micro-analysis of the pit fill, which show it was dug and backfilled; bone histology showing the body decayed where it lay; luminescence dating of the layer to 78,300 ± 4,100 years (Martinón-Torres et al., Nature 2021).

    Ohalo II, Sea of Galilee · ~23,000 years ago

    The household of Brush Hut 1

    At the height of the ice, people camped on the lake shore in six oval huts of branches and brushwood, with hearths outside. Brush Hut 1 was rebuilt on three successive floors. Its sleepers lay near the walls on bundles of grass stems; someone bedded a flat grinding stone into the floor on sand and cobbles and ground wild barley, wheat and oats on it. Flint blades from the camp carry the gloss of cutting still-green wild cereal stalks — the oldest known harvesting knives. The hut floors held more than 90,000 seeds and 20,000 bones, mostly of small, quickly taken animals. Then the camp burned, the lake rose over it, and silt sealed it.

    How we know Charred and waterlogged remains, excavated when droughts lowered the lake in 1989 and 1999; starch grains on the grinding stone; microscopic wear on the blades; radiocarbon dates of ~23,500–22,500 years ago (bedding: Nadel et al., PNAS 2004).

    Lake Mungo, Willandra Lakes, New South Wales · ~40,000 years ago

    Mungo Lady

    She lived beside a chain of lakes that, in her time, swung between full and dry. When she died, her people burned her body, broke the bones, burned them again and buried them by the shore — one of the oldest cremations known anywhere. Mungo Man was laid in his grave sprinkled with red ochre at about the same time. The geologist Jim Bowler found her bones eroding from the dunes in 1968. In 1992 they were returned unconditionally to the Paakantji, Muthi Muthi and Ngiyampaa peoples, the traditional owners of the Willandra.

    How we know Burnt fragments reassembled by Alan Thorne; 25 luminescence ages on the enclosing sands put both burials at 40,000 ± 2,000 years, replacing radiocarbon dates of 20,000–26,000 (Bowler et al., Nature 2003).

    Hoyo Negro, Yucatán, Mexico · 13,000–12,000 years ago

    Naia, a girl of 15 to 17

    At the very end of the ice age — just past this chapter's edge — she went down into a cave system that was then dry and fell into a deep pit, where she lay among the bones of other animals that fell in, many of them now extinct. The rising sea later flooded the caves, and cave divers found her near-complete skeleton in 2007, some 40 metres under water. Her teeth tell how she ate: many cavities and a gum disease aggressive enough to threaten every tooth, but very little wear — a soft diet, at least seasonally rich in carbohydrates, unlike the gritty food that ground down most foragers' teeth. Her small, set-back lower jaw crowded her teeth.

    How we know A skeleton recovered from a flooded cave, dated in 2014 to 13,000–12,000 years ago; tooth-by-tooth studies of decay and wear (2019) and of her jaw (2022). A 2014 claim that her mitochondrial DNA links her to living Native Americans was challenged in 2015 as possibly modern contamination.

    Seen whole

    Seven ways to see the long dawn

    How we know

    • Optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) measures how long a quartz grain has been buried in the dark. It is the method behind Madjedbebe's ~65,000 years, and its assumptions about whether artefacts have shifted downward in sandy sediment are exactly what the site's critics attack.
    • Radiocarbon stops working past ~50,000 years — after about eight half-lives there is too little 14C left to measure, and a trace of modern contamination can make a 60,000-year sample look 40,000 years old. This ceiling is why the oldest Australian dates cannot be cross-checked by the standard method.
    • Uranium-series on cave calcite dates the flowstone that grew over a painting, giving a minimum age for the art beneath — that is how the Sulawesi pig scene reached at least 51,200 years (by laser-ablation uranium-series, which maps the calcite layer by layer) and the red disc at El Castillo ~40,800.
    • Ancient DNA from bone and tooth roots gives whole genomes: Denny's parents, the ~45,000-year Ust'-Ishim man who calibrated the Neanderthal admixture date, the MA-1 boy at Lake Baikal.
    • Sedimentary aDNA recovers a population from cave dirt with no fossil at all — Denisovans and Neanderthals identified from soil columns at Denisova and Baishiya.
    • Palaeoproteomics reads collagen where DNA has perished; it is how the Xiahe jawbone from the Tibetan Plateau was assigned to Denisovans.
    • Strontium isotopes carry the geology of where an animal fed into its shell or bone. A bead whose strontium ratio does not match the rocks around the shelter it was found in was made elsewhere — how the Lesotho eggshell beads were shown to have travelled several hundred kilometres. The method gives a source region and a minimum distance, never the route or how many hands a bead passed through.
    • Radiocarbon with Bayesian modelling combines many dates with the known order of the layers to narrow an event: that is how Cooper's Ferry's first occupation is bracketed at 16,560–15,280 calibrated years ago. The model is only as good as its stratigraphy; charcoal washed down from a higher layer skews it.
    • Kinship from ancient DNA identifies parents, children and cousins directly, and comparing father-to-son Y chromosomes with mother-to-child mitochondria shows which sex moved between groups — the Chagyrskaya result. That last step is a model fit, the best explanation among those tested, not an observation.
    • Bone as a record of violence and disease: microscopic study of each lesion separates healed from unhealed wounds and weapon damage from trampling or excavation (Jebel Sahaba, the Sopochnaya Karga mammoth), and tooth-by-tooth counts give a caries rate (Taforalt). Bone records only what reached bone; most soft-tissue wounds and nearly all infections leave nothing.
    • Environmental proxies and residues — plant waxes, pollen and phytoliths in the sediment around the tools at Bété I; starch grains and crystals on the tools at Kilu — say what grew nearby and what was processed. Both can be contaminated, and starch is usually identifiable only to a genus.
    • The gap nothing fills: the coasts people actually lived on lie under 120 m of water, so the coastal-route evidence is largely unrecoverable — a permanent hole in the record, not a temporary one. Methods in full at How We Know; two of this page's live disagreements — the Cognitive Revolution and when the Americas were peopled — are argued out in its register of contested claims, and the rest (Madjedbebe, Chiquihuite, Neanderthal art, the Neanderthal ending) under Competing explanations above.

    In the hand

    Objects of the awakening mind

    Engraved ochre

    ~75 kya · Blombos

    A block of red ochre scored with a deliberate cross-hatch — the oldest secure abstract design made by our species, and proof someone meant something by it.

    Shell-bead necklace

    ~75 kya · South Africa

    Sea-snail shells pierced and strung — personal ornament, and with it the first sign of identity worn on the body.

    Ground-edge hatchet

    ~65 kya · Madjedbebe

    Perhaps the oldest ground-edge axe known anywhere, if Madjedbebe's contested date holds (the oldest securely dated, from Carpenter's Gap in the Kimberley, is ~49,000–44,000 years old) — its cutting edge abraded smooth, not merely flaked, tens of millennia before polished stone appears elsewhere.

    The Sulawesi pig

    ~51 kya · Indonesia

    A red warty pig painted on a cave wall — the oldest figurative image we have ever found, older than any art in Europe.

    Waisted axe

    ~49–44 kya · Ivane Valley, New Guinea

    A heavy stone blade notched at its sides so it could be hafted — used, its finders argue, to open patches of highland forest for useful plants.

    Bone arrowhead

    ~48 kya · Fa-Hien Lena, Sri Lanka

    A slender bone point with fractures from being shot — among the earliest evidence of the bow outside Africa, from a rainforest cave where the prey lived in the trees.

    Bone flute

    ~40 kya · Hohle Fels

    A flute cut from a vulture's wing bone — the earliest music we can still hold, from the caves of the Swabian Jura.

    Eyed bone needle

    ~32 kya · Yana, 71° N

    A needle with a drilled eye, from a camp deep inside the Arctic Circle. Fire alone will not keep you alive at 71° north; fitted, sewn clothing will.

    Shell fishhook

    ~23 kya · Sakitari Cave, Okinawa

    Ground from sea-snail shell on a small island at the height of the ice — the oldest fishhook yet found, made by people who lived off the sea rather than merely crossing it.

    Ishango bone

    ~20 kya? · DR Congo

    A 10 cm bone from the Nile's Semliki headwaters, a quartz chip set in one end and notches cut in three columns. Tally, lunar count or arithmetic? Readings differ, and even its date is uncertain.

    Incipient Jōmon cooking pot

    ~16 kya · Jōmon Japan

    Fired clay for boiling fish, shellfish and nuts, made by foragers who never farmed — the invention arrives thousands of years ahead of agriculture, not behind it.

    Dog burial

    ~14.2 kya · Oberkassel

    A wolf-turned-dog laid to rest beside people — the first domesticated animal, and the first friend of another species.
