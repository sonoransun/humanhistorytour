The Master Timeline

Seven million years, one spine. This page is the skeleton the whole tour hangs on — a single ordered thread from the first flaked stone to the first large language model, a second thread of ice ages, droughts and dust veils running underneath it, a clock that compresses the lineage into one day, and the handful of transitions that snap the story into acts. Every claim below carries a real table, because deep time is almost impossible to feel without one.

How to read deep time

The trouble with human history is that almost none of it happened when you think it did. The hominin lineage is roughly 7 million years long. Everything a visitor recognizes as "history" — writing, cities, empires, science, machines — fits into the last ~5,000 years. Draw that honestly and the past vanishes. Imagine a fair, linear scroll: 7 million years stretched across a 10,000-pixel page, so one pixel is 700 years. On that scroll, all of recorded history is about 7 pixels — the thickness of a coin at the very bottom. The Industrial Revolution is a third of one pixel. The Internet is invisible.

Why the scale on this page is compressed

Because a linear axis is useless here, the tour compresses time — with a logarithmic axis, a 24-hour clock, and a variable-speed scroll that slows as it nears the present. That fix comes with a warning label. On a compressed or logarithmic scale, equal horizontal steps are not equal spans of years: they are equal multiples of time. A linear ruler gives every decade the same width; a log ruler gives every order of magnitude the same width — so the jump from 3 million to 300,000 years ago (a factor of 10) occupies the same distance as the jump from 300 to 30 years ago. That is exactly what lets the Paleolithic and the last century share the screen — but it means you must never read horizontal distance as elapsed time. The clock below undoes the distortion by mapping the real seconds of a single day onto real proportions of the past.

This page is nothing but dates, and a date is only as good as the method behind it — so the spine and climate tables below name that method row by row. Every figure is the output of a method — stratigraphy, radiocarbon and the IntCal calibration curve, argon-argon, uranium-series, luminescence, tree rings, ice-core layers, ancient DNA — each with its own reach and its own error bars. Those methods, and why a "±100 years" on a Bronze Age date and a "±100,000 years" on a Pleistocene one are the same kind of honesty, are set out in How We Know · dating the past.

The master spine

Here is the whole tour on one thread: nine acts, four headline beats each, from the oldest known flaked stone to today's neural networks. Read the acts in order, left to right and then down, and it is the order in which every chapter unfolds. The spine is deliberately anti-Eurocentric: every act after the first draws its beats from at least three continents — Africa, the Americas, Australia, Oceania and the Arctic carry their own headline events, not footnotes to a European story — and below it the same nine acts are laid out across all sixteen of the tour's regions, so that a region's silence is visible rather than hidden.

The spine in sixteen places

A spine with four beats per act still has to leave most of the world out of each act. The grid below does not: it runs the same nine acts across the sixteen regions every chapter of the tour uses, one short entry per cell. Read across a row to follow one region through seven million years; read down a column to see the whole inhabited world inside a single act. The cells that say "no people yet" are the honest ones — nobody reached Australia before ~65–50 kya, the Americas before ~23 kya at the earliest, or the Caribbean islands before ~6,000 years ago — and every other cell is drawn from that chapter's own "Around the world" panel, where the fuller story and its evidence sit.

The spine hides its own distortion: acts I–II above cover ~99.8% of the elapsed time, acts VII–IX under six centuries. See the same acceleration in the Great Acceleration data story and trace the inventions that unlocked each beat in the human tech tree.

The climate spine

The spine above reads as a list of achievements: someone invents farming, someone founds a city, someone builds an empire. Run a second thread underneath it — ice-core layers, ocean sediment, cave formations, pollen, tree rings — and most of those achievements turn out to sit on top of a shove. The climate did not merely form a backdrop to human history. It repeatedly set the terms: when the wild cereals grew thin, when the land bridges opened, when the Sahara filled with lakes and then emptied, when the harvest failed for a decade in a row across an entire hemisphere.

This is the largest causal thread the tour otherwise leaves implicit, so it is laid out here in full. Two rules travel with it. First, climate is a forcing, not a script: identical droughts produced collapse in one polity and irrigation works in its neighbour, and the difference was politics, storage, and slack. Second, synchronicity is not causation — several of the tidiest stories below (Toba, 4.2 ka, the 536-to-plague chain) are actively contested, and the table marks each one honestly rather than smoothing them into the narrative. Every row also names the evidence it rests on, because a climate event is only as solid as the archive that records it.

The Pleistocene rhythm

Before there is any human story there is a metronome. Earth's orbit is not fixed: its ellipse stretches and relaxes over ~100,000 years, its tilt nods between 22.1° and 24.5° every ~41,000, and its axis wobbles through a ~23,000-year precession. None of this changes the total sunlight much; it changes where and when that sunlight lands, and in particular whether northern ice survives the summer. Milutin Milanković worked the arithmetic out by hand in the 1920s and 1930s. It was ignored for forty years, then vindicated in 1976 when deep-sea cores turned out to carry exactly those three periods.

The clearest evidence is chemical. Foraminifera on the seafloor build shells from seawater whose oxygen-isotope ratio depends on how much water is locked up in ice, so a core is a continuous ice-volume record — chopped into numbered Marine Isotope Stages, odd for warm and even for cold. Around 1.2–0.7 Mya the beat changed: the 41,000-year rhythm gave way to a ~100,000-year one, with much colder, longer glacials, and no matching change in the orbital forcing to explain it. That Mid-Pleistocene Transition is the world Homo erectus and its descendants had to survive — not a stable savanna, but a landscape that reorganized itself every few tens of millennia. One hypothesis for the flexible, generalist, tool-dependent animal that came out the other side is precisely that instability.

The warm stages matter as much as the cold ones. The last of them before our own, MIS 5e (~130,000–115,000 years ago), was warmer than the pre-industrial world — about as warm as today — with seas some 6–9 metres higher; hippos wallowed where London's Trafalgar Square now stands, and a wet phase turned Arabia's sand seas into grassland and lakes. Through that green door Homo sapiens walked out of Africa at least once — burials at Skhul and Qafzeh, footprints beside a vanished lake in the Nefud — and then, as far as the genes can tell, died out or withdrew. The dispersal that succeeded came much later.

Into that rhythm falls the loudest bang in the human record. Toba, on Sumatra, erupted around 74,000 years ago and blanketed South Asia in ash. For two decades the standard telling made it the near-end of our species — a volcanic winter that reduced humanity to a few thousand individuals. That story is now largely unwound: sediment cores from Lake Malawi show no cooling signature, excavations at Pinnacle Point in South Africa and at Jwalapuram and Dhaba in India show people living straight through the ash layer, and at Shinfa-Metema 1 in the Ethiopian lowlands the same microscopic ash coincides with a drier spell that people met by foraging along seasonal rivers. The genetic signal Toba was invoked to explain is better read as the small size of the population that later left Africa. Keep it as a cautionary case: the most dramatic causal story on this page is the one that did not survive contact with better data. See the register of live debates.

The last glacial was not a steady cold but a lurching one. Six times between ~60,000 and ~16,000 years ago, armadas of icebergs broke from the northern ice sheets, above all North America's Laurentide, and dropped their rock debris across the North Atlantic floor — the Heinrich events, found in seabed cores by Hartmut Heinrich in 1988. The freshwater slowed the ocean's overturning and the effect reached the tropics: at the height of the last of them, ~17,000–16,000 years ago, the African and Asian monsoons failed in one of the worst megadroughts of the last 50,000 years. Whether earlier Heinrich stadials helped empty Neanderthal Iberia is argued; the coincidence in time is real, the mechanism unproven.

The hinge — from the ice maximum to the calm

At the Last Glacial Maximum, ~26,000–19,000 years ago, so much water stood frozen on land that the sea fell ~120 metres. That single number redraws the map the tour walks over: Beringia is not a bridge but a grassland a thousand kilometres wide; Britain is a peninsula and the southern North Sea is Doggerland, a plain of rivers and marsh that people hunted across; Tasmania is part of Australia; Sunda is a subcontinent. The peopling of the Americas happens because of an ice age. (Sahul is the exception worth stating precisely: those crossings were earlier, around the MIS 4 low stand ~70,000 years ago, and even at maximum drawdown the Wallacean straits never closed — Australia was always reached by boat.)

Then the ice let go, and not gently. About 14,700 years ago the Bølling–Allerød warming arrived all at once: air bubbles in Greenland's ice show its summit warming by some 9 °C within a few decades, and Meltwater Pulse 1A raised the world's oceans by about 16 metres in a few centuries. Forest and game moved north, and hunters followed them back into northern Europe. The drowning of the land bridges took longer: the Bering land bridge was not cut until ~11,000 years ago, and Doggerland's last islands lasted until ~7,000 years ago.

Deglaciation was well under way when, ~12,900 years ago, the Northern Hemisphere snapped back into near-glacial cold for about 1,200 years — the Younger Dryas, most plausibly caused by a flood of glacial meltwater freshening the North Atlantic and stalling its overturning circulation. Greenland's ice records a drop of roughly 10 °C, and, at the end, a recovery of similar size within a human lifetime.

The Levant at that moment held the Natufians: people who had stopped moving, built stone-founded hamlets, and lived off harvesting wild cereals they did not plant. The Younger Dryas took that away. At Abu Hureyra on the Euphrates the plant remains record the response — drought-tolerant wild foods, then the weed species that follow deliberate sowing. It is the single most cited climate-to-culture link in prehistory, and it should be held at arm's length as well as admired: the correlation is tight, the mechanism is plausible, and it remains an inference. Farming was invented independently at least ten more times, in places the Younger Dryas did not touch the same way.

The calm window

Everything after this point in the tour happens inside a single anomaly. Agriculture, cities, writing, empires, science and industry all sit within one unusually quiet stretch of climate. The preceding 100,000 years were violent — Greenland's ice records twenty-five Dansgaard–Oeschger events, abrupt warmings of 8–16 °C within decades, followed by slow slides back. The Holocene, beginning ~11,700 years ago, has wandered by roughly ±1 °C for eleven millennia. Chapters III through IX of this tour — everything most people mean by "history" — take place entirely within that anomaly. It is not obvious that a civilization-building species could have got started in the climate that preceded it, and the last row of the table above is about leaving it.

Inside the calm window

Quiet is relative. Within the Holocene, several shifts were large enough to be visible in human records. The African Humid Period is the biggest of them: for some five thousand years the monsoon reached far enough north that the Sahara held grassland, rivers, and lakes — Mega-Chad alone covered roughly 350,000–400,000 km², about the size of the Caspian Sea. People herded cattle where there is now nothing but sand, buried their dead at Gobero in Niger, and aligned standing stones at Nabta Playa. When precession pulled the monsoon back south — desertification set in across the eastern Sahara from around 5300 BCE — that entire population had one reliable option, and the density of Predynastic settlement along the Nile is the result. Egypt is, in part, a refugee state.

The 8.2 ka event (~6200 BCE) is the Holocene's sharpest single jolt: the last glacial lakes of North America emptied into the Atlantic, cooling the north by 1–3 °C for around 160 years and drying the Near East. Levantine and Anatolian villages reorganize in this window — Çatalhöyük's occupants move from the East mound to the West — and, in the same handful of centuries, the Storegga slide drove a tsunami across a Doggerland that the rising sea was already erasing.

Two later events are where honesty matters most. The 4.2 ka event (~2200 BCE) has become shorthand for a global megadrought that felled the Akkadian Empire and Egypt's Old Kingdom at once. The dust spikes and abandoned northern Mesopotamian sites are real; the global synchrony is disputed, the Indus decline is actually centuries later, and both empires had internal reasons to fail. The 3.2 ka drought (~1250–1100 BCE) is on firmer pollen evidence but weaker as an explanation: the Late Bronze Age Collapse took down the Hittites, Ugarit and the Mycenaean palaces through drought and migration and earthquake and the fragility of palace economies that had optimized away their own slack. Complex systems fail from several directions at once — which is the actual lesson, and a more useful one than any single cause.

The last two millennia

The written record now runs alongside the proxies, and the two can be checked against each other. The Roman Climatic Optimum gave the Mediterranean warm, wet, dependable centuries — the baseline beneath the grain fleets, the million-person city, and the frontier expansion. When it ended is now argued: a high-resolution record from marine sediments off southern Italy, published in 2024, shows the stable warm phase breaking down from ~100 CE, and more sharply after ~130 CE, with cold phases at ~160–180 and ~245–275 CE that line up with the Antonine Plague and the Plague of Cyprian, where older syntheses carried the good years into the third century.

Then 536 CE, the year the sun dimmed. Ice cores place a large eruption at 536, another in 540 and a third in 547; tree rings across the Northern Hemisphere record 536–545 as the coldest decade in more than two thousand years, with summers 1.5–2.5 °C down. Procopius, then serving with the army in Africa and Italy, wrote that the sun shone "without brightness" for a year. Famine ran from Ireland to China; Scandinavian farms were abandoned; the Justinianic Plague arrived in 541; Rome and Persia ground each other down through the Late Antique Little Ice Age and were exhausted when the Arab conquests began. It is a magnificent causal chain and it is contested at almost every link — the cooling is certain, the plague's mortality is being revised sharply downward by some historians, and "cold caused the caliphate" is not a claim the evidence carries.

Across the Atlantic the Maya lowlands give the page's first rule its clearest test. A stalagmite from Yok Balum Cave in Belize shows unusually wet centuries from 440 to 660 CE, when population and the number of royal courts grew, and then a drying trend punctuated by droughts; gypsum from Lake Chichancanab in Yucatán records annual rainfall falling by roughly half during the Terminal Classic, ~800–1000 CE, and the stalagmite adds a final, extended drought in 1020–1100. The southern kingdoms fragmented and emptied — Tikal's last dated monument is from 869. But Chichén Itzá, in the drier north, flourished through the ninth- and tenth-century droughts. Same forcing, different societies, different outcomes.

The Medieval Climate Anomaly (~950–1250) is best understood as a rearrangement rather than a warming: a warm North Atlantic, a persistently La Niña-like Pacific, and brutal megadroughts in western North America, recorded in tree rings to the year and in tree stumps that grew on the beds of Sierra Nevada lakes while the water was low. Every one of those regional signatures has a human story attached. Norse farmers reach Iceland, Greenland and briefly Newfoundland. Chaco Canyon builds its great houses and its road network — and is emptied after the tree-ring-dated drought of the 1130s; the Great Drought of 1276–1299 helps clear the Four Corners. Cahokia swells to perhaps ten to twenty thousand people and then drains. And in the Pacific, the wind anomalies of these centuries coincide with the final burst of Polynesian voyaging that reaches Hawai'i, Rapa Nui, and at last Aotearoa around 1250–1300 — the last major landmass on Earth to be settled by people.

The Little Ice Age (~1300–1850) reverses the sign, and it opens with a bang. In 1257 Samalas, on Lombok, produced one of the largest eruptions of the Holocene; its sulfate had sat in Greenland and Antarctic ice as a "mystery eruption" for decades until, in 2013, radiocarbon on trees buried in its pumice and a palm-leaf chronicle, the Babad Lombok, pinned it down. Global cooling over the following centuries was modest — a few tenths of a degree — but it fell on marginal harvests: advancing Alpine glaciers, frost fairs on the Thames, failed grain in Scotland and Scandinavia, and at Angkor decades of drought broken by violent monsoons that its canals could not handle. The forcing was volcanic clustering plus solar minima, deepest during the Maunder Minimum of 1645–1715, when observers recorded almost no sunspots for seventy years. Geoffrey Parker's General Crisis lines that up against the seventeenth century's simultaneous catastrophes: the Ming dynasty falling in 1644 after drought and famine, the Thirty Years' War, the Fronde, the English Civil War, the Celali revolts, Japan's Kan'ei famine, and Russia's Time of Troubles famine in the cold summers after the 1600 Huaynaputina eruption. Tambora's 1815 eruption, the most powerful in recorded history, produced the "year without a summer" in 1816 — famine in Yunnan, what the historian John D. Post called the last great subsistence crisis in the Western world, and, at a rain-bound Lake Geneva, Frankenstein.

The inversion

And then the arrow turns around. Atmospheric CO₂ had sat between roughly 180 and 300 ppm for eight hundred thousand years; since ~1850 it has gone from ~280 to about 427 ppm (Mauna Loa's 2025 annual mean), and 2024 was the warmest year ever measured, some 1.55 °C above the 1850–1900 average. The rate is the part that matters: the deglacial rise took about six thousand years (~17,000–11,000 years ago) to add some seventy-five to eighty parts per million, a pace the present is exceeding by roughly two orders of magnitude. For the entire span of this page, climate has been the thing that happened to people. It is now, measurably, a thing people do — the one genuinely new item on the seven-million-year list. The quantities are charted in The Great Acceleration · carbon.

The physical evidence behind every row above — ice cores, speleothems, tree rings, pollen and isotopes — is explained in How We Know · reading the landscape, with the disputed cases in the register of live debates. The human consequences run through Chapter III · Neolithic (the Younger Dryas and the first farmers) and Chapter VI · Medieval (536 CE and the Medieval Climate Anomaly); the carbon curve that ends the story is in The Great Acceleration.

The 24-hour clock of history

The cleanest way to feel the compression is to squeeze the entire ~7-million-year lineage into a single day. At that scale, one second is about 81 years and one minute a bit under 5,000 years. Midnight tonight is now. Wind the clock back to midnight last night and you are at the dawn of the human line. The day is not empty — upright apes are walking by mid-morning and flaking stone soon after noon — but it belongs to other kinds of human: our own species does not appear until about 11 pm (10:58 on the exact count), and everything we call history is crammed into the last minute before the clock strikes twelve again.

Everything else, all 23 hours and 59 minutes, is deep prehistory. Chapter II alone — 286,000 years, from the first Homo sapiens to the end of the Ice Age — fills 58 minutes 50 seconds of the final hour; Chapters III to IX together, every farm and city and machine, share the last 2 minutes 53 seconds. We are a species that spends almost its entire existence before the part it remembers.

The great transitions

Between the acts sit the hinges — the phase-changes where population, energy, social scale, and lifespan all reset at once. Four of them are so large they organize the whole tour: the Cognitive, Agricultural, Industrial, and Information revolutions — the first of them a teaching label for a change that accumulated over tens of millennia, not an event. Nested inside those four are the smaller act-breaks — the peopling of new continents, the invention of cities and writing, iron, printing, electricity, the web. One scheme among several

Four revolutions is a teaching device, not a finding. It is one way of cutting the past, chosen here because it is legible at a glance and because the four breaks it names really do show up in the population, energy and information curves. Historians cut it differently, and the alternatives are not wrong:

  • V. Gordon Childe (1936) proposed just two — the Neolithic Revolution and the Urban Revolution — and put the whole weight of the story on food surplus and the city.
  • Karl Jaspers (1949) centred the Axial Age, ~800–200 BCE, when Greece, Israel, India, Persia and China independently produced their enduring philosophical and religious frameworks. On that scheme the decisive break is intellectual, not material, and it happens once.
  • World-systems periodisation (Wallerstein; Abu-Lughod's thirteenth-century system) drops revolutions altogether and dates change by the reorganization of trade networks and by which region sits at the core — which is why it puts a major boundary at ~1500 and a minor one at 1250, where the four-revolution scheme sees nothing.
  • Braudel's longue durée distrusts events entirely, arguing that geography and climate move at one speed, social structures at another, and politics at a third — three clocks, no acts.
  • Big History (Christian) counts eight thresholds and begins at the Big Bang, so human agriculture is threshold seven of eight and the industrial "modern revolution" is threshold eight — humans occupy only the last three thresholds.

Two cautions about the shape used here. Revolutions are retrofitted labels: nobody in 9000 BCE knew they were in one, and the Agricultural "Revolution" took some four thousand years in its fastest region — twice the span from Augustus to now. And each named break was uneven: the Industrial Revolution is a British and then North Atlantic event for a century before it is a global one, and most of the world experienced it first as something done to them. Use the four acts as a scaffold, then knock it away.

These are the same dates at which every curve in the Great Acceleration bends upward, and the same nodes where the tech tree branches. Four revolutions, one accelerating story.
