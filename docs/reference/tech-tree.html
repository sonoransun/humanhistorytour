The Human Tech Tree

Civilization is not a pile of gadgets — it is a dependency graph. Fire unlocks cooking; agriculture unlocks cities and writing; the transistor unlocks the computer, the internet, and AI. Here is the whole graph: the great unlocks in sequence, the branches cut far from the standard route — in China and Korea, the Americas, the Pacific, the Arctic, Australia and Africa — and the Islamic bridge that carried several of them west, then the ladder of social scale that ratcheted upward beside them, and the branching bush of hominins who cut the first key — each with the data behind it.

From Oldowan flakes to AI

You cannot smelt iron without first taming fire, cannot print a book without writing and paper, cannot train a neural network without the transistor. Read that way, the human story is a strategy-game tech tree that took 3.3 million years to unlock: each invention rests on a foundation of earlier ones, and each one, once cut, becomes the key to the next. Below is that graph — first the roughly a dozen great unlocks in sequence, then seven closer views of how the branches actually forked, including the ones the standard tree leaves off.

Two cautions travel with any tech tree. First, it is not one line: the biggest keys were cut more than once, independently, on different continents — farming was invented at least eleven times, writing at least three, metallurgy in both the Old World and the Andes, and iron smelting perhaps a second time in Africa. Second, branches die. Roman concrete, Greek fire, and Han-dynasty seismographs were lost for centuries; the geared computing of the Antikythera mechanism was not matched in complexity until Europe's astronomical clocks of the 1300s. Progress is a bush that is sometimes pruned, not an escalator.

Sub-tree · the Stone Age toolkit

For 99% of the tree's history there was only stone, wood, bone, and fire. Yet even here the dependencies are real: sharp flakes made butchery possible, butchery and cooking fed a bigger brain, a bigger brain made symbolic language possible, and language made everything after it possible. The oldest knapped stone at Lomekwi (Kenya) predates our own genus by half a million years — technology is older than humanity. And the branch is not only African and European: the oldest securely dated ground-edge axe known anywhere was made in Australia's Kimberley some 49,000–44,000 years ago, tens of millennia before polished stone became a hallmark of the Neolithic elsewhere.

Sub-tree · Agrarian & Classical

Domestication is the hinge of the whole tree. A reliable food surplus let some people stop farming — and those specialists gave us the potter's wheel (and then the cart wheel), metallurgy, cities, and, to track who owed what to whom, writing. Pottery itself is older than the hinge: foragers in East Asia were firing cooking pots thousands of years before anyone there farmed. Every branch here was cut more than once: farming arose independently in the Fertile Crescent, China, New Guinea, West Africa, Mesoamerica, the Andes, Amazonia and eastern North America; writing was invented from scratch in Sumer, in China, and again in Mesoamerica. And the Inca ran an empire of ~12 million with neither the wheel for transport nor an alphabet — proof the tree has more than one path up.

Branches the standard tree omits

The tree above is drawn along the route most tech trees take — Fertile Crescent to Greece to Rome to Europe to the modern West. That route is real, but it is one path through the graph, and following it alone drops branches in two different ways. Some it dates wrongly: China had cast iron by ~500 BCE and Europe not until the 12th–13th century (Lapphyttan, Sweden, c. 1150–1350); Cai Lun's court presented paper in 105 CE, and paper was not made in Europe until the 11th–12th century, at Xàtiva in al-Andalus; the Diamond Sutra was printed and dated in 868 CE, almost six centuries before Gutenberg; Su Song's water-driven clock tower at Kaifeng was running an escapement and an endless chain drive in 1092, two centuries before any mechanical clock in Europe.

Other branches are dropped for a deeper reason: they do not lead to industry, so a tree that reads backwards from the factory cannot see them. Polynesian wayfinding crossed a third of the planet with no instrument carried aboard. The Inca ran a twelve-million-person empire on knotted cord. Gunditjmara engineers in south-west Victoria cut stone channels for eels at least 6,600 years ago (~4600 BCE), some 1,600 years before the first phase of Stonehenge. None of these is a stepping-stone to the steam engine, and every one of them is a solved problem of the first rank. In the Americas every key was cut with no Old World contact at all, which makes them the cleanest test of whether the tree is one line. It is not. Andean smiths worked gold by ~2000 BCE and later alloyed and gilded copper for colour and rank rather than for an edge; Gulf Coast Mesoamericans were processing latex into bouncing rubber more than three thousand years before Charles Goodyear vulcanized it; a positional count with a place for nothing was in use by 36 BCE; the Mexica fed an island capital from gardens built in a lake; and Amazonians made soils that are still fertile and towns that lidar has only just found.

The third set of branches solved problems the Eurasian route never faced: crossing thousands of kilometres of open ocean to islands no one could see, living year-round on sea ice, and managing a whole continent with fire. Africa's iron belongs here too, and it is the hardest case to judge fairly. For an independent invention: across most of sub-Saharan Africa smelting arrives with no copper or bronze age before it, in furnaces unlike Mediterranean ones, and the earliest iron dates from Meroë and Egypt are no older than those from Nigeria or the Great Lakes. Against, or not yet: claims of iron by ~2000 BCE at Lejja in Nigeria and Oboui in the Central African Republic rest on radiocarbon samples that may not belong with the smelting debris or may be older wood than the fire they fed, and the calibration curve is nearly flat between about 800 and 400 BCE — exactly the centuries in dispute. The archaeologist Manfred Eggert (2014) judged the evidence still inconclusive but, on balance, pointing to invention in Africa.

Each of these traditions is followed in place — with its neighbors and its chronology — in Parallel Civilizations: see China, the Andes, Oceania, Australia & the Arctic, and Africa.

The bridge · the Islamic middle

The standard tree hides a middle as well as its margins. Its "Scientific Revolution ~1543" node seems to spring from Renaissance Europe, but much of what it used arrived through the Islamic world, which did not merely carry older knowledge but rebuilt it. Paper was in use at Samarkand before 751 and in Baghdad's mills by the 790s. Al-Khwārizmī's arithmetic brought Indian place-value numerals west, and his name became the Latin word "algorism". Astrolabe makers from Baghdad to Aleppo to Toledo — among them al-ʻIjliyyah, who made instruments for the court of Sayf al-Dawla — turned a Greek idea into the precision instrument of the age, used to tell time, set prayer times and find the direction of Mecca. Ibn al-Haytham's Book of Optics, in Latin, fed the optics of Witelo, which Kepler set out to supplement in 1604. And the Tusi couple, devised in 1247, reappears in Copernicus's De revolutionibus — by a route historians have not yet traced.

Sub-tree · Industrial & Information

The last two branches are the shortest in time and the steepest in consequence. The printing press — woodblock in Tang China by ~700 CE, movable metal type in Korea (the Jikji, 1377) before Gutenberg's around 1450 — made ideas copyable, which made the Scientific Revolution possible, which made the steam engine possible. Steam and then electricity industrialized the planet; electricity's strangest child, the transistor (1947), collapsed into the integrated circuit, the microprocessor, the internet, and finally the machine-learning systems of the 2010s. One root of the computer is not electrical at all: Jacquard's loom of 1804 read its weaving pattern from punched cards, and those cards passed to Babbage's Analytical Engine design and to Hollerith's census tabulator of 1890. From the first controlled fire to the first large language model is about a million years; from the transistor to it, seventy-five.

Keys cut more than once

The claim that the biggest keys were cut more than once is easy to assert and easy to doubt — how would anyone know a second inventor did not simply hear about the first? The answer is that independence leaves physical fingerprints: different wild ancestors, different sign inventories, different alloys, different notations, and, in the American cases, an ocean. Why the tree forked where it did

A tree cut on every inhabited continent still grew unevenly, and why it did is one of the oldest arguments in history. No answer is settled, and each of the leading ones explains a different part of the pattern. Geography: Jared Diamond (Guns, Germs, and Steel, 1997) argued that Eurasia's long east–west axis let crops, animals and techniques spread along bands of similar climate, while the Americas and Africa run north–south across climate zones that slowed them; critics call the argument too deterministic to explain later reversals. The Needham question: the historian of Chinese science Joseph Needham asked why a China that led on paper, print, powder, the compass and the escapement did not go on to a scientific and industrial revolution of its own, and some historians now reject the premise that Europe's path was the normal one.

Coal, colonies and wages: Kenneth Pomeranz (2000) argued that the Yangzi delta matched England in living standards as late as 1750, and that coal close to hand plus the land and coerced labour of the Americas made the difference; Robert Allen (2009) argued that Britain's high wages and cheap coal made labour-saving machines pay there first. It is no accident that the first commercially successful steam engine, Newcomen's of 1712, was built to pump water out of a coal mine. Knowledge: Joel Mokyr (2016) argued that a European "culture of growth", a competitive market for ideas, mattered more than prices or resources.

Whatever the answer, one caution follows for reading this page. The branches that never led to industry, such as khipu, wayfinding and the Budj Bim channels, were answers to other questions, not failed attempts at this one. A tree drawn backwards from the factory will always make the factory look inevitable. The divergence debate is set out side by side in How We Know · live debates and Chapter VIII · why it happened.

How we know a branch is real

  • Slag, not objects. Native copper can be hammered into shape without a furnace, so only smelting slag proves smelting. The Belovode date rests on analysed slag (Radivojević et al. 2010).
  • Use-wear and residue. Small size, impact fractures and the placement of glue residues are what mark the Sibudu points as probable arrowheads rather than spear tips.
  • Radiocarbon. Radiocarbon dates from one of the Lake Condah trap systems at Budj Bim give the ~6,600-year age (McNiven et al. 2012).
  • Genomes. Crop and animal DNA places each domestication in its own region. The genome of a Zhokhov dog ties the first sled dogs to today's.
  • Lidar. Airborne laser scanning found the Upano and Casarabe towns under forest and savanna (Rostain et al. 2024; Prümers et al. 2022).
  • Texts that carry their own dates. The Diamond Sutra is dated 868 in its colophon; the Book of the Later Han records Cai Lun and Du Shi; Shen Kuo's Dream Pool Essays (1088) describe both the magnetized needle and Bi Sheng's type.
  • Experiment. In 1976 Hōkūleʻa sailed from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti without instruments. The voyage answered Andrew Sharp's claim (1956) that Polynesians could have reached their islands only by accidental drift.

The methods themselves are explained in How We Know · dating, reading bodies and molecules and seeing from above and below.

The ladder of social scale

Every rung of the tech tree has a twin on a second ladder: the number of people who can act as a single unit. For most of prehistory that ceiling was the band — a few dozen who all knew one another by face. Each jump upward required a new coordination technology: shared myth to bind a tribe, writing and law to run a city, money and bureaucracy to hold an empire, print and mass media to imagine a nation, telecom and the internet to network eight billion. The rungs are literally nested — each larger scale encloses the smaller ones it is built from.

The branching bush of hominins

Human evolution is popularly drawn as a parade — a stooped ape straightening, step by step, into a modern human. The truth is a bush, not a ladder. For most of the last four million years of its seven-million-year run the human family was plural: at times half a dozen hominin species walked the Earth at once, and every branch but one is a dead end. We are the single surviving twig. This diagram grafts two stories onto that bush — the branching of the hominin family, and the dispersal of our own branch, Homo sapiens, out of Africa to every habitable continent, absorbing a little Neanderthal and Denisovan ancestry on the way.

Draw that bush and a second problem appears at once: no two paleoanthropologists draw it the same way. The diagram below renders one contested model — Homo heidelbergensis as the common ancestor of all three later lineages, Neanderthal, Denisovan and sapiens. Many researchers now treat heidelbergensis as a wastebasket taxon into which awkward mid-Pleistocene skulls were swept, and genetics pushes the sapiens–Neanderthal divergence back to ~600–800 kya, older than most fossils carrying that name. Even the species count is an argument about the same bones: "lumpers" recognize perhaps a dozen hominins, "splitters" closer to thirty. Read every node below as a hypothesis with a fossil attached rather than a settled fact, and the arrows as the current best guess at who begat whom.

One figure that travels with this bush deserves a warning label. Neanderthal cranial capacity runs ~1,400–1,600 cc against our own ~1,350, and H. floresiensis knapped stone and hunted with roughly 420. Cranial capacity is not a ranking. Brain volume scales with body mass and with cold — Neanderthals were shorter, heavier and cold-adapted, and much of that extra volume served vision and body control rather than anything we would call intellect. It predicts nothing about language, planning, or culture. Our own brains have shrunk by perhaps 10% since the late Pleistocene with no loss of capability at all. Read the numbers as anatomy, never as a scoreboard.

The species tables have moved — and grown: The Human Family

  • The australopiths and the side branches — Ardipithecus, Australopithecus, and the robust Paranthropus boisei and P. robustus, the heavy-jawed lineage that shared East and South Africa with early Homo for a million years and left no descendants.
  • Neanderthals — the species and cranial-capacity table that used to sit here, in full: anatomy, Shanidar and Krapina, pigment, burial and toolkit, and the arguments over why they were gone by ~40 kya.
  • Denisovans — a whole population identified from a finger bone and a genome years before anyone could describe a skull, plus Denisova 11, the girl with a Neanderthal mother and a Denisovan father.
  • What the bush teaches — the great-dispersals table in expanded form, the admixture percentages continent by continent, and the case against the parade.
