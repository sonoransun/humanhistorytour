Method & honest limits

Let's be direct about what this is. The Human History Tour is an educational synthesis: a reader-friendly retelling of what standard reference works and public datasets already say about the human past. It was assembled with AI assistance from that widely-taught, non-specialist body of knowledge — the kind you would find in survey textbooks, museum explainers, encyclopedias, and open data portals. It is not original research, it is not peer-reviewed, and it should not be cited as a primary or authoritative source.

What that means in practice: nearly every number here is a best-estimate approximation. Where the scholarship gives a range, this tour shows a range. Where a single tidy figure is quoted, read a quiet "roughly" in front of it. The goal is to get the shape of the human story right — the orders of magnitude, the sequence, the turning points — not to settle any specialist debate.

Honest limits

  • Not authoritative. Treat this as a well-organized starting point, not an endpoint. For anything that matters to you, go to the primary literature.
  • Approximate by design. Dates, populations, and economic figures are rounded and simplified; deep-time figures especially so.
  • Syntheses, not citations. The quantitative claims echo standard datasets (see Data provenance) rather than any single original measurement.
  • A moving target. Prehistory is revised constantly. Some "firsts" below will be outdated by the next field season (see Dating & uncertainty).
  • AI-assisted, human-shaped. Assembling text at this scale invites errors of emphasis and fact; the framing, cross-checks, and where-uncertain ranges are editorial choices, and mistakes are possible. The September 2026 expansion added a double-checked audit of every page (how it was checked); that catches more errors, but it cannot promise that none remain.

Non-defensively: if you find an error, you are almost certainly right to trust the primary source over this page. Start with the further reading list.

Dating & uncertainty

The deeper in time a date sits, the wider its true error bar. A radiocarbon date on a hearth might be good to a century or two; a date on a two-million-year-old tool bed rests on argon-argon or palaeomagnetic methods with uncertainties measured in tens of thousands of years. When this tour writes "~1 Mya," the tilde is doing real work.

Just as important: most "firsts" in prehistory are really "earliest currently known." They mark the oldest evidence found so far, not the true beginning — and they move whenever a new site is excavated. Two well-known jolts make the point, and the 2020s have kept adding to it. In 2015, stone tools at Lomekwi in Kenya pushed the origin of toolmaking back from ~2.6 to ~3.3 million years ago, before our own genus existed. In 2017, fossils from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco pushed the oldest known Homo sapiens back from ~195,000 to ~300,000 years — and shifted the picture from a single East-African cradle toward a pan-African origin. Since then the goalposts have kept moving: the Omo Kibish fossils that Jebel Irhoud displaced were themselves redated to at least 233,000 years in 2022; the earliest known Oldowan toolkit moved to Nyayanga, Kenya, in 2023, possibly ~2.9 million years old (its layers are bracketed to 3.0–2.6 million years); the oldest known figurative painting moved to at least 51,200 years in Sulawesi in 2024; and a 2026 study puts a hand stencil on Muna, an island just off Sulawesi, at a minimum of 67,800 years — a date not yet widely tested.

Three genuinely contested questions

Some debates are not just fuzzy dates but live disagreements among specialists. The tour picks a defensible middle path and flags the dispute; here are the big ones.

  • When did behavioral modernity arrive? Symbolic art, ornaments, and complex tools appear in Africa well before 70,000 years ago — ochre engravings at Blombos, shell beads across North and South Africa. Was there a sudden cognitive "revolution" ~50–70 kya, or a slow, patchy African build-up over 100,000+ years? The gradualist reading is increasingly favored, but it is not settled.
  • When (and how often) did we leave Africa? There were early excursions into the Levant — the Misliya Cave jaw ~180 kya, then the Skhul and Qafzeh burials ~120–90 kya — and into Arabia ~125–85 kya (Jebel Faya's tools and the Alathar footprints, credited to our species without any fossils, and the Al Wusta finger bone); a partial skull from Apidima in Greece may push a probe back past 210 kya, though its species assignment is disputed. But the lasting dispersal that founded all non-African populations is usually dated ~60–70 kya. Whether the earlier waves left any genetic trace is debated.
  • When were the Americas peopled? This is the sharpest fight in the field. For decades the "Clovis-first" model held that people arrived ~13,000 years ago. Pre-Clovis sites (Monte Verde in Chile, ~14,500 years) broke that consensus, and fossil human footprints at White Sands, New Mexico, have been dated to ~21,000–23,000 years ago — dates since corroborated by pollen and luminescence (2023) and by radiocarbon dates from two independent labs on wetland mud traced into the track-bearing layers (2025), though some specialists still argue the seed dates could be too old and treat them as maximum ages. The anchors keep moving: a 2026 geological re-analysis argues Monte Verde's camp is no older than ~8,200 years, a claim its excavators and others dispute. The tour treats a pre-Clovis arrival as likely while flagging the dates as unsettled.

Where the methods themselves are explained

This page is about how this tour was assembled and what its limits are. How the field actually establishes any of it — stratigraphy, radiocarbon and the IntCal calibration curve, argon-argon, uranium-series, luminescence, dendrochronology, ice cores, ancient DNA, palaeoproteomics, isotopes and lidar — has its own page: How We Know. It also carries a register of live debates and a list of things almost everyone gets wrong.

Rule of thumb for the whole tour: read every deep-time date as a midpoint with a wide, invisible bracket around it, and expect the oldest "firsts" to get older.

Reading the dates: Mya, kya, BCE, CE

The tour deliberately switches units partway through, because no single convention reads well across seven million years. Two abbreviations carry the deep past:

  • Mya — millions of years ago. "7 Mya" is seven million years before the present.
  • kya — thousands of years ago. "300 kya" is three hundred thousand years before the present.

Chapters I–II (deep prehistory and the Paleolithic) use these "years ago" units, because the numbers are astronomical and counting them from a zero point 2,000 years ago would be absurd — no one writes "298,000 BCE." From Chapter III onward (the Neolithic and everything after), the tour switches to the familiar calendar convention:

  • BCE — Before Common Era (numerically identical to BC), counting down to the year 1.
  • CE — Common Era (identical to AD), counting up from the year 1.

The prose switches at the II→III boundary: Chapter II's title card already ends in "12,000 BCE," and Chapter III's title card prints both units — 12,000 – 3500 BCE above the title, ≈ 14,000 – 5,500 years ago beneath it — so the change of gears is not silent. One wrinkle for the careful: the timeline rail's live year readout keeps counting in "kya" until about 12,000 years ago — roughly a quarter of the way into Chapter III — and only then flips to BCE.

The two systems differ only by a fixed offset of about 2,000 years: "years ago" is measured from roughly today, while BCE is measured from year 1. That is why 12,000 years ago ≈ 10,000 BCE. (One footnote for the careful: radiocarbon dates use "BP," Before Present, where "present" is pinned by convention to 1950 CE. The tour's casual "years ago" is rounded from today and is not the same precise datum.)

Data provenance

The quantitative through-lines — the population hockey stick, the energy transition, the Great Enrichment, the CO₂ curve, the biomass inversion — lean on a small set of well-known, widely-used datasets and syntheses. These are the standard reference compilations a data journalist or an undergraduate course would reach for. Crucially, the figures here are drawn from these syntheses rather than cited to a single original dataset: think of the list below as "the family of sources this kind of claim comes from," not a footnote to one file.

Deliberately global: these datasets aim at worldwide coverage, so Africa, the Americas, and Oceania appear on the same footing as Eurasia rather than as an afterthought. See the numbers assembled on The Great Acceleration.

The September 2026 expansion — and how it was checked

In September 2026 the tour was expanded chapter by chapter, and every content page — the nine chapters, the eight reference pages, the landing page and the chapter manifest behind the timeline rail — was audited again. The method described above did not change: this is still an AI-assisted synthesis. What changed is how much checking was layered on top of it. This section says what was done, what was added, and what the checking cannot promise.

An audit whose corrections were checked twice

Each page was read claim by claim against published sources — research papers, standard references, museum and data pages — and against the site's own other pages. Every correction the audit proposed was then handed to two independent verifiers with opposite jobs. One tried to defend the original text: to show that the page had been right, or right enough, after all. The other scrutinised the fix: was the replacement itself accurate, current, and consistent with the rest of the site? A correction went in only when both confirmed it, often in wording the verifiers amended. Where one of them was unsure, the change was made only if it could be confirmed separately; otherwise the original was softened into properly hedged wording. Where the defence won, the original stayed.

New material — the sections described below — went to two further independent checkers: one for the claims themselves (dates, places, attributions, the current state of each debate), and one for quotations, numbers and consistency with every other page on the site. Then each page went through the machine checks listed in the table.

Plainly: the auditor, the verifiers and the checkers were all separate AI passes, not human specialists. Setting them against one another is a real cross-check, but it is not peer review — and AI assistance still means errors are possible, including errors that several passes share.

Where the maps come from

The atlas scenes and the region panels' small maps are drawn from Natural Earth, a cartographic dataset placed in the public domain: its 1:50m land, lakes and rivers (only the larger lakes and rivers, simplified so a whole world stays light) and its 1:10m bathymetry at the 200 m depth contour. scripts/build-basemap.py turns them into one shared file, assets/img/world.svg, which every chapter references — nothing is traced by hand. Chapter IX's former Aral Sea shoreline comes from the same project's 1:10m historic-lakes layer.

The ice-age coastline is approximate. Chapter II's ice-age maps show as land everything shallower than today's 200 m depth contour (Chapter I keeps today's coast and tints that same shelf as shallow sea). At the Last Glacial Maximum, ~21,000 years ago, sea level was ~120–130 m lower than now, so the 200 m line somewhat over-draws the land that was exposed. Nor is today's sea floor exactly the old shore: local sea levels departed from the global figure where the crust sagged under the ice sheets and has since rebounded, and sediment has built up the shelves. Read those shores as the right shape, not a survey. Maps in later chapters use today's coasts and rivers, which have moved as well — the Yellow River has shifted its course and its mouth many times, and many deltas have grown seaward.

Errata: what the September 2026 review corrected

These are the significant corrections the review made: claims that were wrong, had been overtaken by newer research, or gave a date or number that disagreed with the rest of the site. Each one was confirmed by both verifiers and then checked again against the page as published. Where the same mistake appeared in several places on a page (a card, a timeline and a table, say), it is listed once. Smaller fixes to wording, tone and hedging are not listed. Each entry gives what the page used to say, what it says now, and why.

I · Deep Prehistory & Human Origins (11 entries)
  • Was: Homo antecessor, ~1.2 Mya, the oldest firmly dated humans in western Europe. Now: the Sima del Elefante fossils at Atapuerca (a jaw, and the 2025 “Pink” face), ~1.4–1.1 Mya, with Europe reached by ~1.4 Mya; H. antecessor follows at ~850–780 kya. Why: the species name and the date belonged to different fossils, and the 2025 face pushed the date back.
  • Was: oldest Homo erectus ~1.9 Mya at Lake Turkana. Now: ~2.0 Mya at Drimolen (South Africa) and Melka Kunture (Ethiopia). Why: older fossils have been published since.
  • Was: the Oldowan begins ~2.6 Mya with Homo habilis. Now: ~2.9–2.6 Mya, first at Nyayanga in Kenya, before any known habilis fossil. Why: the 2023 Nyayanga finds.
  • Was: the Paranthropus radiation ~2.7–1.0 Mya. Now: ~2.9–1.0 Mya. Why: the Nyayanga molars and a 2026 jaw from the Afar are older.
  • Was: “three genera” of hominins in East Africa at 1.8 Mya. Now: at least three species from two genera. Why: Paranthropus and Homo are two genera, not three.
  • Was: Olorgesailie’s new tools appear “in the same lake basin and the same century” as our species. Now: within dating error of the earliest fossils of our species. Why: the oldest sapiens fossils are in Morocco, 6,000 km away, with error bars of tens of thousands of years.
  • Was: the earliest organic toolkit is Schöningen, ~300 kya. Now: Kalambo Falls, Zambia, ~476 kya; Schöningen ~300–200 kya. Why: a 2023 find is older, and a 2025 study redated Schöningen.
  • Was: no sea crossing to Australia “for another 600,000 years”. Now: roughly another quarter of a million years after the chapter ends. Why: the arithmetic was counted from the wrong starting point.
  • Was: OSL moved Jebel Irhoud to ~315 kya. Now: thermoluminescence on burnt flint did. Why: the dating method was misnamed.
  • Was: Trinil, Java, 900 kya. Now: ~830–773 kya. Why: the site was redated in 2023.
  • Was: East Asia reached ~1.5 Mya at “Java & Zhoukoudian”, and China at ~780 kya. Now: China by ~1.66 Mya (Nihewan, Lantian), Java ~1.5–1.3 Mya. Why: 780 kya is Zhoukoudian’s oldest layer, not China’s first people.
II · Paleolithic & the Cognitive Revolution (9 entries)

  • Was: “140 millennia before any sapiens reached Europe”. Now: some 130 millennia before sapiens are securely attested in Europe. Why: the subtraction was wrong, and one Greek skull may be older.
  • Was: Lascaux comes “ten millennia” after the Swabian Jura carvings. Now: more than twenty millennia. Why: ~40 kya to ~17 kya is over 20,000 years.
  • Was: the map labels the Americas ~15 kya. Now: ~23–15 kya. Why: the label disagreed with the White Sands footprints described beside it.
  • Was: the Niah “Deep Skull” shows people in the rainforest ~46 kya. Now: occupation layers reach ~46 kya; the skull itself is ~37 kya. Why: the site and the skull have different dates.
  • Was: Border Cave’s digging stick and poison applicator, 44 kya. Now: 44–24 kya, with the poison applicator ~24 kya. Why: the objects span twenty thousand years.
  • Was: Denisovans known from a finger bone, teeth, one jaw and cave DNA, from Siberia to Tibet. Now: also the Harbin skull and Penghu jaw, with a range reaching north-east China and Taiwan. Why: 2025 protein and DNA studies identified both fossils.
  • Was: pigment processing ~100 kya, listed as the earliest. Now: ochre paint kits ~100 kya. Why: pigment use itself goes back ~320 kya (Chapter I).
  • Was: “composite & projectile tools”, earliest at ~64 kya. Now: probable bow-and-arrow and compound glues, ~71–61 kya. Why: hafted and thrown weapons are hundreds of thousands of years older.

What the checking cannot promise

  • Errors are still possible. This is still AI-assisted work. Two verifiers lower the odds that a wrong claim survives; they cannot bring them to zero, and checkers built on similar models can share blind spots.
  • The literature keeps moving. Several claims on this very page changed during the audit because studies published in 2022–2026 moved them. More will move.
  • Current to September 2026. Findings published after that are not reflected.
  • Still not peer review. Treat the tour as a starting point and the primary source as the authority — and if you find an error, trust the source over this site.

Credits & colophon

The Human History Tour is an open, static, zero-build website. There is no framework, no server, and no tracking.
