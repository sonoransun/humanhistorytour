Parallel Civilizations

History is usually told as a single torch passed hand to hand — Sumer to Egypt to Greece to Rome to Europe. Run the clock globally and that story dissolves: at every date, a dozen civilizations were rising at once, on every inhabited continent. Here they are, side by side — sixteen regional tracks, era by era, none of them allowed to vanish after prehistory.

The world, side by side

Pick any century of recorded history and it was crowded. When Rome ruled the Mediterranean, Han China governed a comparable share of humanity, the Maya were raising Tikal, Teotihuacan was among the largest cities on Earth, Meroë smelted iron on the upper Nile, and Funan's port at Óc Eo on the Mekong delta was handling Roman gold. When western Europe was in its early medieval centuries, Tang and Song China, Abbasid Baghdad, Chola India, Srivijaya's straits, the Ghana Empire, the Classic Maya, and Andean Tiwanaku were all at or near their heights. Below are sixteen regional tracks — the same sixteen regions the tour's chapters use — each run from its first cultures to roughly 1500 CE, each with a closing entry or two that carries it into the modern era (the Caribbean's, which pivots on 1492, runs on to 1804) — deliberately giving Africa, the Americas, Australia, the Arctic, and Oceania the same weight as Eurasia.

The diagram below names a few polities from each track; the table beneath it is the more useful tool. The single best way to feel how parallel the past was is to read it down a column instead of along a row: at each date, look at who was ascendant everywhere at once.

Then read each track down its own history. The sixteen follow the tour's fixed region order, west to east across the Old World and then north to south through the Americas.

West Asia — Mesopotamia, Anatolia, Iran & Arabia

Between the Tigris and Euphrates, humanity built the first cities, the first writing, and the first empires — then handed the land on through a long relay of conquerors. It is the classic "cradle," but it was never the world's only one, and its neighbours were never footnotes: Hittite Anatolia concluded the oldest peace treaty whose text survives, and the incense kingdoms of Yemen dammed a desert wadi to water their fields.

  • ~3500–2334 BCE — Sumerian city-states: Uruk, Ur, Lagash, Kish (cuneiform, the wheel)
  • ~2334 BCE — Akkadian Empire of Sargon; his daughter Enheduanna, high priestess at Ur, is the first author known by name — if the hymns that carry her name are hers
  • c. 2100 BCE — the Ur III revival; the Code of Ur-Nammu, some three centuries older than Hammurabi's
  • ~1894–1595 BCE — Old Babylonian kingdom; Hammurabi's law code (c. 1750 BCE)
  • ~1650–1180 BCE — Hittite Empire (Hattusa): fought Egypt at Kadesh (c. 1274 BCE), then c. 1259 BCE concluded with Ramesses II the oldest known surviving peace treaty — the only ancient Near Eastern treaty preserved in both parties' versions
  • ~911–609 BCE — Neo-Assyrian Empire (Nineveh); ~626–539 BCE — Neo-Babylonian (Nebuchadnezzar II)
  • ~750 BCE–575 CE — Saba, then Himyar, in Yemen: the Marib dam and the frankincense caravans north to the Mediterranean; Himyar's kings turned to Judaism in the late 4th century
  • 550–330 BCE — Achaemenid Persia (Cyrus, Darius) — the first world-spanning empire
  • 247 BCE–651 CE — Parthian, then Sasanian, Persia
  • 750–1258 CE — Abbasid Caliphate, ruling from Baghdad; later the Ottomans (1299–1922)

North Africa & the Nile — Egypt, Kush & Aksum

The Nile carried more than one civilization. Pharaonic Egypt is famous, but upriver the Nubian kingdoms of Kerma, Napata, and Meroë matched it — even ruling Egypt outright as its 25th Dynasty, the "Black Pharaohs." Further south and east, Aksum minted its own gold coinage and adopted Christianity in the 4th century, one of the earliest states in the world to do so. Kerma, Kush, Meroë, and Aksum keep falling through the seam between the "North Africa" and "sub-Saharan Africa" of modern atlases; on the ground, the Nile and the Horn are one cultural continuum, and the Saharan divide is a modern cartographic convention imposed on it — not a boundary anyone at the time observed. The desert itself held a kingdom: the Garamantes farmed the Fezzan through underground channels, and Berber movements from the Sahara and the Atlas built the Almoravid and Almohad empires that ruled from Marrakesh into al-Andalus.

  • ~3100–2181 BCE — Early Dynastic & Old Kingdom Egypt (the Giza pyramids, c. 2560 BCE)
  • ~2500–1500 BCE — Kingdom of Kerma, the first great Nubian state
  • ~2055–1650 & ~1550–1069 BCE — Middle & New Kingdoms (Hatshepsut, Ramesses II)
  • ~747–656 BCE — Kushite 25th Dynasty rules Egypt; Meroë endures to ~350 CE (ironworking, its own script); in 25–21 BCE a Kushite Kandake, generally identified as Amanirenas, fought Roman Egypt to a peace that exacted no tribute
  • 305–30 BCE — Ptolemaic Egypt (Alexandria); then Roman & Byzantine Egypt
  • ~1000 BCE–700 CE — the Garamantes of the Fezzan (Libya): a Saharan kingdom watered by foggara tunnels, strongest from the 2nd century CE
  • ~100–940 CE — Kingdom of Aksum (Ethiopia/Eritrea); Christianity c. 340 CE
  • ~540–1500 CE — Christian Nubia (Nobatia 543, then Makuria and Alodia; the 652 baqt with Arab Egypt); Zagwe & Solomonic Ethiopia (rock-hewn Lalibela)
  • 909–1171 CE — Fatimid Caliphate, which founded Cairo in 969
  • ~1050–1269 CE — the Almoravids (Marrakesh founded c. 1070), then the Almohads (from 1147): empires of the western Maghreb and al-Andalus
  • 1250–1517 CE — Mamluk Sultanate: Cairo, on Tertius Chandler's estimate the largest city on Earth c. 1315–48

West & Central Africa

Far from being "outside history," West and Central Africa built stone villages, ironworking cultures, gold empires, and forest kingdoms — and on its excavators' reading its towns grew before the camel caravans, not because of them: Djenné-Djenno on the inland Niger delta held perhaps 10,000–27,000 people with its satellite villages by c. 800 CE, and no palace. Mali's Mansa Musa spent and gave away so much gold in Cairo on his 1324 pilgrimage that its value there was reported depressed for years; Timbuktu became a centre of Islamic scholarship whose manuscripts number in the hundreds of thousands (some 350,000 were smuggled to Bamako for safety in 2012–13, and most remain there); and the brass heads of Ife and the plaques of Benin are the work of states as sophisticated as any of their contemporaries. The track continues east and south under East & Southern Africa.

  • ~2200–200 BCE — the Tichitt tradition, southern Mauritania: pearl-millet farmers building stone-walled villages and granaries from the mid-2nd millennium BCE — West Africa's earliest known complex society, probably ancestral to the Soninke who later founded Ghana
  • ~1500 BCE–1 CE — Nok culture (Nigeria): terracotta sculpture from ~900 BCE, iron smelting by ~750–550 BCE
  • ~250 BCE–1400 CE — Djenné-Djenno: iron worked from its first levels; a city with no citadel and no trace of a king
  • ~300–1200 CE — Ghana Empire (Wagadu), master of the gold-and-salt trade — origins uncertain; named by Arab geographers by the 9th century, and described by al-Bakri in 1068 as a royal town beside a merchant town with twelve mosques
  • ~800–1200 CE — Igbo-Ukwu (south-eastern Nigeria): intricate lost-wax copper-alloy vessels and regalia, and more than 150,000 glass and stone beads — its exact age is still argued
  • ~700–1893 CE — Kanem, then Bornu, around Lake Chad: Muslim from the 11th century, and one of the longest-lived states in African history
  • ~1235–c. 1600 CE — Mali Empire (Sundiata; Mansa Musa's 1324 hajj; Timbuktu, Djinguereber, Sankore)
  • ~1200–1400 CE — Ife: naturalistic heads in copper alloy, stone, and terracotta — the Yoruba city to which many Yoruba royal lines trace their origin
  • ~1000s–1897 CE — Kingdom of Benin (Edo): at its height in the 15th–16th centuries; its cast-brass plaques and heads were looted by a British force in 1897
  • ~1390–1914 CE — Kingdom of Kongo: King Nzinga a Nkuwu baptised in 1491; Afonso I's letters of 1526 protest Portuguese slaving
  • ~1464–1591 CE — Songhai Empire (Gao; Askia Muhammad) — one of the largest states in African history
  • 1624–1663 CE — Njinga of Ndongo & Matamba fights and out-negotiates Portuguese Angola for four decades
  • 1701–1902 CE — Asante Empire (Kumasi; Osei Tutu and the Golden Stool), rich on gold and on the Atlantic slave trade, until British annexation; 1804–1903 — the Sokoto Caliphate, the most populous state in 19th-century West Africa

East & Southern Africa

The continent's eastern half faced the Indian Ocean, and the monsoon made it a hinge of world trade. Swahili stone towns minted coins and traded gold and ivory for Chinese porcelain; inland, Mapungubwe and then Great Zimbabwe — whose Great Enclosure has mortarless granite walls up to 11 metres high — ran the gold routes; and Austronesian sailors from Borneo settled Madagascar. Long before, the region's first herders had built a monumental cemetery on the shore of Lake Turkana. (Aksum, the Horn's great early state, is followed on the Nile track.)

  • c. 3000–2300 BCE — Lothagam North, Lake Turkana (Kenya): a pillared communal cemetery raised by the region's earliest herders — eastern Africa's earliest known monumental cemetery
  • 5th century BCE–6th century CE — Urewe iron-smelting villages around Lake Victoria; Bantu-speaking farmer-smiths carry iron and crops down the east coast, reaching KwaZulu-Natal by c. 200–300 CE
  • 1st century CE — the Greek Periplus of the Erythraean Sea calls Rhapta "the very last market-town of the continent of Azania" (Schoff's 1912 translation) — on the East African coast, its site still unidentified
  • c. 500–1000 CE — Austronesians from southern Borneo settle Madagascar (firm archaeology c. 700–1000; earlier dates contested)
  • ~800–1500 CE — Swahili coast city-states (Kilwa, Mombasa, Zanzibar), with Mogadishu to the north: Kilwa minted its own coins c. 1100–1600, found as far inland as Great Zimbabwe; Ibn Battuta praised it in 1331; ancient DNA shows African and Asian — mostly Persian — ancestry mixing from c. 1000
  • c. 1000–1300 CE — K2, then Mapungubwe (from c. 1220), on the Limpopo: rulers on the hill, commoners below, gold and ivory for Indian Ocean glass beads
  • ~1100–1450 CE — Great Zimbabwe: ancestral Shona builders, a state of perhaps 50,000 km², the Great Enclosure raised in the 13th–14th centuries
  • ~1300–1500 CE — Bigo bya Mugenyi (Uganda): more than 10 km of ditch-and-bank earthworks, in the Great Lakes country where Bunyoro and Buganda later rose
  • 15th century–c. 1760 CE — Mutapa (Monomotapa), heir to Great Zimbabwe's gold trade on the northern plateau, whose gold drew the Portuguese inland from the 1500s
  • 1593–1698 CE — Portugal's Fort Jesus at Mombasa, until an Omani siege ends Portuguese power north of Mozambique; c. 1540–1897 — the Merina kingdom of Madagascar, ruling most of the island by the 19th century until French conquest

Europe & the Mediterranean

The Mediterranean was a shared sea, not a European lake. Phoenician and Carthaginian merchants, Greek city-states, and Rome all shaped it; the alphabet itself is a Phoenician export. After Rome's western fall, the Greek-speaking Byzantine Empire carried the classical inheritance for another thousand years while western Europe slowly rebuilt — with Muslim al-Andalus, not Paris or London, holding western Europe's great city.

  • ~2000–1100 BCE — Minoan (Crete) and Mycenaean palace cultures
  • ~1200–146 BCE — Phoenician city-states and Carthage, a Mediterranean sea-empire
  • ~800–323 BCE — Greek poleis (Athens, Sparta); Alexander's conquests
  • 509 BCE–476 CE — Roman Republic and Empire
  • 330–1453 CE — Byzantine (Eastern Roman) Empire, Constantinople
  • 711–1492 CE — al-Andalus: Umayyad Córdoba, on Tertius Chandler's reckoning the largest city on Earth c. 935–1013, and a conduit of Arabic learning into Latin Europe
  • ~793–1066 CE — the Viking Age: Norse raiders and traders from Dublin to Kyiv, Iceland settled c. 870, and a North American landfall at L'Anse aux Meadows, where wood was cut in 1021
  • ~800–1500 CE — Carolingian and feudal Europe; medieval Latin kingdoms
  • ~1300–1600 CE — Italian Renaissance city-states (Florence, Venice, Genoa)

Central Asia & the Steppe

The grasslands from Hungary to Mongolia were never empty — they were the engine room of Eurasian history. Steppe herders spread the Indo-European languages and domesticated the riding horse (~2200 BCE by the latest genomes); their mounted-nomad heirs spread the composite bow and the stirrup (paired stirrups are first attested in 4th-century Jin China; the Avars carried them to Europe), and repeatedly overturned the settled world. The line runs from the Scythians through the Xiongnu and the Turks to the Mongol Empire, the largest contiguous land empire ever assembled, whose Pax Mongolica briefly stitched the whole of Eurasia into one trade zone. Its settled half — the oasis cities of Sogdiana — ran the Silk Road's counting-houses.

  • ~3300–2600 BCE — Yamnaya ox-wagon herders (Indo-European dispersals); whether they also rode is disputed — the domestic horse's Eurasia-wide spread follows c. 2200 BCE
  • ~2100–1800 BCE — Sintashta, southern Urals: the oldest known spoked-wheel chariots, c. 2000 BCE
  • ~800–300 BCE — Scythians, Herodotus's mounted nomads
  • ~209 BCE–155 CE — Xiongnu confederation, the first steppe empire and rival to Han China
  • c. 313 CE — the Sogdian "Ancient Letters," found in a watchtower west of Dunhuang: merchants of Samarkand writing home along the Silk Road
  • 552–744 CE — Göktürk (Turkic) Khaganate, whose 8th-century Orkhon-valley inscriptions are the oldest substantial writing in a Turkic language; then the Uyghur Khaganate
  • ~1040–1200 CE — Seljuk Turks; the Kara-Khanids
  • 1206–1368 CE — Mongol Empire (Genghis Khan; the Pax Mongolica)
  • 1370–1507 CE — Timurid Empire (Samarkand, a last great steppe-and-city fusion); c. 1465 — the Kazakh Khanate forms, lasting until Russia abolished its khans in 1822–24

South Asia — the Indian subcontinent & Sri Lanka

South Asia opens with the Indus, one of the three primary Bronze-Age civilizations, its cities laid out on a grid with covered drains. It later blossomed into the Mauryan and Gupta empires — the latter a golden age that gave the world the decimal place-value system and the numeral for zero. In the south, the seafaring Cholas projected naval power clear across the Bay of Bengal, and in Sri Lanka the kings of Anuradhapura ruled for some fourteen centuries from a dry-zone capital watered by great reservoirs.

  • ~2600–1900 BCE — Indus / Harappan cities (Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Dholavira)
  • ~1500–500 BCE — Vedic period; the sixteen Mahajanapadas
  • ~322–185 BCE — Maurya Empire; Ashoka's rock edicts (c. 250 BCE)
  • ~437 BCE–1017 CE — Anuradhapura, Sri Lanka (founding date from the chronicle Mahāvaṃsa): a Theravada Buddhist kingdom of great tanks and canals and of stupas such as the Jetavana, until the Cholas took it
  • ~320–550 CE — Gupta Empire (mathematics, astronomy, a classical "golden age")
  • ~848–1279 CE — Chola Empire (maritime power; Rajendra's fleet raided Srivijaya in 1025)
  • 1206–1526 CE — Delhi Sultanate; Vijayanagara in the south (1336–1646)
  • 1526–1857 CE — Mughal Empire (Akbar; the Taj Mahal)

East Asia — China, Korea & Japan

East Asia sustained the longest continuous tradition of statecraft on Earth, cycling through dynasties for over three millennia. Tang alchemists found gunpowder; Song China turned it into weapons and gave us movable-type printing, true paper money, and the navigational compass — centuries before Europe. Around it, Korea and Japan built literate, bureaucratic states of their own, and Korea printed the oldest surviving book in movable metal type. (Vietnam's Đại Việt is followed on the Southeast Asia track.)

  • ~14,000 BCE onward — Jōmon foragers in Japan make some of the world's oldest pottery (China's Xianrendong is older still)
  • ~3300–2300 BCE — Liangzhu, lower Yangtze: a 290-hectare walled city with valley dams radiocarbon-dated c. 3250–2850 BCE, abandoned after decades of torrential rain
  • ~1600–1046 BCE — Shang dynasty (oracle-bone writing, ritual bronzes)
  • ~1046–256 BCE — Zhou dynasty (Confucius; the "Hundred Schools")
  • 221–206 BCE — Qin unification; 206 BCE–220 CE — Han dynasty
  • 618–907 CE — Tang dynasty (Chang'an, a cosmopolitan capital of perhaps a million)
  • 960–1279 CE — Song dynasty (Kaifeng, then Hangzhou; a commercial revolution)
  • 1271–1368 CE — Yuan (Mongol); 1368–1644 — Ming; 1644–1912 — Qing
  • Korea: 57 BCE (traditional)–935 CE — Silla; 918–1392 — Goryeo (the Tripitaka Koreana carved 1236–1251; the Jikji printed with movable metal type in 1377); 1392–1897 — Joseon (Sejong's hangul alphabet, 1443)
  • Japan: c. 900 BCE — Yayoi wet-rice farming reaches Kyushu (on the contested AMS radiocarbon redating; conventionally c. 300 BCE); 710–1185 — Nara and Heian (The Tale of Genji, c. 1010); 1603–1868 — Tokugawa rule from Edo, a city of about a million by the 1720s

Southeast Asia — the mainland & the islands

The hinge between the Indian Ocean and the China seas was never a corridor that others passed through: it was a world of its own. Villagers on the Khorat plateau cast bronze without palaces or kings; Funan's port at Óc Eo handled Roman, Indian, and Chinese goods; Srivijaya's fleets held the Straits of Malacca from the late 7th century to the early 11th; and Angkor spread a low-density city of canals, paddies, and reservoirs over some 1,000 km² — plausibly the most extensive pre-industrial settlement on Earth, home on one recent model to 700,000–900,000 people. Its states were mandalas, circles of tribute measured in ports and loyalties rather than borders — which is why border-drawing histories keep missing them.

  • ~2000 or ~1050 BCE — bronze at Ban Chiang and Ban Non Wat (Thailand): socketed axes and bangles cast by village rice farmers — whether it began nearer the earlier or the later date is a live dispute
  • 1st millennium BCE–1st century CE — Đông Sơn culture of the Red River delta: bronze drums traded from Yunnan to Java; in 40–43 CE the Trưng sisters raise the delta against Han rule
  • 1st–7th century CE — Funan on the Mekong delta, generally counted the first known kingdom in Southeast Asia; at its port Óc Eo a gold medallion of Antoninus Pius (152 CE) lay among Indian beads and Han mirrors
  • late 600s–1000s CE — Srivijaya (Palembang, Sumatra), the Buddhist thalassocracy of the straits: the Chinese monk Yijing studied there in 671; Chola raids in 1025 badly weakened it
  • c. 800 CE — Borobudur raised in Sailendra Java; 900 — the Laguna Copperplate, the oldest dated document from the Philippines
  • 802–1431 CE — Khmer Empire: Angkor Wat built c. 1113–1150, the city's core emptying gradually from the 1300s, long before the Ayutthayan sack of 1431
  • 938 CE onward — Đại Việt: Ngô Quyền's victory at Bạch Đằng (938) ends a thousand years of Chinese rule; the Trần outlast three Mongol-Yuan invasions (1258, 1285, 1287–88), defeating the last at Bạch Đằng in 1288, at the price of nominal tribute
  • 1044–1287 CE — Bagan (Burma): more than 10,000 Buddhist temples on the Irrawaddy plain
  • 1293–c. 1527 CE — Majapahit (Java), whose court poem of 1365 claims 98 tributaries from Sumatra to New Guinea — how far its reach really ran is debated
  • 1351–1767 CE — Ayutthaya (Siam), which early-16th-century European visitors counted among the three great powers of Asia; c. 1400–1511 — Melaka, the sultanate at the straits, whose Classical Malay became the lingua franca of maritime Southeast Asia, until Portugal took the port — but not the trade — in 1511

Australia

Australia and the Arctic are the two tracks world histories drop entirely — Australia's here, the Arctic's after Oceania's — and for the same reason: neither is monumental in stone temples and neither is written, so both are invisible to a discipline trained on archives and ruins. Australia holds the longest continuous cultural tradition on Earth — roughly 65,000 years from Madjedbebe to today — with fire-stick farming practised as continental-scale land management, songlines working as navigational and legal infrastructure, and, at Budj Bim, stone aquaculture older than the pyramids. Oral traditions there describe coastlines drowned more than 7,000 years ago in detail that matches the measured bathymetry.

  • ~65,000 years ago — Madjedbebe rock shelter, Arnhem Land: earliest currently known occupation of Australia (some researchers argue for nearer 50,000); ~40,000 years ago — Lake Mungo ritual burial and cremation, among the oldest known anywhere
  • ~10,000–6000 BCE (estimates vary) — rising seas flood Bass Strait and cut Tasmania off; its people go on to live in one of the longest isolations of any human population
  • ~4600 BCE — Budj Bim, Victoria: Gunditjmara stone eel channels, weirs, and traps, dated to ~6,600 years old — one of the world's oldest aquaculture systems (UNESCO-listed 2019)
  • ~3000–1000 BCE — backed stone blades proliferate, Pama-Nyungan languages spread over some seven-eighths of the continent, and the dingo arrives by sea (bones from Madura Cave dated 3,350–3,080 years ago)
  • ~1000–600 BCE, and again ~20 BCE–135 CE — Jiigurru (Lizard Island): Australia's oldest securely dated pottery, made on the island by the reef's canoe voyagers
  • From tens of millennia ago — Murujuga, Pilbara: by common estimate more than a million petroglyphs, the oldest thought to be tens of thousands of years old (UNESCO-listed 2025)
  • Before 1788 (how long before is uncertain) — a continental exchange system: engraved Kimberley pearl shell reaches desert communities 2,000 km inland; Mount William greenstone axes travel hundreds of kilometres
  • ~1640–1700 onward (start argued) — Macassan fleets from Sulawesi work the trepang trade with Arnhem Land, Australia's long-running external trade link
  • 1788 CE — British colonisation begins at Sydney Cove; only in 1992 does the High Court's Mabo judgment reject terra nullius

Oceania & the Pacific — the Austronesian world

The greatest maritime expansion in human history was Austronesian: from Taiwan around 3000 BCE, seafarers in outrigger and double-hulled canoes settled a realm spanning from Madagascar to Rapa Nui — more than half the circumference of the planet — navigating by stars, swells, cloud, and birds. The Lapita people seeded the western Pacific; their Polynesian descendants reached the very last uninhabited lands on Earth: Hawaiʻi, Rapa Nui, and finally Aotearoa around 1250 CE. Before any of them, New Guinea's highlanders had invented farming on their own; later, Micronesians built a basalt city on a reef, and Tonga's paramount chiefs ran a maritime state.

  • ~8000–5000 BCE — Kuk Swamp, New Guinea highlands: pits by ~8000 BCE (disputed), mounded taro and banana plots by ~5000 BCE — an independent invention of farming
  • ~3000 BCE — Austronesian expansion begins, out of Taiwan
  • ~1500 BCE (contested) — a crossing of more than 2,000 km of open sea from the Philippines to the Marianas; stricter readings of the radiocarbon bring it close to Lapita's own landfall
  • ~1500–500 BCE — Lapita culture (Bismarck Archipelago to Fiji, Tonga, Samoa), entering Remote Oceania c. 1100–1000 BCE
  • ~900 BCE–1000 CE — West Polynesia settled (Tonga, Samoa), then the "long pause" in which Ancestral Polynesian society took shape
  • c. 500–1000 CE — Austronesians settle Madagascar, off the African coast
  • ~1025–1120 CE — the Society Islands settled, the first step into East Polynesia (radiocarbon on short-lived samples)
  • ~1000–1250 CE — Hawaiʻi and Rapa Nui (Easter Island; the moai) reached — Rapa Nui probably not before ~1200
  • c. 1180–early 1600s CE — Nan Madol, Pohnpei: 92 artificial islets of stacked basalt and coral, seat of the Saudeleur dynasty
  • c. 1200 CE — Polynesians and Native Americans meet: a single contact read from modern genomes, and Native American ancestry in Rapa Nui's ancient genomes
  • ~950–1865 CE — the Tuʻi Tonga line (founded, by tradition, in the 10th century); after c. 1200 its maritime state drew two-thirds of its worked stone tools from Samoa, Fiji and beyond — one adze from the Society Islands, 2,500 km away
  • ~1250–1300 CE — Aotearoa / New Zealand settled (Māori) — the last major landmass humans found
  • 1795–1810 CE — Kamehameha I unites the Hawaiian islands into one kingdom

The Arctic & the circumpolar north

The Arctic is precision engineering in bone, ivory, and skin: the toggling harpoon, the kayak and the umiak, dog traction, tailored clothing sewn for cold no other technology could survive. That package — carried by the Thule, ancestors of the Inuit, from Alaska to Greenland in little more than a century, starting around 1200 CE (radiocarbon revisions since 2008 collapsed the older 1000-CE chronology) — is as consequential as any technology suite in this tour. It also outlasted the Norse, who arrived in Greenland with iron and livestock and were gone by ~1450. On the Eurasian side, the Sámi, Nenets, Khanty, and Chukchi built reindeer, fur, and sea-mammal economies that empires later taxed.

  • ~45,000 years ago — Sopochnaya Karga, Yenisei Gulf: a mammoth hunted at 72° north; ~32,000 years ago — Yana RHS, Siberia: a camp at ~71° north, deep inside the Arctic Circle, in the middle of the last ice age
  • ~7000 BCE — Zhokhov Island (76° N): sled runners and dogs bred for hauling — engineered transport in the high Arctic
  • ~2500–2000 BCE — Arctic Small Tool tradition sweeps from Alaska to Greenland; the Saqqaq culture settles Greenland (a Saqqaq man's hair gave the first ancient human genome, in 2010)
  • ~500 BCE–1300 CE — Dorset (Tuniit) culture across the eastern Arctic; Old Bering Sea and Ipiutak ivory carving on the Bering Strait
  • ~500 BCE–1 CE — Proto-Sámi takes shape, on Ante Aikio's dating, in southern Finland or Karelia, then spreads north and west across Fennoscandia during the first millennium CE; c. 890 CE — Ohthere tells King Alfred's court of Sámi tribute in furs and ship's ropes
  • 985–c. 1450 CE — Norse Greenland (the last written record, a wedding at Hvalsey, 1408)
  • ~1200–1300 CE — Thule expansion from Alaska to Greenland (Thule culture itself forms in Alaska by ~1000); ancient DNA shows the Thule replaced the Dorset rather than descending from them
  • 1582–1639 CE — Russia crosses Siberia to the Pacific on yasak, fur tribute taken from Khanty, Evenki, Sakha, and dozens of other peoples; 1721 — Hans Egede's mission begins the Danish claim to Greenland
  • 1999 CE — Nunavut created, an Inuit-majority territory a fifth the size of Canada

North America, north of Mexico

This half-continent usually gets a single line in world histories, and it deserves a track. It was an independent centre of plant domestication — the Eastern Agricultural Complex, the least-told of the world's farming inventions — and its peoples raised earthen monuments for five millennia, starting with hunter-gatherers who never farmed at all. On the Pacific Northwest coast, salmon societies grew dense, sedentary, and sharply hierarchical without any agriculture — a standing rebuttal to the assumption that complexity requires farming. Europeans who arrived met federations and farming towns, not wilderness.

  • ~23,000–13,000 years ago — the peopling: human footprints at White Sands, New Mexico (~21–23 kya, contested), the pre-Clovis case wedged open by Monte Verde in Chile, then the Clovis horizon
  • ~6500–1600 BCE — Old Copper Complex, Great Lakes: annealed native-copper spearpoints and awls, made by foragers — among the earliest metalworking anywhere on Earth
  • ~3000 BCE onward — Eastern Agricultural Complex: domesticated squash by ~3000 BCE (Phillips Spring, Missouri), sunflower by ~2900 BCE, marshelder by ~2450 BCE, goosefoot by ~1800 BCE, and erect knotweed — an independent invention of farming
  • ~3500 BCE — Watson Brake, Louisiana: eleven mounds raised by non-farming hunter-gatherers, the American parallel to Göbekli Tepe
  • ~1700–1100 BCE — Poverty Point, Louisiana: six concentric earthen ridges some 1.2 km across, parts raised within months, by fishers and foragers trading for copper, galena, and soapstone from as far as 1,000 km away
  • ~500 BCE–500 CE — Adena, then the Hopewell Interaction Sphere: a continental exchange web moving obsidian from Yellowstone, mica from Appalachia, and copper from Lake Superior
  • ~300–1500 CE — Hohokam, Arizona: the most complex canal network of the pre-contact Americas, parts of it now retraced by Phoenix's own canals
  • ~850–1150 CE — Ancestral Puebloan Chaco Canyon: great houses and an engineered road network; the 1276–1299 megadrought later empties the Four Corners
  • ~1050–1350 CE — Mississippian Cahokia (Illinois): Monks Mound and a city usually put at 10,000–20,000 (estimates run 6,000–40,000) — on the highest figures, unmatched by any city north of Mexico until Philadelphia in the 1780s
  • ~1000 BCE onward — Pacific Northwest plank-house villages (Paul Mason site) grow into dense, ranked salmon societies without farming
  • ~1142–1600 — the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy founded (its Great Law dated variously), with clan mothers holding the power to seat and depose chiefs
  • 1680 CE — the Pueblo Revolt under Po'pay drives Spain out of New Mexico for twelve years; horses from the abandoned ranches carry the Comanche and Lakota into wholly new mounted societies

Mesoamerica

In complete isolation from the Old World, Mesoamerica invented writing, the concept of zero, and monumental cities independently. Teotihuacan held perhaps 100,000–150,000 people — one of the half-dozen largest cities on Earth in its day, outranked in Europe only by Rome and Constantinople; the Maya tracked Venus and the eclipse cycle with a calendar of extraordinary precision; and Aztec Tenochtitlan, an island city laced with causeways and raised chinampa gardens, rivalled the largest cities in Europe when Spanish soldiers first saw it in 1519.

  • ~1500–400 BCE — Olmec (San Lorenzo, La Venta): colossal heads, the "mother culture"
  • ~1000 BCE–1697 CE — Maya (Aguada Fénix, Tikal, Calakmul, Palenque; the last kingdom, Itza Nojpetén, fell in 1697): glyphic writing, zero, the Long Count
  • ~500 BCE–800 CE — Zapotec at Monte Albán (Oaxaca)
  • ~100 BCE–550 CE — Teotihuacan (perhaps 100,000–150,000 people; the Pyramid of the Sun)
  • ~900–1150 CE — Toltec (Tula); ~800–1200 — Chichén Itzá, then Mayapán (c. 1220s–1440s), capitals of the northern Maya
  • early 1300s–1530 CE — the Purépecha (Tarascan) state, Tzintzuntzan: it held the Aztecs off in the west and worked metal for tools and weapons
  • 1428–1521 CE — Aztec/Mexica Triple Alliance (Tenochtitlan, ~200,000)
  • 1847–1901 CE — the Caste War of Yucatán: rebel Maya run an independent state in the eastern forest for half a century

The Caribbean

The islands' story is short by this page's standards and pivotal beyond their size. Archaic foragers were on Trinidad by ~6000 BCE and had crossed to Cuba — over water from which no land could be seen — by ~4000 BCE; Saladoid farmers came north out of the Orinoco with pottery and cassava; and by ~1000 CE the Taíno had built chiefdom societies whose words — hurricane, canoe, hammock, tobacco, barbecue — are still in daily use. Then 1492 landed here first. The encomienda and Old World disease produced a demographic catastrophe within a generation; the sugar-and-slavery complex that replaced it made these small islands the most valuable colonies on Earth; and the people it enslaved answered with the maroon wars — and with a revolution.

  • ~6000 BCE — Banwari Trace, Trinidad (Ortoiroid): a fisher-foragers' shell midden, the oldest known site in the Caribbean, from when the island was still joined, or nearly, to South America
  • ~4000–2000 BCE — Archaic (Casimiroid) seafarers reach Cuba, Hispaniola and Puerto Rico; at Canímar Abajo, Cuba, a cemetery in use from c. 2200 BCE shows supposed foragers eating cultivated plants
  • ~500 BCE–600 CE — Saladoid expansion from the Orinoco into Puerto Rico and the Lesser Antilles: pottery, cassava griddles, village life; ancient genomes show the newcomers largely replaced the Archaic islanders
  • ~600–1200 CE — Ostionoid peoples, heirs of the Saladoid, spread through the Greater Antilles
  • ~1000–1492 CE — Taíno chiefdoms under caciques across the Greater Antilles: ball courts (Caguana, c. 1270), cotton, cassava; Kalinago (Carib) peoples in the Lesser Antilles; ancient DNA suggests tens of thousands on Puerto Rico and Hispaniola, not hundreds of thousands — a figure still debated
  • 1492–1518 CE — Columbus reaches the Bahamas and Hispaniola; the encomienda; the 1514 roll counts just 26,334 Taíno on Hispaniola; smallpox arrives 1518
  • 1519–1533 CE — Enriquillo, a Taíno cacique, holds out against Spain in Hispaniola's mountains for fourteen years
  • ~1640–1790 CE — the sugar-and-slavery complex: Barbados, Jamaica, Saint-Domingue — the most valuable colonies on Earth, fed by the Middle Passage
  • ~1605–1694 CE — Palmares, Brazil: a maroon state of perhaps 10,000–20,000 under Ganga Zumba and Zumbi; Maroon wars in Jamaica (Nanny of the Windward Maroons; treaties 1739–40) and Suriname (treaties 1760s)
  • 1760–61 CE — Tacky's Revolt, Jamaica, led by a Fante royal: the largest rising in the British West Indies until 1831; 1797 — after the Carib War on St Vincent, Britain deports thousands of Black Caribs to Roatán, where the survivors found the Garifuna communities of Central America
  • 1791–1804 CE — the Haitian Revolution (Toussaint Louverture, Dessalines): the most successful slave uprising in history — the only one to found a state free of slavery and ruled by the formerly enslaved — and the second independent republic in the Americas

South America — the Andes & Amazonia

The Andes hosted the oldest civilization in the Americas — Norte Chico, raising monuments while Egypt built its first pyramids — and an unbroken 4,500-year chain of cultures that climaxed in the Inca, the largest empire the pre-Columbian Americas ever saw. The Inca ran it without the wheel, without money, and without alphabetic writing, but with terraced mountains, all-weather roads, and the knotted-string quipu. Andean metallurgy — gold, silver, copper, arsenical bronze — was an independent invention, not a borrowing. And the forest below was never empty: manioc was domesticated on the Amazon's southwestern rim, and airborne lidar now shows garden towns in Ecuador's Upano valley as old as the Roman Republic and low-density cities with causeways and reservoirs in the Bolivian Amazon.

  • ~14,500 years ago — Monte Verde, southern Chile: a camp that broke the "Clovis first" model of the peopling of the Americas
  • ~8000 BCE onward — manioc, squash, and chili domesticated in southwestern Amazonia, where people built up thousands of "forest islands" in the Llanos de Moxos; in the Andes, the potato, quinoa, the llama, and the alpaca
  • ~5050 BCE onward — Chinchorro fisher-foragers of the Atacama coast make the oldest known artificial mummies
  • ~3500–1800 BCE — Norte Chico / Caral-Supe (Peru): among the oldest cities on Earth
  • ~1200–500 BCE — Chavín de Huántar, a pan-Andean religious network (newer radiocarbon series put most building before c. 750 BCE)
  • ~500 BCE–600 CE — the Upano valley, Amazonian Ecuador: some 6,000 earthen platforms joined by straight roads, revealed by airborne lidar (published 2024)
  • ~100 BCE–800 CE — Nazca (the Nazca Lines) and Moche (north-coast states; the Lady of Cao, a tattooed woman buried with war clubs, c. 450)
  • ~500–1000 CE — Wari (Ayacucho basin, ~600–1000 CE) and Tiwanaku (Lake Titicaca, ~500–1000 CE) — twin highland empires
  • ~400–1400 CE — Marajoara mound-builders at the mouth of the Amazon, at their height c. 800–1400; ~500–1400 CE — the Casarabe culture of the Llanos de Moxos, Bolivia: platform mounds and pyramids up to 22 m tall, linked by causeways and canals (lidar, 2022)
  • ~900–1470 CE — Chimú (Chan Chan, the largest adobe city of the pre-Columbian Americas)
  • 1438–1533 CE — Inca Empire / Tawantinsuyu (Cusco, Machu Picchu; ~10–12 million subjects) — 1438 is the traditional date, and radiocarbon runs earlier
  • 1598–1641 CE — the Mapuche destroy seven Spanish towns and win recognition of their autonomy south of the Biobío; 1780–83 — Túpac Amaru II's great Andean rising

The largest city on Earth

One clean way to watch the centre of human density move is to track the single largest city on the planet, century by century. The title is a relay, and — tellingly — it spends most of history not in Europe. It starts in Sumer, passes back and forth between Mesopotamia and Egypt, moves to the Ganges valley or Alexandria and to Han Chang'an, rests in Rome and then Constantinople, returns to Tang Chang'an and Abbasid Baghdad, and then to Song, Ming, and Qing China — with brief stops, on Tertius Chandler's count, in Córdoba, Merv, Fez, Mamluk Cairo, and Ottoman Istanbul — and only in the industrial blink of the last two centuries lands in London, then New York, then Tokyo — and, on the UN's 2025 count, Jakarta.

How we know how big a city was

  • Area times density. Most figures before 1800 multiply a walled or mapped area by an assumed number of people per hectare. Change the density and the answer doubles or halves — which is how Chandler can put Rome at ~450,000 around 100 CE while Modelski and Morris put it near a million, and why Hangzhou's estimates run from 200,000 to a million and a half.
  • Counts of the taxed and the fed, not of heads. Where states counted, they counted the people they taxed or fed. Augustus's own record of his reign lists a cash handout to 320,000 of Rome's urban plebs in 5 BCE — citizen men only, so women, children, slaves, and foreigners have to be estimated on top; Chinese registers often give households rather than people, which then need a multiplier.
  • Censuses — and definitions. From Britain's first census in 1801, the figures are counts — but counts of what? The UN's 2025 revision stopped using each country's own city boundaries and measured every city from the same 1-km population grids, and the league table changed: Jakarta first, Tokyo third.
  • What the lists cannot see. Compilations built from walled cities miss low-density urbanism. A 2021 model built from lidar, excavation, and radiocarbon dates puts thirteenth-century Angkor at 700,000–900,000 people spread over some 1,000 km² — a figure that would rank it near the top of this table for its century, yet it appears on none of the three lists.

Why polycentric

The way we usually narrate history is a relay of a single baton — Mesopotamia to Egypt to Greece to Rome to Christian Europe to the modern West. It is tidy, and it is false. At any given moment that baton was only one of a dozen being carried at once, on tracks that rarely touched. The single-file story is a trick of the last two centuries reading themselves backward into the whole of the past.

The clearest evidence is that the great inventions arose in parallel, not down one line. Farming was domesticated independently at least eleven times, on five continents. Writing was invented from scratch at least three times. A placeholder zero appeared at least three times — in Babylon (by c. 300 BCE), in Mesoamerica (Long Count dates from 36 BCE, then the Maya), and in India, which alone went on to treat zero as a number in its own right (Brahmagupta, 628). Cities, monumental architecture, metallurgy, calendars, and the state each emerged more than once, in places that had never heard of one another.

The record understates this parallelism, which strengthens the polycentric case rather than weakening it. Preservation is not neutral: arid limestone country keeps its monuments while tropical soils dissolve wood, cloth, and bone — so a mudbrick ziggurat survives where a rainforest capital becomes a soil stain, until lidar finds its earthworks under the canopy. The shorelines where ice-age peoples actually lived lie beneath roughly 120 metres of postglacial sea rise — a drowning Australian oral traditions independently remember. And excavation money has pointed at the Classical and Biblical lands for two centuries, so the ground between the Tigris and the Nile is simply better searched than the Amazon or the Congo basin. Every one of these biases deletes evidence from the tropics, the coasts, and the unwritten world — precisely the places the single-torch story leaves out. Airborne lidar is now correcting the bias faster than any excavation could: it found Aguada Fénix, a Maya platform of ~1000 BCE larger in volume than the Great Pyramid (published 2020); traced Angkor's urban grid far beyond its temples (2012–2015); mapped causeways, reservoirs, and platform towns of the Casarabe culture in the Bolivian Amazon (2022); and showed some 6,000 platforms and a road network under the forest of Ecuador's Upano valley (2024). See how the evidence is weighed for how historians correct for it.

So progress was never a torch handed down a single line. It was a relay run across many tracks at once, with ideas — the alphabet, paper, gunpowder, the numeral for zero, the horse, the domesticated crop — leaping between them along trade routes, monsoon winds, and Silk Roads. Song China printed books while the Maya read the stars while Ghana taxed the gold caravans while Polynesians read the swell of the open Pacific. No one of them was "the centre of the world."

For all but the last couple of centuries, in fact, there was no centre at all. The centre of the world was wherever you happened to be standing. Only with industrial power, oceanic empire, and global communication did the many tracks braid — at last — into the single, crowded, planet-wide story of the Great Acceleration.
