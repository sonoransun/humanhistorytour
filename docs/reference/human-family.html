The Human Family

For all but the last few tens of millennia of the past four million years, the Earth carried more than one kind of human at the same time. Australopiths walking upright with ape-sized brains; Paranthropus chewing its way through a completely different solution to the same problem; Neanderthals with larger skulls than ours building rings of stalagmites 336 metres inside a French cave; a metre-tall species on Flores; a population in Siberia we named from its DNA before we had seen its face. Every one of them is a control experiment on what "human" means — and being the only survivor is the strangest thing about us.

Bush, not ladder

Everyone knows the picture: a stooped ape at the left, straightening through four or five intermediates into a striding modern man at the right. It was drawn by Rudolph Zallinger for a 1965 Time-Life volume, it was captioned The Road to Homo Sapiens, and its own authors warned that it was a compression. It is wrong in one specific, correctable way: it has no branches. Read literally it says each species turned into the next, that there was one lineage at a time, and that the whole thing was aimed at us. None of those three claims is true.

The real shape is a bush. Lineages split, ran in parallel for hundreds of thousands of years, overlapped in the same landscapes, and mostly ended. Paranthropus boisei was not an early draft of Homo; it was a contemporary of Homo for something like a million years, doing something else entirely, and doing it well. Almost every twig on the bush is a terminal twig. Ours is the exception, and there is no evidence that it was the intended one.

How many twigs there are depends on who is counting. Lumpers read the variation inside a fossil sample as ordinary variation within one species — the way modern humans vary from Copenhagen to Chennai — and end up with perhaps a dozen hominin species. Splitters read the same variation as evidence of separate species and end up with twenty-five or more. At the far lumping end, a 2026 paper proposes folding Australopithecus and Paranthropus into an enlarged Homo altogether, because Australopithecus as usually drawn is not a natural group, just a grade. The Dmanisi skulls (see below) are the sharpest test case in the discipline: five crania from one Georgian site at one moment in time, varying so much that if they had been found on five different continents they would certainly have been given five different names.

Worse for the tidy diagram: these lineages interbred. A species concept built on reproductive isolation simply leaks when Neanderthals, Denisovans and sapiens are all exchanging genes, and when the Denisovan genome itself carries a slab of ancestry from something older still that has never been found. The tree below is therefore one model among several. Its trunk is reasonably secure; its branch points are argued about at every conference, and its shape changes every few years — eleven of the twigs on it were first described in 2001 or later, and in 2025 alone ancient proteins merged two of them and a 3.4-million-year-old foot was finally assigned to a third.

The tree above can only show ancestry. It cannot show the thing that matters most about the human family, which is that it was plural in real time. Freeze the planet at five moments and count who is breathing.

Before the split

The hominin story does not start at 7 Mya; it starts as a footnote to a mass extinction. When the Chicxulub impact ended the Cretaceous 66 million years ago, the surviving mammals were small, nocturnal and unremarkable. Within ten million years primates were diversifying in the warm forests of the Paleocene and Eocene — small-bodied, grasping-handed, forward-eyed animals whose whole anatomy was a bet on branches. Nearly everything we later congratulate ourselves for begins as an arboreal adaptation: stereoscopic vision, a shoulder that rotates, a hand that opposes, a brain that is expensive because judging a gap between branches is expensive.

The apes proper arrive in the Miocene, and the Miocene was their epoch, not ours. Between roughly 23 and 5 Mya, tens of ape genera spread across Africa, Europe and Asia — a radiation far richer than today's eight surviving genera (four of them gibbons) suggest. Proconsul (~21–17 Mya, Kenya) had no tail and an ape's elbow but a monkey's cautious quadrupedal gait. Sivapithecus (~12.5–8.5 Mya, the Siwaliks of Pakistan and India) has a face startlingly like an orangutan's and is generally read as a member of that branch. Dryopithecus (~12.5–11 Mya, France and Spain; the Hungarian ape from Rudabánya is now Rudapithecus) was a European forest ape with suspensory arms. And Danuvius guggenmosi, described in 2019 from the Hammerschmiede clay pit in Bavaria at ~11.6 Mya, preserves a limb combination its describers call "extended limb clambering" — straight legs and a load-bearing extended knee alongside powerful grasping arms. If the reading holds, a posture with elements of bipedality existed in Europe several million years before the human lineage existed anywhere.

When did our line separate from the chimpanzee line? Here the evidence genuinely disagrees with itself. The oldest fossil candidate hominin, Sahelanthropus, is ~7 Mya. Molecular-clock estimates — calibrated from mutation rates and generation times — have ranged from ~6.5 to ~13 Mya, and the spread narrowed only slightly when direct per-generation mutation rates were measured in living families, because a slower measured rate pushes the divergence older. The honest summary is a window of roughly 9 to 6 Mya, with the fossil record sitting at the young end of it and the genetics arguing for something deeper. The split was probably not clean either: genome analyses suggest gene flow between the two proto-lineages continued for a while after they first diverged.

The most common mistake in this entire subject

  • We are not descended from chimpanzees. Chimpanzees and bonobos are our cousins. Both lineages have been evolving for the same ~7 million years since the split; a chimpanzee is not a frozen ancestor, it is a modern animal with its own long history of specialisation.
  • The last common ancestor was neither. It was not a chimp and not a proto-human. Ardipithecus showed that the ancestor probably did not knuckle-walk, which means chimpanzee knuckle-walking is a derived trait on their side, not a leftover from ours.
  • Genetic similarity is not a hierarchy. The ~98.8% figure describes aligned single-nucleotide differences and quietly excludes most insertions, deletions and duplications. It is a real number and a bad slogan.

The living apes still do essential work for us: they bracket what the last common ancestor could already do. Anything shared by chimpanzees, bonobos and humans is more parsimoniously explained as inherited than as invented three times. And what chimpanzees do is a great deal. At Taï in Côte d'Ivoire, communities crack Panda and Coula nuts with stone and wooden hammers on root anvils — a skill that takes years to learn, is transmitted by watching mothers, and leaves an archaeological signature; the Noulo site in Taï yielded 4,300-year-old chimpanzee nut-cracking stones, the oldest excavated non-human Stone Age — Brazilian capuchins have one reaching back ~3,000 years, Thai macaques a far shorter one. At Gombe in Tanzania, Jane Goodall watched David Greybeard strip a grass stem and fish for termites in 1960 — the observation that forced the discipline to redefine "man the toolmaker." At Fongoli in Senegal, savanna chimpanzees sharpen branches with their teeth and jab them into tree hollows to hunt bushbabies, and the hunters are disproportionately female. Different communities have different tool kits, greetings and grooming styles that do not track ecology or genetics — that is culture, by any working definition.

So the baseline is high. Tool use, teaching by tolerance, cooperative hunting, coalition politics, a territorial and sometimes lethal intergroup violence, food sharing, and in bonobos an entirely different settlement of the same social problems through sex and female alliance: all of that plausibly sat in the ancestor. What follows in the hominin record is not the invention of intelligence. It is a very long argument about walking, chewing, and eventually about how much brain a body can afford.

The australopith world, and the other way to be a hominin

Between about 7 and 2 million years ago Africa carried a succession of upright apes with brains barely larger than a chimpanzee's. The sequence is not a queue; it is a thicket with a few reasonably firm nodes.

Sahelanthropus tchadensis (~7 Mya, Toros-Menalla, Chad) is known chiefly from the crushed cranium TM 266, nicknamed Toumaï. Its case for hominin status rests on the position of the foramen magnum — the hole where the spinal cord enters — which sits forward, as it does in animals that carry their head over an upright spine. That reading is live and unsettled: a partial femur and two forearm bones from the same deposit, described in 2022, were read by their authors as evidence of habitual upright walking; a 2025 reanalysis concluded they do not support habitual bipedalism; and a 2026 study found them chimpanzee-like in shape but hominin-like in proportion, with a femoral tubercle it reports only in bipeds. Three teams, the same bones, opposite conclusions. Orrorin tugenensis (~6 Mya, Tugen Hills, Kenya) is a better-preserved argument: its femoral necks are long with a distinctive internal bone distribution that in living primates tracks habitual upright loading.

Ardipithecus matters more than either, because we have so much of it. Ar. kadabba (~5.8–5.2 Mya) is fragmentary; Ar. ramidus is not. The skeleton ARA-VP-6/500 — "Ardi", ~4.4 Mya, Aramis in Ethiopia's Middle Awash — took fifteen years to excavate and prepare from sediment so soft the bones crumbled at a touch. When it was published in 2009 it demolished a comfortable story. Ardi has a grasping big toe, so it climbed; it also has a pelvis restructured for upright walking. And the fauna and plants buried with it describe a woodland, not a grassland. Bipedalism did not arise because forests receded and our ancestors stood up to see over grass. It arose in the trees' company, for reasons still argued — carrying, thermoregulation, feeding posture, or moving between fruiting trees on the ground.

A. anamensis (~4.2–3.8 Mya) was long assumed to be simply the ancestor of A. afarensis, one turning smoothly into the other. In 2019 the near-complete cranium MRD-VP-1/1 from Woranso-Mille, Ethiopia gave anamensis a face for the first time — and dated it to 3.8 Mya, which is younger than the 3.9 Mya afarensis frontal bone from nearby Belohdelie. The two species overlapped for at least 100,000 years. That single date converted a straight line into a branch.

A. afarensis (~3.9–2.9 Mya) is the best-known australopith, because of two finds. In November 1974 at Hadar, Donald Johanson and Tom Gray spotted an arm bone in a gully and eventually recovered AL 288-1 — about 40% of one skeleton, female, roughly 1.1 m tall, with a brain near 400 cc and a pelvis and knee that are unambiguously bipedal. She was named Lucy after the Beatles song playing in camp. Then at Laetoli in Tanzania — where animal prints were noticed in 1976 and Paul Abell found the hominin trackway itself in 1978 — Mary Leakey's team excavated a 27-metre trail of footprints pressed into damp volcanic ash 3.66 million years ago — with a well-developed arch, an adducted big toe and a heel-strike gait. Not a shuffling ape. A walk. Millions of years before anything you could call a big brain.

Walking did not mean leaving the trees. The Dikika child, "Selam" (DIK-1-1) — a girl of two years and four months (861 days, counted from her dental growth lines in 2020; long put at about three), ~3.3 Mya, found in 2000 by Zeresenay Alemseged's team in the Afar and described in 2006 — has shoulder blades built for climbing and a hyoid, the small bone that anchors the tongue, shaped like an African ape's. In September 2026 a study of limb-bone strength, which records how bones were actually loaded in life, found that Little Foot and other australopiths carried the arm-to-leg strength proportions of climbing African apes, while the Dmanisi hominins at ~1.8 Mya were human-like: on that reading the unambiguous move to the ground came with early Homo, not with the first walkers.

Around Lucy stood others. A. bahrelghazali — "Abel", ~3.5 Mya from Koro Toro in Chad — put a hominin 2,500 km west of the Rift Valley, which should have ended the habit of treating East Africa as the whole story. Kenyanthropus platyops (KNM-WT 40000, ~3.5 Mya, west of Lake Turkana) has a flat face and small molars unlike anything else of its age; whether it is a genus, a species, or an afarensis cranium wrecked by matrix distortion is still argued. And in the same Woranso-Mille area that gave anamensis its face, A. deyiremeda (~3.6–3.3 Mya, named in 2015 from jaws found at Burtele) lived alongside Lucy's species. In 2025 its describers assigned it a 3.4-million-year-old foot found at Burtele in 2009 — a foot with a grasping big toe, from an animal that still climbed — and its tooth enamel records a diet of C₃ plants, closer to earlier hominins than to the mixed diet of afarensis. Not everyone accepts it as a separate species; if it stands, the Laetoli walker and the Burtele climber were near-neighbours, two australopiths partitioning one landscape. A. africanus (~3.3–2.1 Mya) is the southern African form, first recognised from the Taung Child in 1924; a 2022 cosmogenic redating of Sterkfontein's richest layer to ~3.4–3.5 Mya would make it a contemporary of Lucy rather than a successor, but faunal dating of the same deposits disagrees, and the question is open. Little Foot — StW 573 from Sterkfontein, dated ~3.67 Mya by cosmogenic burial dating (a ~2.2 Mya uranium-lead date on flowstone is argued to post-date the skeleton), chiselled out of concrete-hard breccia over twenty years by Ronald Clarke and the technicians Stephen Motsumi and Nkwane Molefe — is the most complete australopith skeleton known, arms and legs and skull together. Its species is as disputed as its age: Clarke assigns it to A. prometheus, and a 2025 study found it matches neither that name nor typical africanus. A. garhi (~2.5 Mya, Bouri) was found in a horizon with cut-marked antelope bone. A. sediba, recovered from Malapa in 2008 after Matthew Berger, aged nine, picked up a clavicle in a block of rock, is a mosaic: an australopith pelvis with a Homo-like hand and small teeth.

Paranthropus: the other way to be a hominin

And then there is the branch the popular story leaves out. Around 2.7 Mya a lineage appears with a completely different answer to the question of how to survive a drying, seasonal Africa. Not a bigger brain. A bigger chewing apparatus.

Paranthropus skulls are unmistakable. The molars are enormous — P. boisei's are up to four times the occlusal area of ours — with enamel so thick the teeth look like paving. The cheekbones flare forward to house a massive temporalis muscle, and in males the muscle is so large that the skull grows a sagittal crest along its midline simply to give it somewhere to attach, the same solution gorillas and lions arrived at independently. The face is dished, the jaw deep, the whole architecture organised around force.

Three species are generally recognised. P. aethiopicus is announced by KNM-WT 17000, the "Black Skull", found by Alan Walker west of Lake Turkana in 1985 and stained blue-black by manganese in its sediment: ~2.5 Mya, a brain of only ~410 cc, and already the full robust chewing complex. P. boisei is the East African form, and its type specimen is one of the most consequential fossils ever lifted: OH 5, found by Mary Leakey at Olduvai Gorge on 17 July 1959 after decades of the Leakeys' searching, nicknamed "Nutcracker Man" and originally given its own genus, Zinjanthropus. It was OH 5 that Louis Leakey used to argue for funding, and OH 5 that first anchored African hominins to an absolute radiometric date — 1.75 Mya, by potassium-argon, which at a stroke doubled the accepted depth of the human record. P. robustus is the southern form, from Swartkrans, Kromdraai and Drimolen, described by Robert Broom from 1938.

The old textbook line was that these were failed humans: an evolutionary cul-de-sac, over-specialised, outcompeted. That reading is backwards. Paranthropus was successful. The genus persisted for roughly 1.7–1.9 million years — considerably longer than Homo sapiens has so far managed — and for about a million of those years it lived in the same landscapes as early Homo, at Olduvai, at Koobi Fora, at Swartkrans, sometimes in the same stratigraphic layers. Isotope work on P. boisei enamel shows a diet dominated by C₄ plants — sedges, grasses, tubers — while contemporary Homo ate more mixed and more animal foods. Two hominins, one landscape, two economies. Dental microwear even suggests the giant molars were not usually crushing hard nuts at all but processing large volumes of tough low-quality vegetation, which is a different and less glamorous kind of specialisation.

Since 2023 that argument has gained new evidence almost every year. At Nyayanga on Kenya's Homa Peninsula, Oldowan tools bracketed between ~3.0 and 2.6 Mya — most likely ~2.9 Mya — lie beside butchered hippopotamus bones and two Paranthropus molars, the only hominin fossils found there. In 2024 a single lake-margin surface at Koobi Fora, ~1.5 Mya, was shown to carry the footprints of both P. boisei and H. erectus, each walking in its own way along the same shore. In 2025 came the first hand bones that can be tied unambiguously to P. boisei: KNM-ER 101000, hand and foot bones excavated together with teeth and jaw fragments at Koobi Fora in 2019–21, slightly older than 1.52 Mya, lying in silt directly on top of a hominin footprint surface. Its thumb is long relative to the palm, allowing human-like precision grips, while the rest of the hand converges on a gorilla's powerful grasp; its describers conclude that P. boisei was capable of making and using tools "in some capacity". In the south, enamel proteins from Swartkrans teeth (2025) have sexed individual P. robustus and hint at genetic variability, possibly more than one population. Louis Leakey's 1959 description of OH 5 called it the oldest yet discovered maker of stone tools; the claim passed to H. habilis in 1964, and it is back on the table.

Bone tools polished by digging have been recovered from Swartkrans and Drimolen alongside P. robustus, and the idea that some Oldowan stone tools were made by robust australopiths rather than by Homo is now a live possibility rather than a heresy. Whichever way that resolves, the lesson stands: for a million years, "being a hominin" had at least two viable business models. Ours was not obviously the better one. It just happens to be the one still trading.

Why it happened · a climate that would not sit still

  • The trigger. From about 2.8–2.5 Mya the northern ice sheets grew and East African climate turned drier on average and swung harder between wet and dry phases; soil carbonates and fossil fauna record grasses and other C₄ plants spreading at the expense of woodland.
  • Two answers to one problem. In a seasonal, patchy landscape a hominin can grind large amounts of tough, low-quality plant food (Paranthropus' teeth) or widen its diet with tools, marrow and meat (early Homo's kit). Both lineages are first securely known within a few hundred thousand years of that shift, which is why many researchers link them to it — as a steady drying (the aridity hypothesis) or as selection for flexibility itself (Richard Potts's "variability selection").
  • The caution. The fit is looser than the story. The Lomekwi tools are half a million years older than the shift, bipedalism millions of years older, and dating fossils and climate records to the same few thousand years is rarely possible. Climate set the stage; it does not explain which actors walked on.

Where these dates sit in deep time: the master timeline. The drying, swinging African climate that Paranthropus' teeth and Homo's tools were both answering is traced on the climate spine.

The many Homos

Our genus does not begin with a bang. It begins with a jaw. LD 350-1, half a mandible found in January 2013 by the Ethiopian graduate student Chalachew Seyoum at Ledi-Geraru in the Afar and dated to ~2.8 Mya, carries a mix of primitive and derived traits that put it, tentatively, at the base of Homo — about 400,000 years earlier than the previous oldest. It is a fragment, and the assignment is argued, which is a fair description of the whole first million years of our genus. In 2025 the same project reported more teeth: Homo at 2.78–2.59 Mya, and beside it Australopithecus teeth of ~2.6 Mya that match neither afarensis nor garhi — two non-robust lineages sharing the Afar before 2.5 Mya, while Paranthropus was already chewing its way through the Turkana Basin to the south.

H. habilis (~2.3–1.65 Mya), named by Louis Leakey, Phillip Tobias and John Napier in 1964 from OH 7 at Olduvai, was defined partly on the assumption that it made the tools found with it — hence "handy man". That assumption has since weakened at both ends: Oldowan tools are now known from 2.6 Mya at Gona and Ledi-Geraru — and possibly from ~2.9 Mya at Nyayanga in western Kenya, where they lie beside Paranthropus teeth and butchered hippopotamus — and the Lomekwian assemblage at Lomekwi 3 in Kenya pushes deliberate knapping to 3.3 Mya, half a million years before any Homo, if that date holds: most of the field accepts it, but some archaeologists argue that many of the artefacts came from surface or reworked contexts rather than the dated bed. H. rudolfensis (KNM-ER 1470, found by Bernard Ngeneo in 1972) has a bigger braincase and a broader flatter face; lumpers fold it into habilis, splitters keep it. Either way, from at least 2.4 Mya there were several kinds of Homo at once, sharing East Africa with two species of Paranthropus.

The body changes before the brain finishes. H. ergaster — or African H. erectus, depending on your taxonomy — is best known from KNM-WT 15000, the Turkana Boy, an almost complete adolescent skeleton found by Kamoya Kimeu at Nariokotome in 1984 and dated to ~1.6 Mya. Long legs, hips once read as narrow (the roomier Gona female pelvis has put that in doubt), a barrel chest, a striding runner's build, an estimated adult height of 1.6–1.8 m, depending on how fast he is assumed to have matured (the original 1.85 m projection has been challenged). This is essentially a modern human body carrying a brain of about 880 cc. A narrow thoracic spinal canal was long read as a sign that he lacked the fine breath control speech requires; a 2015 re-measurement found most of his vertebrae, and all of those from Dmanisi, within the modern human range, which leaves that question open. H. erectus then becomes the most durable human species there has ever been: out of Africa by ~1.8 Mya, in Java by ~1.5–1.3 Mya (the arrival date is argued), in China at Zhoukoudian, and possibly surviving on Java at Ngandong until ~110 kya. It ran for roughly two million years. We are 300,000 into ours.

At Dmanisi in Georgia, a medieval hilltop town sits on a 1.8-million-year-old land surface that has produced five hominin crania. Skull 5 (D4500), published by David Lordkipanidze's team in 2013, has the smallest braincase of the group (~546 cc) attached to a very large, projecting face — a combination no one predicted. The team's argument was blunt: these five individuals come from one place and one moment, so their variation is within-population variation; and it spans as much shape as separates habilis, rudolfensis and ergaster in the African record. If they are right, several African "species" are one variable species. The claim is not settled, and it is the single best illustration of the lumper–splitter problem in the whole discipline.

Dmanisi is not the first trace of hominins outside Africa, only the first with skulls. Stone tools lie in loess layers at Shangchen on China's Loess Plateau dated by palaeomagnetism to ~2.1 Mya; cut-marked animal bones at Grăunceanu in Romania are reported (2025) as older than 1.95 Mya; and if the 2026 burial date for Yunxian holds, H. erectus was in central China at ~1.77 Mya, as early as Dmanisi. Europe was entered from the east — stone tools at Korolevo in western Ukraine were dated in 2024 to ~1.4 Mya — and patchily: the record north of the Pyrenees and the Alps flickers on and off, and many researchers read it as repeated arrivals and local extinctions rather than continuous settlement. At Happisburgh on the Norfolk coast, footprints of a mixed group of adults and children, between about a million and 780,000 years old, crossed an estuary mudflat in a Britain that has yielded no hominin bone of that age.

In Europe, H. antecessor from Gran Dolina at Atapuerca (~0.9–0.8 Mya) was for a decade the oldest well-dated western European hominin. Older material has since come from the neighbouring Sima del Elefante — a 1.2 Mya mandible assigned only to Homo sp., and in 2025 a partial face, ATE7-1 "Pink", dated 1.4–1.1 Mya, provisionally called H. aff. erectus and lacking antecessor's modern-looking midface — and from a ~1.4 Mya tooth at Orce. Antecessor's own bones carry cut marks and percussion damage identical to those on the butchered deer beside them — among the earliest evidence of cannibalism in the human record. A few hundred metres away, at the bottom of a 13-metre vertical shaft called the Sima de los Huesos, lie the remains of at least 28 individuals dated to ~430 kya, accompanied by a single beautifully worked quartzite handaxe and no other tools or living debris. One of them, Cranium 17, carries two depression fractures on the forehead, alike in shape and size but struck from different directions — two blows, probably with the same object, either of which was likely lethal. Its 2015 describers call it the earliest evidence of lethal violence between hominins, and read it as support for the view that the bodies were placed in the shaft by other people. Nuclear DNA recovered from those bones in 2016 showed them to be early Neanderthals, not H. heidelbergensis — which is one reason heidelbergensis, long drawn as the common ancestor of Neanderthals, Denisovans and us, is now treated by many researchers as a wastebasket grade rather than a species.

Africa had its own archaics, and they were not all ancestors. Fossils from the Grotte à Hominidés at Thomas Quarry in Casablanca, published in January 2026 and dated ~773,000 years ago, are as old as antecessor but built differently, mixing primitive traits with derived features reminiscent of later H. sapiens and of Eurasian archaic humans; their describers place them near the root of the lineage that became H. sapiens — an African candidate for the ancestral population that Atapuerca was once thought to hold. And the Kabwe skull, found by a miner at Broken Hill in Zambia in 1921 and long thought to be far older, was redated in 2020 to ~299,000 years: a heavy-browed human with a ~1,280 cc brain, filed under H. rhodesiensis or heidelbergensis, alive at almost the same moment as the earliest sapiens at Jebel Irhoud.

Four species that sit off that line

  • Paranthropus boisei, ~450–545 cc at 1.8 Mya — flat on the floor of the chart while contemporary Homo is well above it, and thriving anyway for a million more years.
  • Homo naledi, ~465–610 cc at 335–236 kya — an orange-sized brain in a hominin alive at the same moment as the earliest Homo sapiens. Not an ancestor lingering; a contemporary.
  • Homo floresiensis, ~426 cc until ~50 kya — smaller than Lucy's, in a hominin that made stone tools and butchered Stegodon.
  • Neanderthals, ~1,400–1,600 cc — above the modern human mean of ~1,350. And note the last bar: modern brains are about 10% smaller than those of Upper Palaeolithic humans. Volume is not a scoreboard.

Those last two entries deserve their own paragraph, because they broke the story open. H. naledi was recovered from the Dinaledi chamber of the Rising Star system near Johannesburg, reached through an 18-centimetre squeeze that required an excavation team recruited specifically for being small enough to fit — six women who became known as the "underground astronauts". More than 1,500 fossil elements from at least 15 individuals came out in 2013–14. The anatomy is a mosaic: curved climbing fingers, a Homo-like foot, a hip like an australopith's, and a brain around 500 cc. The killer was the date, published in 2017: ~335–236 kya. Not 2 million years old. Contemporary with the Jebel Irhoud sapiens. How so many bodies reached a chamber deep in the dark remains contested. Deliberate disposal has been the excavators' claim since 2015; in 2023 they went further, arguing for dug graves and engraved wall markings in unreviewed preprints released a month before a Netflix documentary about the work, and pointing to hearths, charcoal and soot-blackened walls as signs of fire used for light — evidence first announced in a public lecture in December 2022 and not yet published as a study of its own. The burial paper became a version of record in eLife in September 2025 with its reviewers still split — one judging the evidence convincing, another incomplete — and a 2025 reanalysis of the sediment chemistry in PaleoAnthropology by Kimberly Foecke, Alain Queffelec and Robyn Pickering could not replicate the result the burial case leans on. The engraving and fire claims remain more disputed still. Then, in June 2026, enamel proteins from at least 20 naledi individuals, analysed by a team that included Berger, yielded no confident male marker in any of them: either the chamber holds no males at all, which would bear directly on how the bodies got there, or the method has a blind spot the authors believe they have ruled out.

H. floresiensis was excavated at Liang Bua on Flores in 2003: an adult female (LB1) about 1.06 m tall with a ~426 cc brain, wrist bones of archaic form, and stone tools around her. Initial claims that she was a pathological modern human have not survived the recovery of further individuals. The likeliest explanation is insular dwarfism — the same island rule that shrank Flores's elephants (Stegodon) and enlarged its rats and storks. What makes it stranger is Mata Menge, 70 km away, where hominin teeth and a jaw fragment dated to ~700 kya are already small, and an adult upper-arm bone described in 2024 is smaller even than LB1's: the island body plan was in place 700,000 years ago. Stone tools at nearby Wolo Sege reach back ~1 Mya. Flores has never been connected to the Asian mainland. An erectus-grade hominin crossed deep water past the Wallace Line — the biogeographic boundary that stops most Asian animals dead — hundreds of thousands of years before any modern human built a boat. H. luzonensis, described in 2019 from teeth and curved foot and hand bones in Callao Cave on Luzon and dated to ~67–50 kya, says the same thing again, independently — and a rhinoceros butchered with stone tools at Kalinga, ~709 kya, shows hominins on Luzon hundreds of millennia before them. Sulawesi makes three: stone tools at Calio, published in 2025, are at least 1.04 million years old, though no bones of their makers have been found.

Two more recent entries fill in Asia. The Harbin cranium — hidden in a well in 1933 by a labourer who did not want it taken by the occupying Japanese administration, and surrendered by his family only in 2018 — is a huge, heavy-browed skull with a ~1,420 cc braincase, dated to at least 146 kya and named Homo longi, "Dragon Man", in 2021. In June 2025 two studies settled what it was: 95 ancient proteins recovered from the cranium include three amino-acid variants otherwise known only from Denisovans; and although no DNA survived in a tooth or the petrous bone, mitochondrial DNA from the calcified plaque on its teeth falls within Denisovan variation, closest to a branch carried by early Denisovans at Denisova Cave. The Harbin cranium is a Denisovan — the first near-complete Denisovan skull — which makes Homo longi the leading candidate for the Denisovans' formal species name, though H. juluensis, proposed in 2024 for a wider group of Chinese fossils, is a rival. And at Nesher Ramla in Israel, a parietal and mandible dated ~140–120 kya describe a late archaic population with Neanderthal-like teeth and more primitive braincase — living, tool-sharing and quite possibly interbreeding alongside Homo sapiens in the Levant. Burials with ochre at Tinshemet Cave, also in Israel and ~100,000 years old, sharpen the point: their 2025 excavators argue that the region's sapiens and Neanderthal-like people shared one way of making tools, one way of hunting large game and one way of burying their dead.

That leaves the deepest open question on this page: how old is the Asian branch? In 2025 a Chinese and British team digitally restored the crushed Yunxian 2 skull from Hubei, found in 1990 and then thought to be about a million years old, and their morphometric and phylogenetic analyses placed it as an early member of the H. longi clade — the Denisovan branch — implying that the lines leading to us and to the Denisovans already had deep roots before the Middle Pleistocene began, some 774,000 years ago. Two results pull the other way. Genetic estimates put the last common ancestor of sapiens, Neanderthals and Denisovans at roughly 765,000–550,000 years ago. And in February 2026 cosmogenic burial dating of the Yunxian gravels returned ~1.77 million years, under a title that calls the skulls Homo erectus. If that date holds, a place on the Denisovan branch becomes very hard to square with the genetic clock. The claim is contested, and it is the kind of result this page expects to rewrite.

Neanderthals, reconsidered

No extinct animal has been more thoroughly libelled. The brute of the popular imagination — stooped, grunting, incapable of abstraction — descends in a straight line from Marcellin Boule's 1911–13 reconstruction of the "Old Man of La Chapelle-aux-Saints", an individual who happened to be severely arthritic and whom Boule reconstructed with bent knees, a slouching neck and a divergent toe. Boule's skeleton was wrong. The adjective outlived the correction by a century.

Start with the hardest single fact. In 1990 a caver squeezed through a blocked passage in Bruniquel Cave in southwest France and, 336 metres from the entrance — far beyond any daylight — found two rings and four smaller heaps built from more than 400 pieces of stalagmite, snapped to length and arranged deliberately, the largest ring about 6.7 m across, with traces of fire on them. Uranium-thorium dating of the calcite that regrew over the broken ends returned ~176,500 years. No Homo sapiens is securely attested in Europe for another 130,000 years; the one earlier candidate, the contested ~210,000-year-old Apidima 1 skull in southern Greece, lies some 1,900 km away. Someone organised a group, carried fire deep underground, quarried two tonnes of speleothem and built something. We do not know what it was for. We know exactly who built it, and when.

The rest accumulates. At Shanidar Cave in Iraqi Kurdistan, Ralph Solecki excavated ten Neanderthals in the 1950s–60s, including Shanidar 1, who survived a crushing blow that blinded one eye, withered an arm and left him deaf — injuries he lived with for years, which is an argument about care. Solecki's "flower burial" of Shanidar 4, based on clumped pollen, is now widely attributed to burrowing bees and rodents; but renewed excavation from 2018 recovered Shanidar Z, an articulated upper body lying in a shallow cut behind a distinctive vertical rock, in a spot where bodies had been placed repeatedly. The pollen was probably wrong. The burial was not.

Care, and kinship, now have harder evidence too. At Cova Negra in Valencia, the inner-ear bones of a Neanderthal child show a congenital malformation associated with Down syndrome; the child lived to at least six, which its 2024 describers argue required others to help the mother for years. And in the Altai, genomes from eleven Neanderthals in Chagyrskaya Cave and two in nearby Okladnikov (2022) include a father and his teenage daughter. Their mitochondrial DNA is far more varied than their Y chromosomes — by an order of magnitude — the signature of small communities in which women, not men, moved between groups. It is the first social fact we have for a Neanderthal community, and it came out of a sequencer.

At Krapina in Croatia, ~130 kya, eight white-tailed eagle talons from at least three birds carry cut marks, notches and a polish consistent with mounting and wearing — an ornament made from the least edible part of a large raptor, 80,000 years before modern humans reached Europe. At Cueva de los Aviones in Spain, ~115 kya, perforated Glycymeris and Spondylus shells were found with lumps of natural pigment and residues of mixed red and yellow pigment with reflective mineral flakes — cosmetics, in a container; a pigment-painted scallop shell from Cueva Antón, also in Murcia, dated by its layer to ~37 kya, suggests the habit persisted almost to the end (that late date is itself part of the argument over how long Iberia's Neanderthals lasted).

Then the chemistry. Neanderthals hafted stone points with birch-bark tar, recovered as lumps — one bearing a fingerprint and the imprints of a flint flake and wood — from Königsaue in Germany (~80 kya; radiocarbon gives only a minimum) and, older still, on flakes from Campitello in Italy (~200 kya). Making it is not a matter of collecting sap. Birch bark must be heated to roughly 340–400 °C. Experiments in 2019 showed that a crude tar condenses on a stone set beside burning bark in the open — but chemical analysis of the Königsaue lumps in 2023 shows they were not made that way. They were distilled underground, in a setting built to starve the heat of oxygen, a process that cannot be watched while it runs: a multi-step transformation with no useful intermediate, chemistry done deliberately, in the Middle Palaeolithic. The same population managed fire well enough to render adhesives, and their diet, read from nitrogen isotopes in bone collagen and from starch grains and plant fragments trapped in dental calculus, was heavily meat-based in the north but included grasses, tubers, pine nuts, mushrooms and cooked legumes — and, at El Sidrón, yarrow and chamomile with no nutritional value and a bitter taste, plus Penicillium-bearing mouldy plant matter in an individual with a dental abscess.

The most contested claim is the oldest art. Uranium-thorium dates on carbonate crusts over painted marks at three Spanish caves — a red ladder-shape at La Pasiega, a hand stencil at Maltravieso, a curtain of speleothem daubed at Ardales — returned minimum ages beyond 64,000 years, which is at least 20,000 years before Homo sapiens arrived in Iberia. The method has been strenuously criticised: carbonate is an open system, uranium can migrate, and the sampled crusts may not be contemporaneous with the pigment beneath. The claim is not accepted, and it is not dead. It belongs on this page precisely as an unresolved case. Less disputed marks exist: finger flutings on the wall of La Roche-Cotard in the Loire valley were sealed by sediment at least ~57,000 years ago, when only Neanderthals are known in the region; and a giant-deer toe bone from Einhornhöhle in Germany, engraved with stacked chevrons, is directly radiocarbon-dated to at least 51,000 years ago, before sapiens is known in Central Europe. And the Spanish dates are no longer the oldest minimum ages for any cave art: in January 2026 a hand stencil at Liang Metanduno on Muna, off Sulawesi, was dated to at least 67,800 years — presumably by Homo sapiens on the road to Sahul, though no bones say who. Sulawesi also holds the oldest known figurative scene: three human-like figures around a pig at Leang Karampuang, at least 51,200 years old (2024).

And the number the rest of this site has printed without comment: Neanderthal cranial capacity averages about 1,400–1,600 cc, against a modern human mean near 1,350 cc. They had bigger brains than we do. Some of that difference is body size — they were shorter, heavier, more muscular, built for cold — and some is a genuinely different internal organisation, with proportionally larger occipital (visual) regions and different cerebellar proportions. But the plain statement should be made once, clearly: brain volume is not a ranking. It correlates poorly with cognitive ability within our own species, it scales with body mass across species, and a hominin with a third of our brain volume (H. naledi) was alive at the same moment as the first of us.

Could they talk? Four lines of evidence, and their limit

  • The hyoid. This small bone anchors the tongue and larynx. Selam's, at 3.3 Mya, is shaped like an African ape's. The Kebara 2 Neanderthal's (Israel, ~60 kya, published 1989) and those from the Sima de los Huesos are, in shape, essentially like ours.
  • The ear. Models built from CT scans of the outer and middle ear (2021) give Neanderthals a band of best-heard frequencies wider than the Sima de los Huesos people's and similar to living humans' — the band that carries speech.
  • The spine. A narrow thoracic spinal canal was once read as too little nerve supply for the breath control speech needs; the 2015 re-measurement found H. erectus had a canal of modern size by ~1.8 Mya (see the Turkana Boy) — though the same authors doubt that canal size says anything about speech at all.
  • The gene. Neanderthals carry the same two amino-acid changes in the protein FOXP2 that separate our version from a chimpanzee's — the gene whose mutation causes a severe speech disorder in one modern family.
  • The limit. All of this is equipment, not use. Soft tissue, brain wiring and words do not fossilise, and no bone can tell a language from a rich call system. The anatomy points towards some form of speech in Neanderthals; what it sounded like, or what it could say, is beyond the evidence.

So why are they gone? The last securely dated Neanderthals are around 40,000 years ago, at sites from Belgium (Spy) to the Caucasus (Mezmaiskaya); Croatia's Vindija, long cited as the latest, was redated to more than 44,000 years in 2017, and the very late Iberian claims rest on artefacts rather than directly dated bone. The overlap with incomers was real: bone fragments from Ranis (Ilsenhöhle) in central Germany, identified as human by their proteins and DNA, show Homo sapiens north of the Alps ~47,000–45,000 years ago, thousands of years before the last Neanderthals disappeared further west — and their genomes (2024) reveal a small, isolated population linked by distant family ties to a woman whose skull was found at Zlatý kůň in Czechia, which left no known descendants. Early contact did not mean early victory. There is no consensus, and the honest position lists the candidates rather than picking one. Demographic swamping: modelling suggests even a small, sustained influx of sapiens would absorb a population as small as theirs — total Neanderthal effective population size may have been only a few thousand breeding individuals across all of Eurasia. The Altai genomes show small, isolated and inbred groups; but 27 late Neanderthals from Belgium and France, sequenced in 2026, show no close inbreeding and no genes from the incoming sapiens, so the last western communities were larger and better connected than that. Mitochondrial DNA published the same year adds a timeline: nearly all of Europe's late Neanderthals descend from one female line that spread from a refuge in southwestern France around 65,000 years ago, and its effective population size fell sharply from ~45,000 years ago to a low near 42,000 — just before the end. Climate volatility: the Heinrich stadials around 44–40 kya swung European conditions violently, and a species with a narrower dietary and technological range fares worse under whiplash. Absorption: introgression means they did not entirely die — a real fraction of their genome is sitting in you. Disease: two populations separated for 500,000 years, meeting, exchange pathogens as well as genes, and the smaller population loses that exchange. Marginal competitive difference: no dramatic cognitive gulf required, just slightly larger networks, slightly better long-distance exchange, slightly higher fertility, compounded over 5,000 years. The plausible answer is most of these at once.

A people named from a genome

In 2008, a Russian team working in Denisova Cave in the Altai Mountains of southern Siberia recovered the tip of a juvenile finger bone from a layer later dated to roughly 76,000–52,000 years ago. It was unremarkable. Nobody could have said which hominin it belonged to by looking at it. Two years later, Svante Pääbo's laboratory in Leipzig sequenced its mitochondrial genome and then its nuclear genome — and the result matched neither Homo sapiens nor Neanderthals. It was a distinct population, sister to Neanderthals, diverged from them perhaps 400,000 years ago.

This is the inversion at the heart of modern palaeoanthropology. For 150 years, a new kind of human meant a skull: you described the morphology, you named the species after the valley, you argued about the brow ridges. The Denisovans were named from a molecule. They still have no agreed formal species name, and until 2025 they had no face: the physical material long amounted to a finger bone, a handful of teeth, some skull fragments and a mandible found 2,000 km away. Then proteins added a rib from the same Tibetan cave (2024) and a jaw dredged from the floor of the Taiwan Strait (2025), and proteins together with dental-calculus DNA claimed the near-complete Harbin cranium (2025) — making Homo longi the leading candidate for their name. Yet long before the face, we knew their population history, their divergence dates, their interbreeding, and a list of the genes they left in living people. The evidence arrived in exactly the reverse of the traditional order.

Denisova Cave keeps producing. Denisova 11 — "Denny" — is a 2.5 cm long bone splinter, identified in 2016 by collagen peptide fingerprinting (ZooMS) as hominin among thousands of unidentifiable fragments, then sequenced in 2018. The genome showed something nobody expected to catch: roughly half Neanderthal and half Denisovan, distributed across every chromosome in the pattern produced by a first-generation hybrid. Her mother was a Neanderthal; her father was a Denisovan whose own ancestry included a Neanderthal several hundred generations back. A single teenage girl, dead around 90,000 years ago, demonstrating that these populations met and had children — not as a statistical inference from modern genomes but directly, in one body.

The other landmark is the Xiahe mandible. A monk found it in Baishiya Karst Cave on the Tibetan Plateau at 3,280 m in 1980 and gave it to the sixth Gung-Thang Living Buddha, who passed it to Lanzhou University; it sat unstudied for decades. Published in 2019, it is dated to at least 160,000 years by U-series on its carbonate crust — and it contained no usable DNA at all. It was identified as Denisovan by palaeoproteomics: ancient collagen peptides survive far longer than DNA, and a single diagnostic amino-acid position in the collagen sequence placed it with the Denisovan lineage. Sediment DNA from the same cave later confirmed Denisovan occupation across tens of thousands of years. Proteins reached where DNA could not — which now matters for every warm-climate site where DNA is hopeless. In 2024 proteins identified a Denisovan rib from the same cave, dated ~48,000–32,000 years ago, among butchered animal bones — the youngest Denisovan fossil known. In 2025 they identified Penghu 1, a jaw brought up in fishing nets from the Penghu Channel off Taiwan, as a male Denisovan, of uncertain age. And in October 2025 researchers in Leipzig posted, as a preprint not yet peer-reviewed, a second high-quality Denisovan genome, from the molar of a man who lived at Denisova Cave some 200,000 years ago. His small group had mixed with early Neanderthals and was later replaced by Denisovans who had mixed with later ones; the genome also confirms gene flow into Denisovans from a still more ancient hominin lineage; and read against living people, the two genomes point to at least three distinct Denisovan groups contributing to modern ancestry.

What the introgression does is the part that makes this more than a curiosity. The Xiahe mandible is on the Tibetan Plateau, and Tibetans carry a version of the gene EPAS1 — a transcription factor governing the body's response to low oxygen — that is Denisovan in origin and is found at ~80–90% frequency in Tibetans and almost no one else. Lowlanders at altitude respond to thin air by manufacturing more red blood cells, which thickens the blood and causes chronic mountain sickness, pre-eclampsia and infant mortality. The Denisovan EPAS1 haplotype blunts that response: Tibetans live at 4,000 m without the polycythaemia. A population last seen in the fossil record tens of thousands of years ago solved a physiological problem, and the solution is still in daily use by millions of people. Elsewhere the legacy is immunological — Denisovan-derived variants in the TNFAIP3 and HLA regions tune inflammatory response, the TNFAIP3 variant reaching high frequency in populations east of the Wallace Line: Papuans, Aboriginal Australians and eastern Island Southeast Asians; a 2026 study of 177 genomes from Near Oceania, among the least-sampled people in human genetics, recovered some 830 million base pairs of Denisovan sequence still carried by living people and found the archaic variants that alter gene activity enriched in interferon-γ immune signalling — and metabolic, including lipid-processing variants in Greenlandic Inuit. One variant took a longer road. A Denisovan-like version of the mucin gene MUC19 passed into Neanderthals and then, through them, into modern humans; a 2025 study found it at its highest frequency in ancient Indigenous American genomes that predate any European or African admixture, and under positive selection.

The shares are uneven and geographically sharp. Papuans and Aboriginal Australians carry roughly 3–5% Denisovan ancestry — and the Ayta Magbukon of Luzon, the island of H. luzonensis, carry some 30–40% more than that, the highest share known (2021). East and South Asians carry ~0.2%, and it comes from at least two genetically distinct Denisovan populations, meaning the group was diverse and widespread across Asia for a very long time. Neanderthal ancestry, by contrast, is nearly uniform: about 1.8–2.6% in every population outside Africa (1.5–2.1% on the first, Altai, genome). A 2024 study of more than 300 ancient and present-day genomes traces almost all of it to one shared, extended episode of gene flow between about 50,500 and 43,500 years ago — centred near 47,000 years ago, probably somewhere in Southwest Asia — and the oldest European sapiens genomes, from Ranis and Zlatý kůň, independently put it at ~49,000–45,000 years ago. Africans carry ~0.3%, from Eurasians migrating back into Africa.

What "2% Neanderthal" actually means

  • It is not 2% of a person. You are not 98% one thing and 2% another in any anatomical sense. The figure is the fraction of your genome — of the three billion base pairs — whose ancestry traces back to a Neanderthal rather than to an African sapiens population.
  • Different people carry different 2%. The segments are scattered and largely non-overlapping between individuals. Summing across many living people recovers a large share of the Neanderthal genome — current estimates put it around 20–40% of it, still present, distributed across humanity. They were absorbed, not merely replaced.
  • Some regions are empty. Large stretches of the genome — especially the X chromosome and regions expressed in the testes — carry essentially no Neanderthal ancestry, which implies selection removed it. The hybrids were viable but likely had reduced male fertility. A 2026 study offers a second, compatible cause. Neanderthal X chromosomes carry a 62% relative excess of the sapiens ancestry they picked up in earlier encounters, which its authors read as pairings mostly between Neanderthal men and sapiens women, driven by mate preference. If the later mixing followed the same pattern, that alone would thin Neanderthal ancestry on our X.
  • It is doing work. Introgressed variants affect keratin, immune signalling, skin and hair, fat metabolism, blood clotting, and susceptibility to severe COVID-19 — a Neanderthal haplotype on chromosome 3 raises risk, another on chromosome 12 lowers it.

What they taught us about being human

Extinct relatives are not a gallery of near-misses. They are the only comparison group our species has ever had. Every claim about what makes humans human is a claim about a difference, and a difference needs something to differ from. Here is what the bush has actually established.

1 · Walking came first, by millions of years. The Laetoli footprints are 3.66 Mya and were made by an animal with a 450 cc brain. The brain does not begin its serious expansion until after 2 Mya. The gap between "upright" and "encephalised" is longer than the entire history of the genus Homo. Whatever bipedalism was for, it was not for carrying a big brain — the big brain came later, and may partly have been affordable because of what free hands and an efficient gait made possible. Nor did walking end climbing: on the evidence of Selam's shoulder blades and of australopith limb-bone strength (2026), the first walkers still spent real time in trees, and the full commitment to the ground came with early Homo.

2 · Technology is older than our genus. The Lomekwi 3 assemblage in Kenya dates deliberate stone knapping to 3.3 Mya — 500,000 years before the oldest fossil of Homo — if the date holds: most of the field accepts it, but some archaeologists argue that many of the artefacts came from surface or reworked contexts rather than the dated bed. Whoever made those cores was an australopith or a Kenyanthropus; and the ~2.9 Mya Oldowan at Nyayanga lies beside Paranthropus, not Homo. Toolmaking is not the threshold of humanity; it is something our ancestors were already doing when they still had ape-sized brains, and something chimpanzees do today. "Man the toolmaker" died at Gombe in 1960 and was buried at Lomekwi in 2015.

3 · Brain size is not a ranking. Neanderthals averaged 1,400–1,600 cc against our 1,350, and made pigment, ornament, adhesive and architecture. H. naledi managed roughly 500 cc and was alive alongside the earliest Homo sapiens. H. floresiensis made tools and hunted with a brain smaller than Lucy's. Modern human brains have shrunk ~10% since the Late Pleistocene. Volume is one crude proxy for one part of a question about organisation, connectivity, development and culture.

4 · There was more than one way to be a hominin. Paranthropus answered the same environmental pressure with teeth instead of tools and ran for some 1.7–1.9 million years. Island lineages shrank. Neanderthals built cold-adapted bodies and a technology of care and chemistry. None of these were failed attempts at us. They were solutions, and most of them lasted longer than we have.

5 · "Human" has no clean boundary. The biological species concept requires reproductive isolation, and these lineages were not isolated. Neanderthals, Denisovans, sapiens and at least one unidentified archaic population exchanged genes. Denny had one parent from each of two "species". A category that leaks like that is a convenience for filing fossils, not a fact about the world — and it means the boundary of our own species is a smear, not a line.

6 · Every branch but one is a dead end — including, so far, all the smart ones. Large brains, fire, burial, pigment and complex tools did not save the Neanderthals, and did not save H. erectus after two million years. There is no evidence that intelligence is a guarantee of persistence. The fossil record is a very long list of capable hominins who are not here.

7 · Our solitude is the anomaly. This is the one worth sitting with. For all but the last few tens of millennia of the past four million years — more than 99% of that span — the planet carried more than one kind of human simultaneously, often three or four at once and at times likely more. Sharing the world with other people who were not your people was the normal human condition, and it ended only around 40,000–30,000 years ago: the last Neanderthals date to ~40 kya, the youngest known Denisovan fossil (a Baishiya Karst Cave rib) to ~48–32 kya, and the Flores and Luzon hominins are last attested around 50 kya. It may have ended later still — one genetic study (2019) puts the latest Denisovan gene flow east of the Wallace Line at ~30,000 years ago, and possibly nearer the end of the Pleistocene — but no bone yet says so. We have been alone for about a hundredth as long as we had company — roughly 1% of the last four million years, and less than 1% of the family's history. Every intuition we have about human uniqueness was formed during that unusual 1%.

How the story was assembled

None of the above was obvious. The record was assembled over 170 years by people working with the wrong expectations, in the wrong places, under political conditions that shaped what they were willing to see. The history of the discovery is part of the evidence.

The first Neanderthal recognised as such came out of a limestone quarry in the Neander Valley near Düsseldorf in 1856 — and was very nearly discarded; the anatomist Rudolf Virchow pronounced it a pathological modern human. In 1891 the Dutch army physician Eugène Dubois, who had deliberately enlisted for the East Indies to look for the missing link and who was working with convict labour on the Solo River at Trinil, found a skullcap and a femur and named Pithecanthropus erectus — "Java Man", now H. erectus. He was ridiculed, and eventually locked the fossils away.

The most instructive failure is the Taung Child. In 1924 a fossil-bearing block from a lime quarry in the North West Province of South Africa reached Raymond Dart at the University of the Witwatersrand. He spent 73 days chipping it out — partly with his wife's knitting needles — and found the face and natural endocast of a young hominin with a small brain, human-like teeth, and a forward foramen magnum indicating upright posture. He published in Nature in February 1925 as Australopithecus africanus. He was rejected for decades. Two reasons: the fossil was African, and the establishment expected humanity's cradle in Europe or Asia; and it was small-brained with human teeth, whereas the prevailing model said the brain enlarged first. That model had powerful physical evidence behind it — Piltdown Man, "found" in Sussex in 1912, a large modern human braincase with an ape's jaw, exactly the big-brained European ancestor English science was hoping for. Piltdown was a forgery. It was not exposed until 1953, by Kenneth Oakley, Joseph Weiner and Wilfrid Le Gros Clark, using fluorine dating: the jaw was an orangutan's, filed and stained. Dart had been right since 1924, and a fraud that flattered its audience cost the discipline thirty years.

Then East Africa. Louis and Mary Leakey worked Olduvai Gorge for nearly three decades on almost nothing before Mary found OH 5 in 1959; the potassium-argon date that followed reset the timescale of the whole field. Kamoya Kimeu, who led the "hominid gang" of Kenyan fossil hunters and found the Turkana Boy in 1984, is the reason a large fraction of the East African record exists at all. In 1974 Donald Johanson and Tom Gray found Lucy. In 1994 Tim White's team described Ardipithecus ramidus. In 2003 Liang Bua yielded LB1. In 2010 a finger bone from Denisova produced a new population out of a sequencer. In 2013 Rising Star produced H. naledi. In 2019, H. luzonensis and the MRD cranium arrived in the same year. In 2022 Svante Pääbo received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for sequencing the Neanderthal genome and discovering the Denisovans — the first Nobel awarded, in effect, for palaeoanthropology. The pace has not slackened since. In 2025 ancient proteins and DNA from dental plaque turned the Harbin skull into the first Denisovan face, a partial face from Sima del Elefante became western Europe's oldest, and hand bones were finally tied to Paranthropus boisei; in 2026 a new date for Yunxian reopened the age of the whole Asian branch, fossils from a Casablanca quarry offered an African candidate for the root of our own lineage, and enamel proteins found no sign of a male among at least twenty H. naledi.

Two darker threads run through all of it. The first is loss. The Zhoukoudian "Peking Man" fossils — dozens of specimens excavated from 1921 and studied by Franz Weidenreich and the Chinese geologist Pei Wenzhong, who found the first complete skullcap in 1929 — were packed for shipment to the United States for safekeeping in 1941 and vanished in transit as the Pacific war began. They have never been recovered. What survives are Weidenreich's casts and drawings, which is why his meticulous documentation is itself a landmark in the field.

The second is the discipline's own colonial history. Fossils and human remains were removed from colonised territories under legal regimes the source communities did not write, and many sit in European and North American institutions today. Named "discoveries" routinely credit the funded foreigner rather than the local finder; Kamoya Kimeu is now honoured, but for decades the pattern was invisibility. Human remains were collected as racial-typological specimens well into the twentieth century — a history that reaches from the trafficking of Indigenous ancestors' bodies to the treatment of San and Khoekhoe individuals in South African collections. Hominin fossils are part of the same argument. The Kabwe skull was shipped to London in the year it was found, 1921, and is still there: Zambia formally asked how it had been acquired in 1974, many states spoke for its return at UNESCO's committee on returning cultural property in 2018, and in 2024 Zambia reported no positive outcome from its talks with Britain. The response is not merely apology. It is repatriation — of ancestral remains to Aboriginal Australian, Native American, Māori and southern African communities — and Indigenous data sovereignty, the principle that communities have authority over genetic and cultural data derived from their ancestors, including the right to refuse sampling. Ancient DNA is destructive: every sequence costs a piece of a body that belongs to someone's forebears. Consent frameworks now developed with First Nations, Māori, and Native American partners are part of the method, not a courtesy attached to it.

Where the dates in this page come from

Method · potassium-argon, U-series, ESR, OSL, palaeomagnetism

East African hominins are mostly dated by ⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar on volcanic tuffs bracketing the fossil layer — which is why the Rift Valley record is so much better resolved than the South African cave record, where dating depends on U-Pb and palaeomagnetism of flowstones. Cave art and speleothem contexts use uranium-thorium; burnt flint and tooth enamel use thermoluminescence and electron spin resonance; sediment burial uses OSL and cosmogenic nuclides. Radiocarbon is useless beyond ~50,000 years, which is precisely where most of this page lives.

Why these dates keep moving

Caution · read every figure as provisional

H. naledi was assumed to be ~2 million years old until it was dated to ~335,000–236,000 years. The Schöningen spears moved from ~300,000 to ~200,000 years in 2025, and perhaps from one species to another; the Yunxian skulls moved from ~1 million to ~1.77 million years in 2026. H. floresiensis's youngest date was revised from ~12,000 to ~50,000 years when the Liang Bua stratigraphy was re-read. The anamensis–afarensis succession became an overlap on the strength of one cranium. Nothing on this page should be read as final; several claims here will be wrong within a decade, and that is the field working correctly.
