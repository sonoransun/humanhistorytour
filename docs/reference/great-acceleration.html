The Great Acceleration

For 99% of the human story the numbers barely move. Then, in the blink of the last two centuries, population, energy, wealth, carbon, and cities all turn vertical at once. This is the Anthropocene, told in its curves — population, energy, wealth, poverty, trade, health, carbon, cities and biomass — each with the data behind it, and the largest of them broken open region by region, because no global average happened to everyone at the same time.

Population: the hockey stick

For most of history there were only ever a few million humans alive at once — the line hugs the floor. It lifts a little after farming, dips with the Black Death, and then, after 1800, goes nearly vertical: 1 billion around 1800, 2 billion by 1927, 8 billion in 2022. About 117 billion Homo sapiens have ever been born, on the Population Reference Bureau's estimate; roughly 7% of them are alive right now.

Two things the 10,000 BCE baseline hides

  • We were not the only humans, and the chart starts after the others are gone. By 10,000 BCE Homo sapiens was the sole surviving hominin. For most of the preceding two million years there were several species alive at once: Homo erectus out to perhaps ~110,000 years ago in Java, Neanderthals to ~40,000, Denisovans to ~40,000 or later (a Denisovan rib from Baishiya Karst Cave on the Tibetan Plateau comes from a layer dated ~48–32 kya), H. floresiensis on Flores and H. luzonensis on Luzon to ~50,000. A count of sapiens alone is a count of the survivors — see the human family for who else was here, and what their disappearance actually teaches.
  • That "natural" starting line is already a human-altered world. Long before the first point on this chart, sapiens had spread across every habitable continent and the largest animals had gone with startling consistency shortly after arrival: Australia's Diprotodon, giant kangaroos and Genyornis by ~46–40 kya; the Americas' mammoths, mastodons, ground sloths, native horses and camels between ~15 and ~11 kya; Eurasia's woolly rhino by ~14 kya, and the cave bear earlier still, around 26–24 kya in the depths of the last glacial maximum. Climate and hunting are both implicated and the proportions are still genuinely argued over — see how we know · contested — but the biomass baseline every later chart is measured against had already lost most of its giants to us.

Why the curve is already bending

The hockey stick is no longer steepening. World population growth peaked at about 2.3% a year in 1963 and is now under 0.9%; the largest yearly additions, over 90 million people, came around 1990. The cause is the fertility transition: families, and above all women, choosing fewer children once most children survive and girls stay longer in school. The average woman worldwide had ~4.9 children in 1950 and ~2.25 in 2024; Europe is at ~1.4, South America ~1.7, India ~2.0, Africa as a whole still ~4, with South Korea at ~0.7 and Niger near 6. China's population peaked in 2021, and India overtook it as the most populous country around 2022–23. The UN's medium variant has humanity topping out at ~10.3 billion in 2084 and easing to ~10.2 billion by 2100 — a projection resting on assumed fertility paths, not a forecast of fate.

Energy through the ages

Every era is defined by what it burns and who does the work. A forager lived on about 5,000 kcal a day of energy — food, fire and muscle; the average American around 1970 commanded some 230,000 kcal, roughly forty-five times as much (Earl Cook's classic 1971 ladder). Today the high-income-country average is about 124,000 kcal a day and the world average about 48,000. The mix shifted too: almost all biomass (wood) in 1800, coal overtaking wood in the first decade of the 1900s, oil passing coal in the mid-1960s, and solar and wind the fastest-growing sources today — solar output rose by about 30% in 2025 alone. Fossil fuels still supply about 86% of the world's commercial primary energy.

The Great Enrichment

For thousands of years real income per person barely moved — any productivity gain was converted into more people, not richer ones. This is the Malthusian trap. After 1800 the line breaks free: world GDP per person rose roughly fifteenfold in two centuries, from about $1,100 in 1820 to about $16,700 in 2022 (in 2011 prices), the fastest and most widespread escape from poverty in the human record.

This single world line conceals the Great Divergence and the convergence that followed it — both are unpacked in the same curves, region by region.

Extreme poverty, counted in people

The income curve has a human translation. Before the nineteenth century most people lived at what we would now call extreme poverty. Reconstructions differ in method, but Michalis Moatsos's cost-of-basic-needs estimates (2021) put about three-quarters of humanity in 1820 — and still about half in 1950 — unable to afford minimal nutrition and adequately heated shelter at local prices. The World Bank's household-survey series is the measure for recent decades: in 1990, 2.3 billion people — 43% of humanity — lived on less than $3.00 a day (in 2021 prices); in 2024 it was about 820 million, one person in ten. The fall was overwhelmingly Asian. In East Asia and the Pacific the rate went from two-thirds of the population to under 2%; in South Asia from about half to a few per cent. Sub-Saharan Africa's rate fell too, from ~62% to ~44%, but its population grew faster, so the number of Africans in extreme poverty rose from about 320 million to about 580 million. Roughly seven in ten of the world's extremely poor now live there, and the global count has barely moved since 2019.

Trade: two globalisations and the collapse between them

For most of history long-distance trade moved luxuries, not livelihoods: even on generous bounds, exports plus imports were only ~2–9% of world output in 1820. Steamships, the telegraph and the gold standard lifted that to roughly a fifth to three-tenths of world GDP by 1913. Two world wars and the Depression then cut it by more than half by 1945, and merchandise exports did not regain their 1913 share of world output until the late 1970s. The second globalisation — the shipping container (from 1956), jet freight, the opening of China and India — carried trade to about 60% of world GDP by 2008. Since then it has plateaued rather than climbed: dips to ~52% in the slumps of 2009 and 2020, a price-inflated spike to the series high of ~62% in 2022, when energy and food prices soared, and ~57% in 2024. The silver and sugar trades that the tour's Chapter VII follows were world-changing, yet in these aggregates they barely register: their weight lay in what — and whom — they reorganised, not in their share of world output.

The same curves, region by region

Every chart above is a single global aggregate, and global aggregates hide the two largest stories in modern economic history. Humanity did not accelerate as one body. It accelerated in one corner first, at a speed that briefly made a temporary lead look like a permanent order, and since roughly 1990 the gap has been closing again — fast in East, Southeast and South Asia, slowly and unevenly in Latin America and Africa. Broken out by region, each hockey stick becomes a set of overlapping curves with different start dates, different steepness, and, in the case of carbon, two opposite answers depending on whether you ask about the flow or the stock. Will Steffen and colleagues, whose "Great Acceleration" graphs give this page its title, make the same point about their own dashboard: global aggregates, they wrote in 2015, mask strong equity issues.

Where people have actually lived

Asia has held somewhere between ~55% and ~67% of all living humans for essentially the whole of recorded history, and holds about 59% today. That is the baseline, and it barely moves — but inside it the weight has shifted south. South Asia (India, Pakistan, Bangladesh and their neighbours, ~2 billion people) has outnumbered East Asia since about 2005, and Southeast Asia, at ~700 million, holds more people than the European Union (~450 million). Europe's share was ~15% at 1 CE and ~18% in 1500; it peaked around 1900 at ~25% (counting Russia), on the back of the demographic transition arriving there first, and has fallen ever since — ~9% today, and a projected ~6% in 2100. The "rise of the West", read demographically, is a century-long bulge inside a two-thousand-year record. Latin America and the Caribbean rose from under 7% of humanity in 1950 to ~8% today; like Southeast Asia, its population is projected to peak in the 2050s and then shrink. Africa moved the other way from Europe: 8–12% for most of the last two millennia, held down for four centuries by the Atlantic and Indian Ocean slave trades and then by colonial-era mortality, it passed Europe in 1995, is ~18.5% today, and is projected to add about 2.3 billion people by 2100 — more than the whole world's net growth, because Europe is already shrinking and Asia and Latin America are projected to after mid-century. That makes roughly 37% of humanity by 2100, with the largest additions in the DR Congo, Nigeria, Ethiopia and Tanzania.

The Great Divergence — and the closing of it

Around 1700, China and India between them produced roughly half of everything made on Earth. That was not a golden-age claim about either; it followed from holding roughly half the people at broadly comparable output per person. On Maddison's reconstructions the richest large region in 1700 was only about two to three times the poorest — nothing like a civilisational chasm. How close the leaders were is itself argued over. On Kenneth Pomeranz's reading the lower Yangzi delta in 1750 looks about as commercialised and as long-lived as the most advanced parts of England; Broadberry, Guan and Li's 2018 national-accounts reconstruction instead finds China well behind north-west Europe by 1750, with even the delta already trailing Holland and England, so the size of the starting gap is disputed. The divergence opens after ~1800 and it opens fast: by 1913 a western European was ~5–6× a Chinese contemporary in real income and an American ~10×; the gap peaked at roughly 11–12× for western Europe around 1970 and at about 20× for the United States — in 1950, and again in 1961, when the famine of the Great Leap Forward gutted Chinese output (Maddison Project 2023). Whether the cause was coal and colonies (Pomeranz), institutions (North, Acemoglu), high wages inducing mechanisation (Allen), or the printing-press-and-tinkerer culture of "useful knowledge" (Mokyr) is one of the genuinely open arguments in the field — see how we know · contested.

Then it closes. By 1990 the ratios had narrowed to ~8.5× and ~12×; by 2022, to ~2× and ~3×. China's real output per person rose roughly tenfold between 1980 and 2022, the fastest large-scale escape from poverty ever recorded. The two-century story is therefore not "the West rose"; it is "the West rose first, and the ranking is reshuffling again inside a single lifetime." But convergence has not been one story, and four regions the world average flattens show how different the curves are:

  • South Asia. India's output per person has nearly quadrupled since 1990 and Bangladesh's roughly quintupled, carrying hundreds of millions of people over the World Bank's extreme-poverty line (see extreme poverty) — yet an Indian's income is still under half the world average.
  • Southeast Asia. Vietnam's income per head is about five times its 1990 level and Indonesia's about three times; together with Thailand, Malaysia and the Philippines the region now produces ~6% of world output at purchasing-power parity, up from ~4% in 1990.
  • Latin America. The reminder that convergence is not automatic. In 1950 the average Latin American was richer than the average Japanese; the region lost a decade to the debt crisis of the 1980s (income per head fell ~6% between 1980 and 1990), grew slowly after, and East Asia taken as a whole overtook it only in the early 2010s.
  • Sub-Saharan Africa. The gap that has not closed. Income per head was lower in 2000 than in 1980 after two lost decades of debt, structural adjustment, falling commodity prices and war; it has grown by about 70% since, but at ~$3,400 it is still under a tenth of the North American figure.

Energy: still more than an order of magnitude apart

Even the high-income average quoted above (~124,000 kcal a day) sits near the top of a distribution that spans a factor of fifty. A person in the United States or Canada uses roughly 75,000 kWh of commercial primary energy a year; the EU-and-UK and the Chinese averages are now almost identical, at ~31,000; Latin America and Southeast Asia use ~13,000–14,000, India ~7,400, and sub-Saharan Africa outside South Africa about 1,500. That spread is the best single predictor of almost everything else on this page: appliance ownership, cold chains for vaccines, industrial output, night-time light on satellite imagery. Two cautions make the African number less stark than it looks, not less real: it leaves out the firewood and charcoal on which much of rural Africa and South Asia still cooks, and the physical-energy-content method counts the region's large hydropower supply at roughly 40% of the weight the older method gave it. In 2024 some 655 million people still had no electricity at all, about 86% of them in sub-Saharan Africa.

Carbon: the flow and the stock are different questions

CO₂ persists for centuries, so warming tracks the cumulative total emitted, not this year's output. The two accounts point in opposite directions, and that is precisely why both belong here. On the flow, China is by far the largest emitter today — about 32% of fossil CO₂ in 2024, more than the United States and the European Union plus Britain combined. On the stock, the United States alone accounts for about 24% of everything emitted since 1750 with ~4% of the world's people, the EU and Britain for another ~21%, and China for ~15% and rising. The regions that hold most of humanity carry little of the stock: Africa is ~18.5% of humanity and ~3% of it, India ~18% and ~3.6%, Southeast Asia ~8.5% and ~2.5%, Latin America and the Caribbean ~8% and ~4% (more if deforestation is counted). Per person, someone in the United States or Canada emits about 14 times what the average African does, and about 40 times the average in sub-Saharan Africa outside South Africa.

Stated plainly: responsibility for the stock and responsibility for the current flow are different questions with different answers, and both answers are true. Any argument that quotes only one of them is choosing a conclusion first.

This is the numerate version of the tour's central claim — that history is a relay between centres, not a single ascent: see the relay, not the rise and the region-by-region record in parallel civilizations.

The long health transition

For nearly all of history, life expectancy at birth sat near 30. This is probably the most misread number in the whole subject, so it is worth being exact about what it means: it is an arithmetic average of all deaths, including infants, and it is dragged to 30 by the roughly one child in two who died before adulthood. It is not a statement that thirty-year-olds were old, and there was never a society in which people routinely dropped dead at 33.

Unpack the average and a different picture appears. In the ethnographically documented forager populations — the Hadza, Ache, !Kung and Tsimane, pooled by Gurven and Kaplan (2007) — someone who survived to 15 could expect roughly forty more years, and the modal adult age at death falls between about 68 and 78. Grandparents were normal; the Paleolithic had elders, and the transmission of knowledge across three living generations is one of the things that makes our species work. What changed after 1900 was not a new human lifespan but the near-elimination of dying young: clean water, germ theory, vaccines and antibiotics cut under-five mortality from ~40% to ~3.7% in 2024. That collapse, not longer old age, is the true engine behind the population hockey stick. It is also unfinished: in 2024 about one African child in fifteen still died before the age of five (~6.6%), and about one in nine in Nigeria and Niger, against fewer than one in two hundred in Europe.

This and the other numbers people routinely misread are unpacked in how we know · misconceptions.

How we know

Modern child mortality comes from the UN's IGME, which reconciles vital registration, censuses and hundreds of household surveys that ask mothers about every child they have borne; where registration is weak, as across much of sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, the estimate is a model with an uncertainty band. The pre-1950 world figures are reconstructions stitched from the few countries with long records. Forager numbers come from anthropologists' demographic censuses of living groups, pooled by Gurven and Kaplan; Roman ones from tombstones, the census returns of Roman Egypt and skeletal series — each biased in its own direction, which is why they are given as ranges.

Carbon & the Anthropocene

Through the ice ages, atmospheric CO₂ oscillated between about 175 and 300 parts per million. It held steady near 280 ppm for the last two thousand years of a Holocene that began nearer 265 — until, around 1850, the line departs its flatline and climbs to 427 ppm in 2025, roughly half as much again as the pre-industrial level. Some time around 1913 it passed ~300 ppm, the highest concentration in the 800,000 years that Antarctic ice records. That hockey-stick departure is the single clearest signature of the Anthropocene.

The curve also carries a quieter, earlier human mark. Between the late 1500s and the 1610s the ice records a fall of about 7–10 ppm. Koch, Brierley, Maslin and Lewis (2019) estimate that the death of some 90% of the Indigenous peoples of the Americas in the century after 1492 let ~56 million hectares of farmland revert to forest; with the carbon-cycle feedbacks that followed, they put the human share at roughly half to two-thirds of the dip, leaving the rest to natural causes. The size of that share is still debated — but if it holds, one of the clearest early human fingerprints on the global air is a demographic catastrophe, not a factory.

"Great Acceleration", not "Anthropocene epoch"

The page's title comes from Will Steffen and colleagues, whose graphs of socio-economic and Earth-system trends — first drawn in 2004, updated in 2015 — show nearly every curve bending upward after 1950: population, GDP, energy, fertiliser, dams and water use on one side; CO₂, methane, ocean acidification and forest loss on the other. The Anthropocene Working Group proposed making that mid-century shift a formal geological epoch, with its reference section in the sediments of Crawford Lake, Ontario. In March 2024 the International Commission on Stratigraphy's Quaternary subcommission voted the proposal down, so "Anthropocene" is used here informally — a description, not a unit of the geological time scale. The warming that follows the carbon is measured, not proposed: 2024 was the first calendar year more than 1.5 °C above the 1850–1900 average (~1.55 °C), 2025 came in at ~1.44 °C, and 2023–2025 together averaged ~1.48 °C (World Meteorological Organization, consolidated from eight datasets).

That 427 ppm has an address: who emitted it historically, and who is emitting it now, are two different lists — see the same curves, region by region.

Cities & urbanization

Ten thousand years ago essentially no one lived in a city; there were none. On the definitions countries themselves use, humanity crossed the urban-majority line in 2007 and is about 58% urban today. The UN's World Urbanization Prospects 2025 adds a second, harmonised yardstick, the Degree of Urbanisation, which classifies every square kilometre of the planet the same way: a city is a contiguous cluster of at least 50,000 people at 1,500 or more per km², a town at least 5,000 people at 300 or more per km², and everything else is rural. On that measure 45% of humanity lives in cities (20% in 1950), another 36% in towns, and only 19% in rural areas. The two numbers differ because many countries — especially in Central and South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa — count as "rural" dense settlements the harmonised method calls towns.

The title of "largest city on Earth" is a relay that traces the moving centre of gravity of human density — Uruk, then Rome, then Chang'an and Baghdad, then Beijing, then from around 1825 London, then New York and Tokyo — and, on the UN's 2025 harmonised definition, now Jakarta, with Dhaka second and projected to take the title by mid-century. Of the world's ten largest cities on that measure, Cairo is the only one outside Asia.

Who owns the biomass

Ten thousand years ago, wild mammals made up over 99% of mammal biomass and humans were a rounding error by weight — though not by effect. That 99% baseline is already a post-extinction figure: the mammoths, mastodons, ground sloths, Diprotodon and giant kangaroos were gone before it was measured, and the wild-mammal biomass of 50,000 years ago was plausibly two to three times larger than the 10,000 BCE stock this comparison starts from. Today the ratio has inverted almost completely: livestock is about 60% of mammal biomass, humans 36%, and wild mammals — land and sea together — just 4%. Among birds, domestic poultry is roughly 70% of all bird biomass. In one Holocene we became the dominant large animal on Earth — and remade the rest into our herds.
