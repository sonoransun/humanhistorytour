Why this is hard to picture

All of history is the last minute of the day

If the seven-million-year lineage of our ancestors were compressed into a single 24-hour day, anatomically modern humans appear only at 11:00 pm. Farming begins at 11:57. Everything we call recorded history — every pharaoh, empire, war, and invention — is the final 62 seconds before midnight.

This tour solves that vertigo with a persistent timeline rail that shows two scales at once: where you are on the page, and where you are in actual time. Nine chapters carry you from bipedal apes to the Anthropocene — each roughly equal in reading length, even though the first spans millions of years and the last barely a single human lifetime.

No centre of the world

History did not happen in one place

The familiar story is a single torch passed from Sumer to Egypt to Greece to Rome to Europe. Run the clock globally and it dissolves: at almost any date in the last ten thousand years, people on several continents were farming, building, trading and inventing at once, and several of the biggest ideas were invented more than once, by people who never met.

01

Invented more than once

Farming arose independently roughly a dozen times — in Asia, Africa, New Guinea and both Americas. Writing was invented from scratch at least three times. Zero appeared at least twice, in worlds that never met: Mesoamerica and India.

Parallel civilizations

02

Older than the pyramids

At Budj Bim in Victoria, Gunditjmara engineers had begun cutting eel channels and traps into volcanic rock by about 4600 BCE — at least 6,600 years ago — one of the oldest aquaculture systems on Earth, and still there.

Australia & the Arctic

03

We were not alone

For more than three million years — until only about 40,000 years ago, and perhaps 30,000 for the last Denisovans — the Earth carried more than one kind of human at once: Neanderthals, Denisovans, naledi, floresiensis. We have been the only kind for about a hundredth as long; being the only one is the anomaly.

The human family

04

How anyone knows

Every date here rests on a method — a calibration curve, a tree ring, an ice core, a protein, a genome. The error bars are real, and the oldest "firsts" keep getting older.
